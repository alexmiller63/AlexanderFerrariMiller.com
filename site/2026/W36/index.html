The fast star at the foot of the Eagle

Altair is one of the nearest bright stars and one of the three unmistakable corners of the Summer Triangle.

Its apparent simplicity hides a remarkable physical fact: Altair rotates very rapidly. The spin is fast enough to distort the star from a sphere, making it wider around its equator than from pole to pole. Modern interferometry can actually resolve that flattened shape and the resulting differences in surface temperature.

The name belongs to a much older observing tradition. Altair marks Aquila, the Eagle, and for a naked-eye observer it is the southern vertex of the enormous Summer Triangle with Vega and Deneb. That geometric role makes Altair useful before any telescope is involved: find the triangle and a large section of the northern summer sky suddenly has a framework.

Altair is therefore a fine example of the two scales on which a bright star can matter. To the eye it is a point used to navigate the heavens. To modern instruments it is a nearby rotating stellar surface whose shape can be measured.

Sources: NASA/JPL and modern optical-interferometry studies of Altair; IAU Working Group on Star Names.

A dying star made the Dumbbell

M27 was the first planetary nebula ever discovered, although the name “planetary nebula” has nothing to do with planets.

Charles Messier recorded this object in 1764. Through a small telescope its two brighter lobes suggested the shape that gave it the name Dumbbell Nebula. What looks like a quiet gray patch is the expelled outer atmosphere of an aging star, illuminated by energetic radiation from the hot remnant at its center.

That makes M27 more than a pretty deep-sky target. It is a visible stage in the evolution of a star broadly similar in mass to the Sun. The star has cast much of itself into space; the glowing nebula is temporary, expanding and thinning while the exposed stellar core continues toward its future as a white dwarf.

The historical accident in the terminology is useful. Early observers sometimes saw these small round nebulae as resembling planetary disks, so the label survived even after their true nature became known. M27 therefore carries two histories at once: the physical history of a dying star and the observational history of astronomers learning what fuzzy things in the telescope actually were.

For the visual observer, photographs set the wrong expectation. The reward is not saturated color but structure: a small luminous form that becomes increasingly unmistakable as the eye settles into the field.

Sources: NASA Hubble Messier Catalog, “Messier 27 (The Dumbbell Nebula)”; NASA Astronomy Picture of the Day, “M27: Not A Comet.”
