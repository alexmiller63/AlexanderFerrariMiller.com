A star swarm with something strange at its heart

M15 is one of the densest globular clusters known, an ancient swarm whose brilliant center is crowded on a scale the quiet binocular glow does not suggest.

Jean-Dominique Maraldi discovered M15 in 1746 while searching for comets. Today it is known as an old globular cluster roughly 34,000 light-years away in Pegasus. Its stars become dramatically more concentrated toward the center, making the cluster a classic example of the dense stellar environments that globular clusters can create.

M15 also contains Pease 1, a planetary nebula discovered in 1928. Planetary nebulae are rare inside globular clusters, so finding the remains of a dying star embedded in such an ancient stellar population gives M15 an unusual second identity.

Studies of the cluster’s crowded core have also been used to investigate whether an intermediate-mass black hole may reside there. The central dynamics are difficult to untangle, which makes M15 not merely a beautiful object but an active astrophysical laboratory.

At the eyepiece all of that begins as a small round glow. Knowing what is packed into that glow changes the view.

Sources: NASA Hubble Messier Catalog, “Messier 15”; NASA Hubble studies of the M15 core.

A comet hunter found a city of 150,000 stars

M2 can look like a small misty ball, but the haze is the unresolved light of more than 150,000 stars bound together by gravity.

Jean-Dominique Maraldi discovered the cluster in 1746 while observing a comet. Messier later added it to his catalog of objects that could be confused with comets—the practical observing list that accidentally became one of astronomy’s most famous tours of the deep sky.

M2 lies roughly 37,000 light-years away in Aquarius and spans more than 150 light-years. Like other globular clusters, its stars crowd much more densely toward the center, producing the bright core and fading halo that observers can compare directly with other globulars.

The contrast between discovery and reality is part of the pleasure. To an 18th-century comet hunter it was a persistent fuzzy nuisance. To us it is an ancient gravitational system containing a stellar population on a scale no single glance through the telescope can resolve.

Sources: NASA Hubble Messier Catalog, “Messier 2.”

A cluster that wants a wide field

M39 is an open cluster whose loose bright stars make more sense together than they do one at a time.

Unlike a globular cluster, M39 is not a compact ball of ancient stars. It is a comparatively sparse stellar family in Cygnus, spread broadly enough across the sky that binoculars or a low-power telescope often show its character better than high magnification does.

That makes M39 a useful reminder that more magnification is not automatically more astronomy. An object has a natural scale. Push too close and a broad cluster can dissolve into unrelated-looking stars; step back and the pattern reappears.

Open clusters are temporary by astronomical standards. Their stars formed together, but interactions among the stars and the gravitational influence of the Milky Way gradually disperse them. The recognizable cluster is therefore a phase in the life of a stellar family, not a permanent arrangement.

M39 rewards the observer who gives it room.

Sources: standard Messier catalog and open-cluster references.

The cluster that sorted its stars by weight

M30 is an ancient globular cluster whose crowded history has literally rearranged its stellar population.

The cluster is about 28,000 light-years away in Capricornus. Like other globulars, it contains an old population held in a dense spherical system, but M30 is especially interesting because its core has undergone gravitational collapse, driving stars into an extremely concentrated center.

Hubble observations found two distinct populations of blue stragglers—stars that appear younger and bluer than the cluster’s ordinary old population. Their distribution preserves evidence of different formation routes and of the cluster’s internal dynamical evolution.

So the small glow in the telescope is not static. Over immense spans of time, encounters between stars and binary systems redistribute energy, move stars inward or outward, and alter what kinds of stars appear in different parts of the cluster.

M30 looks quiet because stellar dynamics move slowly on a human clock. On the cluster’s clock, it has been sorting itself for billions of years.

Sources: NASA Hubble Messier Catalog, “Messier 30,” and Hubble studies of blue stragglers in M30.
