Eclipses

The Sun, Earth, and Moon write their alignments in shadow. An eclipse lets us watch that geometry unfold.

Why eclipses happen · Solar eclipses · Lunar eclipses · Watching safely · Eclipse catalog

Why eclipses happen

A solar eclipse happens at new Moon, when the Moon passes between Earth and the Sun. A lunar eclipse happens at full Moon, when the Moon passes through Earth’s shadow.

Why not every month? The Moon’s orbit is tilted by about five degrees to Earth’s orbital plane. Most new and full Moons pass above or below the alignment needed for an eclipse. Eclipses become possible near the two places where those planes cross, called the orbital nodes.

A shadow has two main regions. In the umbra, the light source is completely hidden; in the penumbra, only part of it is hidden. The Moon’s central shadow also has an extension called the antumbra, where the Moon appears smaller than the Sun and a bright ring remains.

Solar eclipses: the Moon’s shadow on Earth

A solar eclipse is a local experience. Two people watching the same event from different places can see very different amounts of the Sun covered. A narrow central track may receive totality or annularity while a much larger surrounding region sees a partial eclipse.

Total
The Moon completely covers the Sun’s bright disk along the path of totality. The corona becomes visible, daylight fades, and the surrounding horizon may glow.
Annular
The Moon appears too small to cover the entire Sun. Along the central path, sunlight forms a ring around the Moon. The ring remains dangerously bright.
Partial
The Moon covers only part of the Sun. An event classified as partial has no total or annular central path on Earth.
Hybrid
The central eclipse changes between annular and total along its track. Earth’s curved surface changes the observer’s distance from the Moon enough to make the difference. The classification describes different places along one event’s path.

The apparent sizes of the Sun and Moon are close, but neither stays exactly constant. Their changing distances help determine whether the Moon can hide the Sun completely.

Lunar eclipses: Earth’s shadow on the Moon

During a lunar eclipse, everyone who can see the Moon sees the same phase at the same instant. Whether you can watch the beginning, middle, or end depends on moonrise, moonset, and local conditions.

Penumbral
The Moon crosses only the penumbra. Look for subtle shading rather than a sharply defined dark bite; shallow events can be hard to notice.
Partial
Part of the Moon enters the umbra. Earth’s curved shadow advances across the familiar lunar surface.
Total
The whole Moon enters the umbra. Sunlight passing through Earth’s atmosphere can still reach it, giving the Moon copper, orange, or red tones. Its appearance varies with conditions in Earth’s atmosphere.

Watching safely

Lunar eclipses are safe to watch directly. Binoculars reveal changes across the lunar surface.

For direct solar viewing, use undamaged eclipse glasses or a handheld solar viewer meeting ISO 12312-2. Ordinary sunglasses are unsuitable. Cameras, binoculars, and telescopes need purpose-made solar filters secured over the front of the optics; eclipse glasses alone do not make those instruments safe.

Only during complete totality, at a location inside the totality path, may you look without a solar viewer. Replace it before the Sun’s bright surface reappears. Keep protection in place throughout partial and annular phases. Indirect pinhole projection offers another way to follow the changing solar crescent without looking at the Sun.

Reading an eclipse card

Each catalog link opens an individual event card with explanations, UTC times, calculated geometry, and available path information. UTC is a shared time standard: convert both the date and clock time to your location.

Greatest eclipse is one instant for the event as a whole. Global beginning and ending times describe the eclipse somewhere on Earth; they are not your local contact times or your duration of totality.

Our calculated central-path plots show latitude and longitude along the shadow axis, with coordinate downloads. The center line does not show the track’s width or the wider partial-eclipse visibility region. The current spherical-Earth model has limitations described on each card. Calculation records are available through download links.
