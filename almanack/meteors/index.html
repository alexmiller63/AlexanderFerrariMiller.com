Meteor showers

A meteor is a brief streak of light produced as a small particle enters Earth’s atmosphere. A shower occurs when Earth crosses a stream of debris. Perspective makes the meteors appear to spread from one part of the sky: the radiant.

How to read these cards

Each card gives a calculated nominal peak, radiant constellation, reference rate, Moon illumination, and observing guidance. The catalog covers ten selected annual showers for each available year; it is not a complete list of meteor showers.

ZHR means zenithal hourly rate: a standardized rate for a radiant overhead in a very dark, clear sky. Your count is usually lower. The radiant’s altitude, sky brightness, clouds, and Moon position all affect what you see.

Sources and calculation

Original explanations and publication calculations: Star Almanack. Nominal peak solar longitudes, radiant constellations, and reference rates: International Meteor Organization shower calendar and working list. Peak longitude crossings and approximate Moon illumination come from our stored calculations using JPL/NAIF ephemeris kernels.

A calculated longitude crossing is a nominal seasonal marker, not a forecast of the exact observed maximum. Shower strength and timing vary between years. Moon illumination alone is not a local visibility calculation.
