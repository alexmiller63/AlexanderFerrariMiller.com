# NGC 1535 — Cleopatra's Eye has a wind behind its gaze

A bright inner nebula and a fainter outer region frame a hot stellar core whose ultraviolet spectrum reveals material moving away from it.

Cleopatra's Eye is the popular name of NGC 1535, a planetary nebula in Eridanus. NASA describes a brighter inner region within an outer envelope. A planetary nebula forms as an evolved star loses outer material and leaves a compact hot core on the path toward a white dwarf. The old class name reflects the planet-like appearance of some examples through early telescopes; it does not connect this object with an actual planet. Source: NASA, https://science.nasa.gov/missions/hubble/hubble-spies-eye-in-the-sky/.

Hubble observed NGC 1535 in a survey of more than a hundred planetary nebulae with nearby stars. The purpose was to investigate whether proximity on the sky could represent a gravitational connection. NASA reports that the observed separation of this nebula's central star and a possible companion suggests a bound binary. The word suggests matters: a nearby point on an image is not, by itself, proof of companionship. Source: NASA, above.

An ultraviolet spectrum supplies evidence that the visual picture cannot. The Space Telescope Science Institute's archive presents an Astro-1 Hopkins Ultraviolet Telescope spectrum of the central star. Absorption features are attributed to atomic and molecular hydrogen in surrounding material. A strong dip with an adjacent emission peak is attributed to a fast stellar wind. This is evidence based on the distribution of light by wavelength, not motion that could be watched directly through an eyepiece. Source: STScI/MAST, https://archive.stsci.edu/hut/science/pneb.html, identifying Bowers et al. (1995), Astrophysical Journal 444, 748.

A spectrum and a photograph preserve different information. The photograph locates features in an apparent shape; the spectrum tests how light varies with wavelength. For the archived wind feature, the absorption dip and neighbouring emission peak must be interpreted together. An isolated dark notch would be a much less informative description of the observation. Source for this particular feature: STScI/MAST, above. Reading the pair as evidence for an outflow gives the quiet-looking central point a physical story without requiring visible motion across the sky.

The Eye nickname can suggest a helpful visual comparison, but it is an unreliable finding method. An observer should first establish the field, then ask whether an inner concentration and a more diffuse surround are distinguishable. Selecting a convenient circular-looking patch without checking the surrounding stars reverses that process. The resemblance belongs to interpretation after identification.
