# Beta Reticuli — an orange giant with a hidden companion

A naked-eye point in the southern Reticle conceals a stellar pair, while its far-southern position places it below the horizon for many northern observers.

Beta Reticuli (β Ret) has visual magnitude about 3.84 and lies roughly 100 light-years away. Its visible component is an evolved orange K-type giant. A 2009 study adopted classification K2 IIIb and estimated a surface temperature of 4,580 kelvin and an age of 5–6 billion years. Older classifications called it a subgiant; its exact evolutionary status and age depend on stellar models.

The hidden companion reveals itself through periodic Doppler shifts in the giant’s spectral lines. The measured orbital period is about 1,918 days, or 5.25 years, with eccentricity 0.335. The study estimated a relative orbital semimajor axis of 3.53 astronomical units. One astronomical unit is the Earth–Sun distance; the semimajor axis describes the size of the elliptical orbit, not a constant spacing.

The companion is consistent with an M-type red dwarf. The estimated maximum apparent separation is only about 0.16 arcsecond, and the companion is much fainter: this is not an ordinary small-telescope double. The first orbital solution appeared in 1928, based on spectra collected during 1903–1925.

The orbit’s numbers make the hidden system easier to picture. Using the reported semimajor axis and eccentricity, the calculated separation ranges from about 2.35 astronomical units at closest approach to 4.71 at greatest separation. That is roughly the scale of the outer asteroid belt through the region approaching Jupiter’s orbit in our Solar System. This comparison describes distance, not planets: the orbit belongs to two stars. The separation we see projected on the sky also depends on the orbit’s orientation and the observing date.

The constellation is Reticulum, the Reticle. A reticle is a pattern of fine lines, such as crosshairs or a grid, placed in an instrument’s eyepiece to help aim it or measure positions. In an astronomical telescope, those lines provide a reference for measuring where stars appear; the constellation commemorates this measuring tool. Reticuli is the Latin genitive, meaning “of Reticulum.” Beta is its Greek-letter designation. Its southern position kept it outside the skies of many northern naming traditions, but that does not establish that southern cultures had no names for it.

Its reference position is approximately right ascension 3h 44m and declination −64° 48′. Geometrically, it never rises north of about 25.2° N, including the almanac’s default latitude of 45° N. South of about 25.2° S it is circumpolar. At 35° S it reaches about 60° altitude at upper culmination. These limits are calculated from declination, ignoring refraction and local obstructions; being above the horizon does not guarantee darkness.

For finding it, match Reticulum’s four-star figure to the chart. Beta is the pointed vertex where the sides from Alpha and Delta meet. Follow both sides to confirm the identification rather than selecting a star by brightness alone. Binoculars can help show the pattern and the giant’s warm colour; the close companion remains unresolved in an ordinary view.

The most rewarding ordinary observation is therefore the visible giant in its surrounding pattern. First use a wide field to establish the figure, then compare Beta’s colour with nearby stars. A telescope can make the star brighter to the eye, but magnification alone cannot overcome the close companion’s tiny spacing and strong contrast. Seeing a single point is entirely consistent with the scientific evidence for two stars. The star’s spectrum and changing motion reveal information that its visual appearance does not.

This account describes the system and its enduring observing geometry. Check the current week and your location for darkness, culmination time, Moon interference and any nearby planet. A planet beside Beta is a temporary visitor, not a permanent member of the pattern.
