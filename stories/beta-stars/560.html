Beta Mensae — a distant giant over Table Mountain

The Greek letter suggests a prominent marker, but Beta Mensae is a fifth-magnitude giant in one of the faintest southern constellations—a useful reminder that a Bayer letter is a label, not a ranking guaranteed by the modern sky.

Beta Mensae (β Men), permanent object 560, is also HD 33285, HIP 23467 and HR 1677. The stored visual magnitude is about 5.3. SIMBAD identifies the same star and classifies its spectrum near G8 III, the notation for a yellow giant rather than a Sun-like dwarf: https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=bet+men. A modern abundance survey likewise lists HD 33285 as G8 III and reports a parallax of about 4.1 milliarcseconds. Inverting that small angle gives a rough distance near 240 parsecs, or about 790 light-years, but the result should be read as an estimate because parallax uncertainty becomes important at this scale. Source: Luck and Heiter, *Abundances in the Local Region I*, https://doi.org/10.1088/0004-637X/737/2/84.

The luminosity class carries the physical story. A class-III star has left the compact main-sequence state in which hydrogen fusion in the core supported it for most of its life. Its outer layers have expanded and cooled enough to give the G-type spectrum, while the large radiating surface makes it visible across hundreds of light-years. “Yellow giant” describes that evolutionary state and spectral appearance; it does not mean an eyepiece will show a saturated yellow disk. Like every ordinary star at this distance, Beta Mensae remains an unresolved point for a visual observer.

Mensa is the Latin name for Table Mountain, the landmark above Cape Town. Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille introduced the constellation after surveying the southern sky from the Cape in the eighteenth century. The IAU now preserves Mensa as an official constellation name with the abbreviation Men and genitive Mensae: https://www.iau.org/IAU/IAU/Astronomy-FAQs/Constellations.aspx. That history explains the unusually modern figure but not the star's physics. Beta and the other Greek letters simply organize stars within the figure; they do not make the constellation a physical cluster.

The stored position is approximately right ascension 5h 03m and declination −71° 19′. That far-southern declination controls the practical view. The star never rises north of about latitude 18° 41′ N. At 45° N its geometric upper culmination remains roughly 26° below the horizon; at 35° S it climbs to about 54° altitude. South of about 18° 41′ S it is circumpolar. These are original spherical-geometry calculations from the preserved coordinates, ignoring refraction, topography and daylight.

For a search, first decide whether the latitude makes the target possible. From southern mid-latitudes, use a naked-eye chart to establish the dim Mensa figure between the brighter landmarks of Dorado, Hydrus and Volans, then match Beta's recorded coordinates rather than assuming the second-brightest point must carry the letter. Binoculars help under suburban skyglow, but magnification adds no visible surface detail. The observing limit is part of the object’s character. At magnitude 5.3, Beta Mensae can be naked-eye under a dark, transparent sky, yet haze and light pollution may erase it. A successful record can note the field identification, estimated colour and limiting magnitude without claiming to see the star's giant dimensions. Keep Moon phase, twilight and the week's culmination time in the almanac entry; the enduring story is the far-southern giant behind the modest point.
