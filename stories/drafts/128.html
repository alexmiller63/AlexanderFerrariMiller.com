NGC 40: Reading a Planetary Nebula in the Catalog

NGC 40 is identified by SIMBAD as a planetary nebula. The database places it at right ascension 00h 13m 01.017s and declination +72° 31′ 19.03″ for the J2000 coordinate system—a precise address that can be entered into a sky chart or telescope computer. Those coordinates put the object far north of the celestial equator, but a coordinate alone does not describe what an observer will see. [SIMBAD: NGC 40](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%2040)

The database offers a compact portrait in numbers. It lists NGC 40’s V-band magnitude as 11.46, alongside measurements in other bands, including B, G, J, H and K. A magnitude is a brightness measure, not a description of the object’s appearance. The V value is useful for comparing catalog entries, but it cannot, by itself, promise how conspicuous NGC 40 will look through a particular telescope or under a particular sky.

The catalogue also records a parallax of 0.5612 milliarcseconds, with a listed uncertainty of 0.0172 milliarcseconds. It gives the object’s proper motion as −7.260 milliarcseconds per year in right ascension and −1.848 in declination. These measurements describe apparent changes in position as recorded in the database; they are not a drawing of the nebula or a direct account of its visible details. The figures let the catalog describe NGC 40 in ways that a name and a sky position cannot.

There is another notable entry: the catalogue lists the spectral type as [WC8]. The bracketed label is part of the database’s spectral classification for NGC 40. It sits alongside the object type, position and motion as a separate kind of information. Taken together, these entries show how different observations can contribute different pieces to an astronomical record. The classification says how the source is categorized; the coordinates tell where it is; the magnitude and motion values add measured quantities.

A catalog is not an observing report. The catalogue's data do not, in the listed entries, supply a verbal description of NGC 40’s appearance at the eyepiece, nor do they establish what equipment an observer needs. For someone planning to look, the coordinates provide a starting point: set a chart to J2000 and search for NGC 40.
