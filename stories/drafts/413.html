PN G138.8+02.8: A Small Nebula in the Northern Sky

*This planetary nebula is easier to pin down on a chart than to judge by its catalogue name. Use precise coordinates, a dark sky and modest expectations at the eyepiece.*

Some celestial objects arrive with a name that sounds like a location. PN G138.8+02.8 is one: its designation gives its position in the Milky Way’s coordinate system, at galactic longitude 138.8° and latitude +2.8°. It is also catalogued as IC 289, and SIMBAD identifies it as a planetary nebula. Its J2000 position is right ascension 03h 10m 19.3s, declination +61° 19′ 01″—a useful address for a star chart or telescope controller. ([SIMBAD: IC 289](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=IC%20289))

“Planetary nebula” is a historical label, not a description of a planet. Such nebulae form late in the lives of some stars, when a star sheds surrounding gas that is then energized by the hot stellar remnant. The glowing gas can make a compact, detailed target, though the label alone cannot tell an observer how bright or easy a particular nebula will look. For IC 289, SIMBAD lists a radio angular size of about 0.765 arcminutes—roughly 46 arcseconds across. That is a catalogue measurement in radio data, not a promise that the nebula’s visible outline will match it. ([SIMBAD: IC 289](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=IC%20289))

The position places IC 289 in the northern sky, among the rich star fields of Cassiopeia. A go-to mount can take you straight to the coordinates; otherwise, begin with a detailed chart and work from nearby stars, checking the field carefully. At this small apparent size, the nebula may look like a faint, slightly fuzzy point rather than an obvious disk. Don’t mistake every soft-looking star for the target: compare the field at different magnifications and, if possible, confirm the position against a chart or image.

Choose a clear, moonless night and give your eyes time to adapt. Start at low power to identify the field, then increase magnification gradually. A narrowband nebula filter may improve contrast, but results vary; compare the filtered view with the unfiltered one rather than assuming the filter will help. If the nebula remains elusive, that is useful information, not a failure. The catalogue gives a precise position and a compact radio size, but it does not guarantee a visual show in any particular telescope.

For imaging, the same coordinates provide a reliable starting point for a careful field check. Take a short test exposure, inspect the stars and framing, and then build up the integration time if the target is where expected. A recorded observation can be satisfying even when the eyepiece view is subtle: you have found a stellar remnant whose name encodes its place in the Galaxy, and whose faint gas carries the story of a star’s final act.
