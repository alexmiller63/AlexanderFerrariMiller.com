Beta Oct: A Southern Beacon with a Measurable Drift

*In Octans, a modest fourth-magnitude star offers a useful lesson in distance, motion and observing the far-southern sky.*

The southern sky has its own quiet landmarks. Beta Oct, in the constellation Octans, is not dazzling, but it is bright enough to pick out under a dark sky—and close enough to the south celestial pole to stay in view for many observers in the Southern Hemisphere. Its catalogue entry gives it a position of right ascension 22h 46m and declination 81°23′ south, placing it deep in the circumpolar sky for most southern locations. [SIMBAD identifies the star as HIP 112405](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=HIP%20112405).

At visual magnitude 4.13, Beta Oct is a naked-eye star under reasonably dark conditions, though it may be hard to see through haze, moonlight or city glow. It is a useful target for learning the southern sky, but Octans does not offer the bright, unmistakable patterns found in some better-known constellations. A printed chart or sky app can help distinguish its stars; binoculars can make the search easier.

The star’s distance gives it a second kind of appeal. SIMBAD lists a parallax of 21.85 milliarcseconds. Parallax is the tiny apparent shift in a nearby star’s position as Earth travels around the Sun. Converting that measurement gives a distance of roughly 46 parsecs, or about 150 light-years. The number is not something the eye can perceive directly. Instead, it is inferred from careful measurements of that annual shift.

Beta Oct’s classification is A9 IV/V. The “A9” describes its spectral type, while “IV/V” places its luminosity class between subgiant and main-sequence categories. In other words, it is not simply a point of light: its spectrum and brightness provide clues to the star’s physical character. Its measured visual magnitude, also listed by SIMBAD, is about 4.13.

The catalogue also records significant proper motion: about 54.5 milliarcseconds per year in right ascension, with a much smaller north-south component of about 1.2 milliarcseconds per year. Proper motion is a star’s apparent motion across the sky, caused by its movement through space relative to the Sun. That drift is far too small to notice during an evening’s observing, but over many generations it changes a star’s position against the more distant background.

**To find it:** Choose a clear night and look toward the southern horizon. Beta Oct is circumpolar—never setting—at latitudes south of about 8.6° north, though from near the equator it skims low above the horizon. The farther south you are, the higher it culminates. Use a chart or app to locate Octans, then look for the star marked Beta Oct. Under dark skies, give your eyes time to adapt; if the constellation is difficult to trace, sweep the area slowly with binoculars.

Once you have found it, pause on the contrast: the star looks fixed, yet precise measurements reveal both its distance and its slow motion. Beta Oct is a small but rewarding reminder that even a quiet southern landmark carries a story written in light and geometry.
