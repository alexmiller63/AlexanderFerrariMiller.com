Kraz, the Crow’s Quiet Beacon

An unhurried yellow giant marks one corner of Corvus—and rewards a closer look at what a star’s light can tell us.

The crow in the spring sky is small, but its outline is easy to remember: four bright points make a crooked quadrilateral below and west of Spica. At one corner shines Kraz, also catalogued as Beta Corvi. It is not the sky’s brightest star, nor does it need to be. On a clear evening, its steady light is enough to anchor the pattern.

Kraz has the useful distinction of being both familiar and physically unusual. The catalogue lists its visual magnitude as 2.64, bright enough to see without optical aid from a reasonably dark site. Its spectral classification is G5 II Ba0.3: “G5” places it among G-type stars, “II” identifies it as a bright giant, and the Ba notation records a mild barium spectral peculiarity. That chemistry is not apparent to the unaided eye; it is read from the star’s spectrum, where light is separated into its component wavelengths. The same database gives a parallax of 22.0049 milliarcseconds. Turning that angle into distance puts Kraz roughly 148 light-years away.

The numbers offer one kind of intimacy. Kraz’s measured proper motion—its slow drift against more distant stars—is about 56.7 milliarcseconds per year, mostly southward. That works out to less than three arcseconds over fifty years: far too little for a casual observer to notice, but enough for careful astrometry to measure. The star seems fixed in the crow’s corner because the sky’s great motions unfold on timescales longer than a human glance.

For an observing night, begin with Spica, Virgo’s brightest star. In Northern Hemisphere spring, look below and a little west of it for Corvus’s compact four-star shape. Kraz is one of the pattern’s corner stars; give your eyes a moment to adapt, and the outline should emerge even when the surrounding sky is not perfectly dark. Binoculars can help you pick out the field, but Kraz itself is bright enough to find unaided. From far northern locations, it sits low in the southern sky; from farther south, it climbs higher. A clear southern horizon helps.

Try a simple comparison: look at Kraz, then sweep your gaze across the other three main stars of the crow. The figure is not a literal bird, of course, but the old constellation gives the points a shared story. Kraz’s catalogue name and classification give it another—one written in coordinates, parallax, and spectral lines. Both ways of looking belong beneath the same night sky.

On your next spring evening, find Spica, trace the little quadrilateral, and pause at Kraz. Its light began its journey around 148 years ago. The point you see is quiet, but its spectrum and measured motion tell a history that the eye alone cannot read.
