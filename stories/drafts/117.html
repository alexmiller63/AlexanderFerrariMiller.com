Beta Pegasi: A Long-Period Variable

the catalogue’s catalog entries give a starting point for finding and following this star—but not a schedule for its changing light.

Beta Pegasi is identified in the catalogue as a long-period variable. That label makes it more than a point to locate: it is a star to revisit. The catalog supplies a position, a parallax, a spectral type and a visual magnitude, but it does not give a period or a brightness range in the record cited here. So the best observing plan is a patient one: look, make a note, and return.

The catalogue lists the star’s J2000 coordinates as right ascension 23 hours 03 minutes 46.46 seconds and declination +28 degrees 04 minutes 58.03 seconds. Its identifier is HIP 113881, and the database associates it with “bet Peg.” The coordinates are a precise way to identify the target on a star chart or in an observing app. They also place it north of the celestial equator, at a declination of about 28 degrees.

The same record gives a parallax of 16.64 milliarcseconds. Converting that value gives a distance of about 60 parsecs, or roughly 196 light-years. These are calculated approximations from the cataloged parallax, not separate distance measurements quoted in the record. The catalogue also lists a spectral type of M2.5 II–III. That designation is a compact classification attached to the star; it is another useful point of comparison when looking up Beta Pegasi in astronomical references.

For brightness, the record lists a visual magnitude of 2.42. Because the catalogue classifies the star as variable, that catalog entry should be treated as a reference value—not as a promise that the star will appear exactly that bright on a particular observing night. The supplied record does not state how far its brightness varies or how long a cycle takes. It therefore cannot tell an observer when to expect a noticeable change.

A modest observing project can fill in a different kind of record: your own. Find Beta Pegasi using its catalog designation or coordinates, then write down the date, time and observing conditions. Add a careful estimate of its brightness. If you repeat the observation, use the same approach each time and note any differences you perceive.
