M70: A Quiet Knot in Sagittarius

The globular cluster’s coordinates point toward a crowded part of the Milky Way—but finding it depends as much on your horizon as your telescope.

Somewhere in Sagittarius, amid the bright sweep of the Milky Way, M70 gathers its stars into a compact knot. It does not announce itself with a brilliant flash. For an observer looking from far north, it may barely clear the rooftops; farther south, it climbs high enough to reward a patient search. M70 is a globular cluster: a dense, roughly spherical association of stars bound together by gravity.

Its catalogued position is right ascension 18h 43m 12.76s, declination −32° 17′ 31.6″ (J2000). That southerly declination is the first practical fact to keep in mind. From latitude 40° north, M70 reaches a maximum altitude of only about 18° above the southern horizon. At that height, haze and turbulence can blur the view. From farther south, it climbs higher. Choose a site with an open southern horizon, and plan to observe when the cluster is highest—not simply when the sky first gets dark.

The name M70 comes from the Messier catalogue; NGC 6681 is its other common designation. The catalogue classifies it as a globular cluster and lists an optical angular size of 7.8 arcminutes. That figure describes a measured extent, not a sharp edge: a cluster gradually fades into the surrounding star field. In the eyepiece, expect a compact, softly concentrated glow rather than a crisp disk. Under a dark sky, a telescope may begin to pick out stars around the outskirts, while the crowded center can remain unresolved.

There is more to the cluster than its appearance. The catalogue lists a heliocentric radial velocity of about 217 kilometres per second. In other words, measurements of its spectral lines indicate that M70 is moving away from the Sun along our line of sight. That number is one component of its motion, not a complete account of its journey through the Galaxy. The database also gives proper-motion measurements, which describe its apparent drift across the sky.

For a first attempt, use the coordinates in a planetarium app, a setting circle or a telescope’s GoTo system. If you prefer a star chart, look in Sagittarius and use a detailed chart to guide a short hop from familiar nearby stars. Start with a low-power eyepiece, which gives a wider field to help identify the faint, rounded patch. Once centered, increase the magnification gradually. Averted vision—looking slightly to one side of the cluster—can make dim detail easier to notice. Give your eyes time to adapt, and avoid observing when the Moon is bright or M70 is low in murky air.

The cluster’s low altitude for many northern observers can make it a challenge, but also a reminder that the sky is local: the same object occupies a different place above every horizon. Find M70 on a clear night, and that small glow becomes a meeting point between careful observing and the measured motions of a vast stellar system.
