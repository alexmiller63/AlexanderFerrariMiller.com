Rasalhague: A bright marker in the Serpent Bearer

*An easy summer target, Alpha Ophiuchi offers a naked-eye welcome—and a reminder that even a familiar point of light has a measurable place and character.*

On a clear summer evening, look toward Ophiuchus, the Serpent Bearer, and find its brightest star: Rasalhague. It stands out without optical aid, a steady-looking point that has helped give this broad constellation a recognizable anchor. But the star is more than a handy signpost. Its catalogued position, distance and spectral classification turn a quick glance into a small exercise in stellar astronomy.

The catalogue identifies Rasalhague as Alpha Ophiuchi, or alf Oph, and classifies it as a double or multiple star. The database lists a visual magnitude of 2.07—bright enough to be readily seen from a reasonably dark site, though haze, city lighting and the Moon can all make fainter stars harder to pick out. Its listed spectral type is A5IVnn: the “A” indicates a hot, blue-white class of star, while “IV” places it in the subgiant category. The classification’s “nn” is part of the catalogued spectral type; it is a reminder that stellar labels can carry detail beyond the broad color-and-temperature shorthand familiar from star charts.

Distance is another way to give this point of light depth. The catalogue reports a parallax of 67.13 milliarcseconds. Parallax is the apparent shift of a nearby star against more distant background stars as Earth moves around the Sun. Converting that value gives a distance of about 15 parsecs, or roughly 49 light-years. That is close by the scale of the Milky Way, though still far beyond any journey a person could make. The star’s measured proper motion—its slow change of position across the sky—also shows that the apparently fixed pattern of the constellations is not truly fixed.

For a first look, go out on a summer evening in the Northern Hemisphere and give your eyes a few minutes to adapt. Ophiuchus occupies a large area of sky; a planisphere or astronomy app can help you trace its outline and identify Rasalhague near the constellation’s northern end. If you use binoculars, steady them against a wall or tripod and take in the surrounding star field. The catalog’s multiple-star classification does not, by itself, mean that a companion will be easy to separate in binoculars or a small telescope. For this session, the main target is the bright primary itself.

A simple way to make the observation more meaningful is to note the date, time and sky conditions, then compare the star’s location with a chart. You can also record whether it appeared white or slightly blue-white; apparent color is subtle and can be affected by atmospheric conditions and individual vision. Rasalhague rewards both kinds of attention: the unaided glance that recognizes a bright star, and the slower one that asks what the light has crossed to reach us.
