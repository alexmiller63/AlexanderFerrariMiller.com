c Ori: A Star in the Catalog

*One entry for HIP 26237 offers several ways to describe the same object—and a reminder that a catalog label is not a complete account.*

A star’s catalog record can look like a tidy identity card. For HIP 26237, it is more like a set of clues. The catalogue lists the object as “c Ori” and classifies it as a young stellar object. In the same entry, it gives a spectral type of B1V+? and records measurements of its brightness, position, motion and parallax. These details describe different aspects of the star; they do not all answer the same question.

The spectral-type entry is particularly suggestive—and carefully qualified. The catalogue gives it as **B1V+?**. The question mark is part of the listed classification, so it is worth preserving rather than smoothing away. The catalog also identifies the object as a young stellar object. Read together, those labels make HIP 26237 an intriguing entry, but they do not, by themselves, explain how the classifications relate or settle the star’s full nature.

The record’s brightness measurements offer a more direct numerical portrait. The catalogue lists a visual magnitude of **4.59**. It also includes measurements in other filters, including U, B, R, I and G, along with infrared J, H and K values. Those numbers are not interchangeable: each belongs to a named filter, and the entry reports them as separate observations. The visual value is a useful place to begin, while the wider set shows how much more a catalog can hold than a single brightness figure.

Position is another piece of the identity. The catalogue gives HIP 26237’s J2000 coordinates as right ascension **05h 35m 23.164653s** and declination **−04° 50′ 18.059785″**. The record marks the coordinate quality as A. A coordinate is not a description of what the star is; it is a way to specify where to look and to distinguish this object from others.

The entry also reports a parallax of **6.5163 milliarcseconds**, with an uncertainty of **1.5234 milliarcseconds**. Parallax is one of the measurements astronomers use to investigate distance, but the uncertainty matters: the catalog does not present the value as exact. The catalogue lists proper motion as **−4.124 milliarcseconds per year** in right ascension (with the declination correction included) and **−1.974 milliarcseconds per year** in declination. These figures describe apparent motion across the sky, not the star’s brightness or classification.

A radial velocity appears in the entry too: **47.10 ± 3 kilometers per second**, with a 1970 reference. That measurement adds another dimension to the record, but its age and source are part of its context.
