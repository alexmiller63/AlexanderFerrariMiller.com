NGC 185: One Galaxy, Several Catalog Labels

*NGC 185 is identified in astronomical records as a companion of NGC 147. Its measurements describe a broad, faint patch of light, while its catalog classifications show why a galaxy’s identity can be more complicated than a single label.*

A galaxy need not have brilliant arms or a sharply defined core to draw attention. NGC 185 is described in the catalogue as a Seyfert 2 galaxy, but the same entry gathers other classifications from different references—including galaxy, active-galaxy, and quasar labels. The range is a reminder that catalog entries preserve the history of how an object has been described, rather than offering a single, final verdict.

The name itself is straightforward: the object discussed here is NGC 185. The catalogue’s entry also notes that it is a companion of NGC 147. That relationship gives the galaxy a place in a wider neighborhood, though the database note alone does not specify the details of their interaction or how the pair formed.

Numbers help make NGC 185’s appearance more concrete. The catalogue lists its apparent visual magnitude as 9.2, with an uncertainty of 0.1, citing a 2012 Astronomical Journal measurement. Its listed optical dimensions are about 11.48 by 9.55 arcminutes, from a 2003 Astronomy & Astrophysics reference. Together, those figures describe an object that covers a substantial area of sky while appearing relatively faint. They are measurements of how it appears from Earth, not a measure of its intrinsic size or luminosity.

The classification details call for more care. The catalogue’s basic-data heading calls NGC 185 a “Seyfert 2 Galaxy,” while its list of other object types includes “G,” “AGN,” “QSO,” and additional labels tied to different references. Its morphology field gives the code “dGspD,” with a D-quality flag. Without treating these entries as interchangeable or equally authoritative, one can see why a galaxy may appear under more than one description in databases: different studies and catalogs classify objects for different purposes. The labels are evidence of that record, not a reason to combine them into a more specific claim than the sources support.

The entry also provides coordinates, placing NGC 185 at right ascension 00h 38m 57.94s and declination +48° 20′ 15.04″ in the J2000 frame. The catalogue lists a heliocentric radial velocity of −181 kilometers per second, with a redshift of −0.000605, citing a 2016 Astrophysical Journal Supplement Series catalog. Those values add another layer to the object’s recorded identity: position on the sky, apparent extent, brightness, and measured motion.

NGC 185 is therefore more than a name attached to a smudge of light. It is a cataloged galaxy with measured properties, a recorded association with NGC 147, and classifications that vary across references. Read carefully, those records tell a modest but useful story: astronomical knowledge accumulates through observations and labels, and the most accurate account sometimes begins by keeping those labels distinct.
