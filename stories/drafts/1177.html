Alfirk: Cepheus’s quiet, pulsing beacon

*This blue-white giant is easy to find in the northern sky—but its small changes in brightness ask observers to look more carefully.*

On a clear evening, the stars of Cepheus sketch a crooked house above the northern horizon. Among them, Alfirk stands out: Beta Cephei, a blue-white star that shines at about magnitude 3.2. It is bright enough to find with the unaided eye under a reasonably dark sky, yet its most interesting behavior is almost impossible to see at a glance. Alfirk subtly changes its light, a reminder that even a steady-looking point can be in motion.

The star lies at right ascension 21h 28m and declination +70° 34′, placing it high in the northern sky for observers at mid-northern latitudes. Look for Cepheus near the familiar W of Cassiopeia and in the general direction of Polaris. In autumn evenings, the constellation is well placed for many northern observers; from farther north, it can remain above the horizon through much of the year. Alfirk’s catalogued visual magnitude is 3.23, the astronomical database of the Strasbourg astronomical data center.

Alfirk’s spectral classification, B0.5 III, identifies it as a hot, blue star with the luminosity class of a giant. The catalogue also classifies it as a beta Cephei variable. Stars in this class undergo small, repeating changes in brightness as they pulsate: their outer layers expand and contract, altering the light we receive. The shifts are modest, so the star does not visibly swell or flicker like a lamp. Its variability is a measured rhythm, not a spectacle for the naked eye.

That makes Alfirk a useful lesson in the difference between seeing and observing. To locate it, start with the broad outline of Cepheus and use binoculars if the constellation is hard to trace. Once you have identified the star, take a moment to look without magnification: Alfirk is a point of light, not a visibly colored or changing orb. A telescope can sharpen the view, but it will not make the brightness cycle obvious. The challenge is not to magnify the star; it is to compare its light carefully over time.

For a simple observing project, note the date, time, sky conditions and the star’s apparent brightness. More serious variable-star work uses comparison stars nearby in the sky, whose light is treated as steady, and records repeated estimates or instrumental measurements. Avoid drawing conclusions from a single look: changing haze, altitude and eyesight can imitate a star’s variation. Because Alfirk’s changes are small, unaided-eye estimates are unlikely to reveal its pulse reliably. A camera or photometer, used consistently, can help reveal the pattern.

In the end, Alfirk offers two kinds of reward. It is an accessible marker in a northern constellation, and it is a reminder that stars are not fixed lamps. The distant giant quietly changes its light while Cepheus turns overhead. Finding Alfirk takes only a clear night and a little sky knowledge; following its pulse takes patience.
