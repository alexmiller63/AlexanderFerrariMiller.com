Nusakan, the Quiet Spark in the Northern Crown

*An F-type variable star marks a delicate turn in Corona Borealis—and rewards a little patience more than a powerful telescope.*

Nusakan is not a blaze but a steady-looking point in a small, graceful constellation. Find it in Corona Borealis, the Northern Crown: a curved chain of stars between the better-known patterns of Boötes and Hercules. The Crown’s arc is easy to recognize once you spot it, and Nusakan, also cataloged as Beta Coronae Borealis, sits among its jewels.

Its name may sound like a spell, but the star’s scientific identity is wonderfully specific. The catalogue lists Nusakan as an Alpha2 Canum Venaticorum variable, a category of variable star, and gives it the spectral classification F2VpSrCrEuSi. In that notation, F2 describes its spectral type; the remaining letters flag unusual spectral features, including those associated with strontium, chromium, europium and silicon. The classification hints that this is not an entirely ordinary star, even if it looks calm to the unaided eye.

The word “variable” needn’t mean a star that visibly pulses like a warning light. Nusakan’s listing gives a visual magnitude of about 3.68—bright enough for naked-eye viewing under a reasonably dark sky—but its cataloged variability is a reason for closer study, not a promise of an obvious nightly change. The star’s measured position and parallax also give us a way to place it in space: the catalogue reports a parallax of about 27.9 milliarcseconds. In plain language, that tiny apparent shift as Earth moves around the Sun puts Nusakan roughly 117 light-years away. The estimate is approximate; the catalog’s listed parallax uncertainty matters at that scale.

To observe Nusakan, start with your eyes. In Northern Hemisphere evenings, Corona Borealis is a rewarding target in the late spring and summer, when the constellation climbs into a comfortable part of the sky. Look for its semicircle of stars rather than searching for Nusakan in isolation. A star chart or planetarium app can help you identify the Crown; then compare the sky with the chart until the arc’s shape clicks into place.

Binoculars are useful if city glow or haze makes the fainter members of the constellation hard to pick out, though Nusakan itself should be visible without them under decent conditions. A small telescope will show a tighter field and sharpen the surrounding star pattern, but don’t expect a dramatic surface or color change: stars remain points of light in ordinary amateur instruments. For an observing log, note the date, time, sky conditions and whether you used optical aid. If you want to investigate the variable-star designation, repeated estimates over many nights are more meaningful than a single glance.

There is a particular pleasure in finding this star by its neighborhood. The Crown’s curve offers a simple route through the sky, and Nusakan’s catalog details open a deeper one: a measured distance, a spectral signature, and a place in a class of variable stars. The light reaches your eye as a quiet point; the science reveals a star with a more intricate story.
