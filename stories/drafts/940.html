Alchiba: The Raven’s Quiet Beacon

*An easy-to-miss spring star with a measurable drift across the sky—and a useful lesson in how astronomers read starlight.*

Alchiba is not the brightest point in Corvus, the Raven. At magnitude 4, it is the sort of star that can disappear in a city’s glow, yet reward anyone who takes the time to find the small southern constellation. Its catalog identity is Alpha Corvi, or HIP 59199; its name sounds ancient, but its coordinates and motion belong to the precision era of modern astronomy.

The numbers give Alchiba a more intimate scale. SIMBAD lists its parallax as 66.77 milliarcseconds. Parallax is the apparent shift of a nearby star against more distant background stars as Earth moves around the Sun. Turning that measurement into a distance puts Alchiba about 15 parsecs away—roughly 49 light-years. That is close by stellar standards, though the light arriving tonight began its journey before the early 1970s.

Alchiba’s classification is F1V. The “F1” describes its spectral type, a clue to the star’s surface temperature and the light it emits; “V” identifies it as a main-sequence star, like the Sun, steadily producing energy through nuclear fusion in its core. The catalog’s listed visual magnitude is 4.01. Under a dark sky, that is bright enough for unaided vision, but not bright enough to shrug off haze, moonlight or nearby streetlamps.

There is another subtlety: Alchiba is listed as a high-proper-motion star. Proper motion is a star’s apparent change of position across the sky, caused by its movement through space relative to the Sun. SIMBAD gives its motion as about 97 milliarcseconds per year east-west and 40 milliarcseconds per year north-south (with the east-west value adjusted for its position on the celestial sphere). Those tiny annual steps add up over centuries, not over an evening at the eyepiece. The star will look fixed to the eye; a long record of careful measurements reveals the drift.

**To observe:** Look for Corvus on spring evenings, low in the southern sky from many northern locations. A sky map or planetarium app can help identify the constellation and guide you to Alpha Corvi; its catalog position is right ascension 12h 08m, declination −24° 44′. From a dark site, try first with unaided eyes, then use binoculars to steady the view and pick the star out against the surrounding field. In a telescope, Alchiba is not a dramatic disk—stars are too distant for ordinary telescopes to resolve as disks—but it can be a satisfying target for practicing precise pointing. Give your eyes time to adapt, and choose a clear view toward the southern horizon.

The pleasure of Alchiba is partly in the contrast: a modest point of light, and a rich story encoded in measurements. Its color classification, distance and slow apparent motion are not visible as separate features in the eyepiece. They emerge through spectroscopy, parallax and repeated astrometry—the patient craft of turning starlight into evidence.
