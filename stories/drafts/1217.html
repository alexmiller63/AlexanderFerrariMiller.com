Tiaki: A Red Giant on the Southern Wing

*An easy naked-eye target in Grus, Tiaki offers a glimpse of a cool giant star—and a reminder that some stars change their light over time.*

Tiaki, catalogued as β Gruis and HIP 112122, is one of the bright stars that helps give the southern constellation Grus, the Crane, its shape. SIMBAD lists it as a cool M4.5 III giant and a candidate long-period variable. That “candidate” matters: the catalogue flags a possible kind of slow brightness change, not a guaranteed spectacle or a published schedule of variations. Still, Tiaki rewards a look, even if you simply find it and let your eyes rest on its steady-seeming glow.

The star lies at right ascension 22h 42m and declination −46° 53′. Its listed parallax, 18.43 milliarcseconds, puts it roughly 54 parsecs—about 177 light-years—from Earth. Parallax is the tiny apparent shift a nearby star makes against more distant background stars as Earth moves around the Sun. The “M4.5 III” classification tells another part of the story: Tiaki is a relatively cool, evolved giant. Its outer layers extend far beyond the compact scale of a main-sequence star like the Sun.

Long-period variable stars change brightness over timescales of months or years. Many are cool giants whose outer layers expand and contract, affecting the light they emit. SIMBAD’s entry calls Tiaki a long-period variable candidate; it does not provide a period or range of brightness in the cited record. So treat the variable label as an invitation to observe carefully, not a promise that you will see change in a single night.

For a first sighting, look in the southern sky during spring in the Southern Hemisphere. Grus is best placed for evening observing around October, when Tiaki’s position brings it high above the horizon for many southern observers. At latitude 35° south, for instance, it culminates roughly 78° above the northern horizon. From far northern locations it is much harder: its southerly declination means it barely clears the horizon near 43° north and remains below it farther north.

Tiaki is bright enough to seek with the unaided eye under a reasonably dark sky; SIMBAD lists a visual magnitude of 2.11. Start with a planisphere or star-chart app set to your location and date, and use the constellation outline to pick it out. Binoculars can help when the horizon is hazy or city lights wash out fainter stars, but let your eyes adapt to darkness first. Note the date, time, sky conditions and how bright Tiaki appears compared with nearby stars. If you repeat that simple estimate over many weeks, you can contribute useful observations of a star whose changes are slow.

The catalogue identifies a target with coordinates and measurements; observing gives it a place in your own sky. Find Tiaki on a clear evening, then return to it. Its light has travelled for generations before reaching you, and the star may be changing on a timescale longer than any single night.
