Alpha Ind: The Quiet Traveler of Indus

*At magnitude 3.1, this southern star is easy to spot under a dark sky—and its measured motion gives it a longer story to tell.*

Alpha Ind doesn’t announce itself with a dramatic color or a famous constellation shape. It is simply a steady point of light in Indus, the southern constellation of the Indian. But look its way on a clear night and you are seeing a nearby star whose position is slowly changing against the distant background.

The name is also a useful invitation to explore. “Alpha” marks the star as the constellation’s alpha-designated star; “Ind” is the standard abbreviation for Indus. Alpha Ind shines at visual magnitude 3.11, bright enough to see with the unaided eye from a dark site. Its light began its journey roughly a century ago: the star’s measured parallax places it about 30 parsecs away, or approximately 99 light-years from Earth.

That distance comes from a small shift in apparent position as Earth circles the Sun. Astronomers measure the shift—parallax—and use geometry to work out how far away the star is. Alpha Ind’s parallax is about 32.9 milliarcseconds, a tiny angle, but one that pins down its neighborhood in the solar vicinity.

The star’s classification, K0III-IV, offers another clue to its character. “K0” identifies its spectral type, while “III-IV” places it between the giant and subgiant luminosity classes. In other words, Alpha Ind is not classified as a main-sequence star like the Sun; its spectrum indicates a star in a more evolved stage. The catalogue evidence is precise about the classification, but it does not, by itself, tell us the details of the star’s interior or future.

Its movement across the sky is subtle but measurable. SIMBAD lists a proper motion of about 51 milliarcseconds per year eastward and 66 milliarcseconds per year northward. Combined, that is roughly 83 milliarcseconds annually—less than a tenth of an arcsecond, far too little to notice from one night to the next. Over a century, however, the shift adds up to about eight arcseconds. The star is not fixed in the celestial pattern; it only appears so because the sky is vast and our observing lifetimes are brief.

**How to find it:** Alpha Ind is at right ascension 20h 37m and declination −47° 17′ (J2000 coordinates). Use those coordinates in a planetarium app or star chart to locate Indus, then look for a naked-eye star of about third magnitude. It is best placed for observers with a clear view toward the southern sky; from far northern locations it may sit very low or remain below the horizon. A pair of binoculars can help pick it out when the sky is bright or hazy, though they are not required under dark conditions. Give your eyes time to adapt, and avoid judging the star’s color through turbulent air near the horizon.

Alpha Ind rewards a patient kind of attention. First find the point of light; then imagine the slow, steady displacement hidden in catalogue measurements. The star’s motion is almost imperceptible to us, yet it is part of the same grand movement that carries every star through the Milky Way.
