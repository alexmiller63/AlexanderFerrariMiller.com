Rastaban, the Dragon’s Steady Eye

*An easy naked-eye star with the light of an evolved giant, Rastaban rewards a quiet look north on a summer night.*

The first thing to notice about Rastaban is how little it asks of you. No telescope, no dark-sky expedition, no special trick: find a clear patch of northern sky, let your eyes adjust, and look for the bright point that marks Beta Draconis. It shines at about magnitude 2.8—bright enough to pick out without optical aid under ordinary conditions, though city glare can make the search harder.

The name and catalogue designation point to the same star: Rastaban is Beta Draconis, listed by the catalogue as HIP 85670. Its position, for the J2000 epoch, is right ascension 17 hours 30 minutes and declination +52 degrees 18 minutes. Those coordinates place it far north of the celestial equator, in the constellation Draco. From a location north of roughly 38 degrees latitude, Rastaban stays above the horizon all night; farther south, it still rises high enough to be a rewarding target when Draco is well placed.

To find it, start with the season and the direction. On northern summer evenings, Draco lies across the northern sky. Give your eyes a few minutes away from bright screens, then use a star chart or astronomy app set to your location to trace the constellation’s winding outline. Rastaban is a useful waypoint: its brightness makes it easier to recognize than many of the fainter stars around it. If you have binoculars, scan the surrounding field slowly; the instrument won’t change the star into a disk, but it can bring more of Draco’s star pattern into view. Avoid looking through a window, where reflections and imperfect glass can soften the scene.

There is more to this point of light than its unassuming appearance suggests. The catalogue gives Rastaban the spectral classification G2 Ib–IIa. The “G2” indicates a G-type star, while the luminosity classes Ib–IIa describe a luminous, evolved star—not a modest, Sun-like dwarf. Its apparent brightness is therefore not a simple measure of how nearby it is. The catalogue lists a parallax of 8.58 milliarcseconds. Turning that angle into a distance gives roughly 117 parsecs, or about 380 light-years. The number is an estimate, but it offers a useful change of scale: the light reaching your eyes tonight began its journey centuries ago.

That long journey makes a small observing ritual feel unexpectedly deep. Find Rastaban once, then return to it on another clear evening. The star will seem fixed against the familiar pattern, though its measured proper motion shows that it slowly shifts over time. Our eyes register the quiet point; instruments record the motion, distance and stellar classification behind it.

For the simplest view, go out after twilight, face north, and give yourself time to identify the pattern rather than hunting for a single star in isolation. A printed chart or planetarium app can help, especially if you are new to Draco. Once you have found Rastaban, pause before moving on. Its light is not spectacular in the way of a meteor or a planet, but it carries a record of a distant, evolved star—and a reminder that even a steady point in the sky has a history.
