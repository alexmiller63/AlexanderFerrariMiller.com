Alkes: The Cup’s Quiet Flame

*An orange giant in Crater offers a modest naked-eye target—and a reminder that even familiar-looking stars are in motion.*

Some stars announce themselves with brilliance. Alkes does something quieter: it waits in the small constellation Crater, the Cup, a warm point of light on the southern side of the spring sky. Its catalog designation is Alpha Crateris, and its position and stellar data are recorded by [SIMBAD](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=HIP%2053740).

The name Alkes suits a star that can feel like a small treasure found after searching. At visual magnitude 4.07, it is bright enough to see without optical aid under a reasonably dark sky, but it may be lost in city glare. Crater itself is not among the sky’s most conspicuous constellations. The pleasure here is less a dramatic first sighting than the moment a faint pattern resolves—and one of its stars begins to stand out.

Alkes lies about 160 light-years away. That estimate follows from its measured parallax: about 20.37 milliarcseconds, the tiny apparent shift caused by Earth viewing the star from different parts of its orbit. Its light began its journey roughly 160 years ago, arriving now from a star whose catalog position is given as right ascension 10 hours 59 minutes and declination 18 degrees south. Those coordinates, like the parallax, come from the [SIMBAD entry for HIP 53740](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=HIP%2053740).

The same entry classifies Alkes as K0IIIb. In stellar shorthand, K0 identifies a relatively cool, orange-hued star, while III marks it as a giant: a star that has expanded beyond the main-sequence phase in which stars spend most of their lives steadily fusing hydrogen in their cores. “Giant” does not mean that Alkes dominates the night sky. Its apparent brightness is tempered by distance, and its magnitude makes it a subtle target compared with the brightest stars nearby.

There is another kind of motion hidden in the catalog. Alkes has a measured proper motion of about 0.48 arcseconds per year across the sky. That is far too small to notice from one evening to the next, but over centuries it shifts the star’s position against the more distant background. The sky’s patterns feel fixed to us; they are more like a slow dance than a painted ceiling.

**To observe:** Look for Crater on spring evenings, when it is well placed in the southern sky. From mid-northern latitudes, Alkes remains relatively low; the farther north you are, the lower it culminates. Begin with a current sky chart or planetarium app and use the listed coordinates—RA 10h 59m, Dec −18°—to confirm the field. Let your eyes adapt to darkness, then scan the Cup with the naked eye. If light pollution washes out the pattern, binoculars can help distinguish its stars, though Alkes itself is bright enough for unaided viewing from a dark site. A small telescope will show it as a point of light, not a disk; its story is in its color, distance and motion, rather than surface detail.

For one observing session, that is enough: find the Cup, pick out its quiet flame, and remember that the light reaching you has crossed a gulf measured in light-years.

**Source:** [SIMBAD: HIP 53740 / Alpha Crateris](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=HIP%2053740)
