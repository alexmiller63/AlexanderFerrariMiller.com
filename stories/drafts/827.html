Gomeisa: A Small Dog’s Blue-White Beacon

*Canis Minor has only two stars that stand out to the naked eye. One of them is a hot, emission-line star whose quiet glimmer hints at a lively story.*

On a clear winter evening, Orion gets the attention. Follow the line of his belt, then look toward brilliant Procyon, a little east of the Hunter. Nearby, a fainter blue-white point marks Gomeisa, Beta Canis Minoris. It is one of the two principal stars of Canis Minor, the “Lesser Dog”—a modest constellation with a bright companion and a lesser-known star worth seeking out.

Gomeisa’s light is easy to underestimate. At visual magnitude 2.89, it is comfortably visible without optical aid from a reasonably dark site, though city glare can wash it out. Its catalogued position is right ascension 7 hours 27 minutes, declination +8 degrees 17 minutes. On the sky, that places it roughly four degrees northwest of Procyon—about the width of your fist held at arm’s length. Start with Procyon, then sweep a little north and west to pick out Gomeisa. Binoculars help in bright skies, but the star is best appreciated unaided: binoculars show more surrounding stars, while a telescope’s magnification can make locating it less convenient.

The name comes with a scientific clue. SIMBAD lists Gomeisa as a B8Ve star and a Be star. “B8” identifies a hot, blue-white spectral type; the “e” marks emission lines in its spectrum. These lines indicate that the star’s light carries the signature of glowing gas. Be stars are associated with gas around the star, a reminder that a point of light can be more physically complicated than it appears to the eye. Gomeisa’s brightness is therefore not the whole story: astronomers can study the light spread into a spectrum to learn about material around the star.

It is also a neighbor, by stellar standards. SIMBAD lists a parallax of 20.17 milliarcseconds. Parallax is the apparent shift of a nearby star against more distant background stars as Earth moves around the Sun. Turning that value into a distance gives about 50 parsecs, or roughly 162 light-years. The number is too vast to feel in a backyard, but it adds scale to the view: the starlight arriving tonight began its journey when the first modern humans were spreading across Earth.

For observing, choose a night when Canis Minor is well above the horizon. In mid-northern latitudes, that usually means winter evenings into early spring. Find Orion first, then use Procyon as your stepping-stone. Give your eyes 15 to 20 minutes to adapt to darkness, and avoid looking at a phone screen just before searching. If Gomeisa seems elusive, try binoculars and sweep slowly around the expected spot; then look again without them. Its steady blue-white point is the target, not a dramatic pattern.

A small constellation rewards a patient glance. Procyon may be the dog’s unmistakable lantern, but Gomeisa offers a subtler invitation: a visible star whose spectrum reveals activity hidden inside its simple pinprick of light.
