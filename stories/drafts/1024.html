Unukalhai: A Steady Light in Serpens

An orange giant with a measurable motion across the sky, this bright star rewards both a quick glance and a closer look.

Some stars announce themselves with a flash; Unukalhai keeps a quieter appointment. Look toward Serpens on a clear night and you’ll find a warm point of light, bright enough to catch the eye without needing a telescope. Its catalogue designation is Alpha Serpentis, and the catalogue identifies it as HIP 77070. The database lists its visual magnitude as 2.63—bright, though not among the very brightest stars in the sky.

That modest brightness comes from a star quite unlike the Sun. The catalogue gives Unukalhai’s spectral type as K2IIIbCN1. The “K2” marks a cooler, orange-toned star; “III” identifies it as a giant. In other words, Unukalhai has expanded beyond the compact main-sequence state our Sun occupies today. The classification’s additional letters record further spectral details, but the broad picture is already vivid: this is a giant star, shining with a color that can seem subtly golden beside bluer stars.

Its distance can be estimated from its parallax, the tiny apparent shift measured as Earth moves around the Sun. The catalogue lists a parallax of 43.945 milliarcseconds. Turning that value into distance gives roughly 23 parsecs, or about 74 light-years. The light reaching your eyes tonight began its journey when events on Earth belonged to another era—not ancient by cosmic standards, but far beyond the span of a human life.

The catalogue also records Unukalhai’s proper motion: about 134 milliarcseconds per year in right ascension and 46 in declination. That is a real drift against the background of more distant stars, though it is far too small to notice in a single evening. The sky’s patterns feel fixed because our observing lives are brief; stellar measurements reveal the slow motion beneath that familiar stillness.

How to observe: Start with the naked eye. Find Serpens on a star chart, planisphere or astronomy app, then use the chart to identify Alpha Serpentis within the constellation. The catalogue’s J2000 coordinates are right ascension 15h 44m 16s and declination +6° 25′ 32″; enter them into a planetarium app if you want a precise pointer. These coordinates describe a standard reference position, not a promise that the star will be above your horizon at any particular hour. Check a current sky chart for your location and date.

Choose a spot with an open view and give your eyes time to adjust. Unukalhai should be a straightforward naked-eye target under a reasonably dark sky; binoculars can help distinguish it from the surrounding star field, though they are not required. A small telescope will show it as a point of light, not a disk. Try comparing its hue with nearby stars: color impressions vary with eyesight, sky conditions and the contrast in the eyepiece.

There is pleasure in knowing what the point represents, but the observation doesn’t depend on memorizing its numbers. Let the chart guide you to Serpens; then look up and meet a distant giant whose light has crossed the dark to become, for a moment, part of your night.
