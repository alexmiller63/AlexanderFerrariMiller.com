The Eyes with 4435

*Two galaxies in Virgo share a famous nickname—and a field of view. Here’s how to find them, and what to look for.*

On a clear spring evening, point a telescope into Virgo and you may find two faint smudges looking back: NGC 4438 and its close neighbor NGC 4435, together nicknamed “The Eyes.” They are galaxies, not stars, and their likeness to a pair of watchful eyes comes from their arrangement in the sky. The view is subtle, but knowing what you’re seeing makes it a rewarding stop among the galaxies of Virgo.

NGC 4438 is the larger-looking member of the pair. In SIMBAD, its position is listed as right ascension 12h 27m 45.7s and declination +13° 00′ 32″ (J2000). The catalog gives its apparent dimensions as about 8.9 by 5.0 arcminutes and classifies it as a spiral galaxy with a LINER-type active galactic nucleus. A LINER is a galaxy nucleus whose spectrum shows emission from relatively low-ionization atoms; that label describes the light, not a bright point that observers should expect to see through an eyepiece.

The two galaxies are close neighbors on the sky, though they are separate objects. Their proximity and the disturbed appearance of NGC 4438 have made the pair a natural subject for studies of how galaxies change in crowded environments. In a telescope, however, the first impression is less about structure than contrast: two uneven patches of light against a dark background.

To find them, begin with the bright stars of Virgo and use a detailed star chart or planetarium app to guide you to the pair. NGC 4438’s listed coordinates are a useful check when setting digital circles. The galaxies lie in the broad region between Denebola, in Leo, and Vindemiatrix, in Virgo, amid the rich galaxy fields of the Virgo Cluster. A low-power eyepiece can help place both in the same view; they are separated by only a few arcminutes.

Give your eyes time to adjust to darkness, and observe on a moonless night away from bright lights. Start with low magnification, then try a moderate increase if the sky is steady. Use averted vision—looking slightly to the side of the galaxies—to make their faint light easier to detect. An 8-inch telescope under a dark sky offers a reasonable chance of showing both as small, hazy patches; larger instruments and excellent conditions may reveal more of NGC 4438’s elongated outline. Don’t expect the crisp detail of a photograph: the galaxies’ low surface brightness makes them a test of patience as much as aperture.

The “eyes” may not leap out at first. Let the field settle, compare the two glows, and look for their paired arrangement. Then remember that each smudge is a galaxy, composed of stars too distant to resolve individually. What appears as a quiet gaze is, in fact, light gathered across enormous distances—and a small, memorable encounter with the crowded neighborhood of Virgo.
