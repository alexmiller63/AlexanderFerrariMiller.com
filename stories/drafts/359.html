Collinder 380: A Loose Gathering in the Summer Sky

NGC 6633 is a broad open cluster in Ophiuchus. Its generous span makes it a rewarding target for binoculars and low-power telescopes.

On a clear summer evening, look toward the constellation Ophiuchus for a patch of sky that rewards a wide view. Collinder 380, also catalogued as NGC 6633, is an open star cluster whose listed apparent diameter is about 43 arcminutes—roughly one and a half times the Moon’s width. Rather than a tight knot, it is a spacious gathering, best approached with an instrument that takes in the whole scene.

The cluster’s coordinates are approximately right ascension 18 hours 27 minutes and declination +6 degrees 37 minutes. That puts it in the summer sky for northern observers, though its modest declination means it will not climb especially high from many northern locations. Choose a site with a clear southern horizon, and observe when Ophiuchus is well above nearby rooftops and trees. The position, angular size and classification as an open cluster are listed by the catalogue.

An open cluster is a family of stars associated with one another, rather than a chance pattern of unrelated points. The word “family” does not mean the stars are packed together like beads: in Collinder 380, the broad apparent spread is part of the attraction. At 43 arcminutes, the cluster can be cramped by high magnification. Begin with binoculars or a telescope at low power, then increase magnification only if you want to inspect smaller parts of the field. Give your eyes time to adapt to the dark, and let the view settle before deciding how many stars stand out. Under a bright sky, fewer members may be apparent; a darker site can bring the pattern into better relief.

The catalog measurements offer a second way to picture the cluster—not as a view through an eyepiece, but as a population moving through space. The catalogue lists a parallax of 2.5232 milliarcseconds. A simple inversion of that value suggests a distance of roughly 400 parsecs, or about 1,300 light-years; this is an estimate based on the listed parallax, not a tape measure to every star. The same entry gives a proper motion of about 1.16 milliarcseconds per year eastward and 1.74 milliarcseconds per year southward, and a radial velocity of −28.70 kilometres per second. Proper motion describes apparent motion across the sky; radial velocity describes motion toward or away from us along our line of sight. Together, these measurements help astronomers characterize stellar motion in three dimensions.

For a first visit, don’t rush to identify every point. Center the field near the published coordinates, keep the magnification modest and notice how the stars are distributed across the view. Then try a slightly narrower field and compare: the cluster’s character changes as the framing changes. Collinder 380 is a useful reminder that “cluster” need not mean compact. Sometimes the pleasure lies in seeing a collection of stars spread generously across the dark.
