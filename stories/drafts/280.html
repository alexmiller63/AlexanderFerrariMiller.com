Siamese Twins: A Close Pair in Virgo

*Two spiral galaxies share a name that hints at their appearance. Spotting the resemblance takes dark skies, patience and a telescope.*

In the spring sky, the Siamese Twins offer a quiet lesson in perspective. NGC 4567 and NGC 4568 are two galaxies seen close together from Earth; in a telescope, their faint light can suggest a pair of overlapping forms. The nickname makes a promise, but not a guarantee: these are subtle targets, and the “twins” may appear more like a soft patch than two neatly separated spirals.

The catalog entry for NGC 4567 places it at right ascension 12h 36m 32.7s and declination +11° 15′ 29″ (J2000). It lists the galaxy’s apparent visual magnitude as 11.31 and its angular size as about 3.9 by 2.4 arcminutes. Those figures help set expectations: the target is small on the sky and faint compared with the stars used to find it. The entry classifies it as an SA galaxy, a designation for an unbarred spiral. [SIMBAD: NGC 4567](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%204567)

A galaxy’s glow is the combined light of many stars, spread across a wide area. That makes a dim galaxy harder to see than a star of similar total brightness: its light does not arrive at one sharp point. In a close pair, apparent proximity also doesn’t mean the galaxies are side by side in space in the simple way two stars in a constellation might be. They are distant systems, and their shapes are projected onto the same small region of our sky. A telescope gathers more light than the eye alone, but dark skies and careful observing matter too.

Look for the pair on a clear spring evening, when the constellation Virgo climbs high in the sky. A star chart or planetarium app can guide you to the coordinates; use a low-power eyepiece first to identify the surrounding star field, then increase magnification if the view allows. A telescope of moderate aperture under a dark, transparent sky offers a better chance than binoculars. Let your eyes adapt to the dark for 20 minutes or more, and shield the eyepiece from stray lights.

Don’t expect a colorful portrait. Galaxies usually appear as pale, low-contrast smudges in amateur telescopes. Try averted vision: look just to one side of the target rather than directly at it, so the more light-sensitive parts of your eye can detect the faint glow. Averted vision won’t reveal details that the sky and equipment can’t deliver, but it can help make a faint object easier to notice. Give yourself time at the eyepiece; brief glances can miss a feature that emerges after patient viewing.

If the Siamese Twins remain indistinct, the night is not wasted. The search itself draws the eye across a rich stretch of spring sky, and the pair’s name invites a useful question: how much of what we see is the object itself, and how much is the way distance and direction arrange it for us? With a little patience, a faint patch becomes a reminder that the sky’s smallest-looking encounters can belong to immense worlds.
