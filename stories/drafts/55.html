Gacrux: The ember at the top of the Southern Cross

An orange-red giant gives Crux its northern tip—and a simple target for southern-sky observers.

On an autumn evening in the Southern Hemisphere, look south for a small, unmistakable cross. Its upper point glows a different color from the blue-white stars nearby: Gacrux, a red giant whose warm hue makes the Southern Cross feel less like a diagram and more like a thing in the night.

Gacrux is the traditional name of Gamma Crucis, the star at the northern end of Crux’s long axis. The astronomical database the catalogue identifies it as a high-proper-motion star and classifies its spectrum as M3.5III: an M-type giant. Its apparent visual magnitude is listed as 1.64, bright enough to stand out to the unaided eye under ordinary dark-sky conditions. The catalogue gives a parallax of 36.83 milliarcseconds. Turning that into distance gives roughly 27 parsecs, or about 89 light-years. These figures are measurements, not a portrait of the star’s surface: the ruddy color is the eye’s impression of light from a cool giant, not a view of its actual shape.

“Giant” describes a stage in a star’s life, not a promise that it will look enormous from Earth. Gacrux is far enough away to remain a point of light to the naked eye. Its spectrum, however, tells astronomers that its surface is cooler than that of blue-white stars such as those that dominate the Southern Cross’s other bright points. Cooler stars radiate proportionally more of their visible light toward the red end of the spectrum. That is why Gacrux can look like an ember set among sharper, whiter sparks.

The star also shifts against the background over time. The catalogue lists its proper motion as about 28 milliarcseconds per year in right ascension and –265 in declination. Those numbers are tiny on a human timescale; no observer will watch Gacrux visibly travel across the sky in a night. But accumulated over centuries, stellar motions alter the familiar patterns that seem fixed to us.

How to find it: Crux is compact, but its four main stars make a clear cross. Gacrux marks the northern end of the longer arm; Acrux marks the southern end. Start with the Southern Cross itself, rather than trying to identify Gacrux in isolation. In much of the Southern Hemisphere, Crux is a reliable evening landmark during autumn. From farther north, it stays low in the southern sky and may be hidden by buildings, trees or haze; choose a place with an unobstructed view toward the south.

Gacrux needs no telescope. Give your eyes several minutes to adapt to the dark, then compare its color with nearby stars. Binoculars can sharpen the surrounding star field, but they are not required to enjoy the contrast. A small telescope will not reveal a disk; it may instead make the star’s color and the neighboring field easier to examine. For the best view, observe on a clear, moonless night and let the cross rise well above the horizon.

A star can be both a navigational marker and a distant physical world. Gacrux is especially satisfying because it offers both at once: a bright point that helps define a famous southern pattern, and a cool giant whose measured light carries clues to what kind of star it is.
