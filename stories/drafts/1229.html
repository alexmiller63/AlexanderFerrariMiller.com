Fomalhaut: A Southern Beacon on the Move

*Bright, nearby and easy to spot when it rides high, the star offers a naked-eye glimpse of stellar motion—and a useful lesson in reading the sky.*

Fomalhaut can look like a solitary lantern in the southern sky. Its name is more than a point of light: catalogues identify it as Alpha Piscis Austrini, and measurements place it about 25 light-years from Earth. That combination—bright, close and readily found—makes it a fine star to return to through the seasons.

The catalogue lists Fomalhaut at visual magnitude 1.16, spectral type A4V and parallax 129.81 milliarcseconds. Parallax is the apparent shift of a nearby star against more distant background stars as Earth travels around the Sun. Converting that measurement gives a distance of about 7.7 parsecs, or 25 light-years. The A4V classification identifies it as an A-type main-sequence star. These figures turn a bright point into a physical object: a star whose light has crossed a measurable, finite stretch of space to reach us.

Fomalhaut is moving across the sky, too—not visibly from one night to the next, but measurably over time. The catalogue gives its proper motion as about 329 milliarcseconds per year eastward in right ascension and 165 milliarcseconds per year southward in declination. Together, that is roughly 0.37 arcsecond per year, or about 37 arcseconds in a century. The shift is tiny beside the Moon’s apparent width, but it is a reminder that the constellations are not fixed drawings. Their stars drift at different speeds and in different directions.

For observers, Fomalhaut’s catalogue position is a practical guide: right ascension 22 hours 57 minutes, declination nearly 30 degrees south. Look toward the southern sky on autumn evenings in the Northern Hemisphere; in the Southern Hemisphere, the corresponding season is spring. Around late September, it reaches its highest point near midnight local time, then culminates earlier as the weeks pass. “Highest” is relative: at latitude 40 degrees north, it peaks only about 20 degrees above the southern horizon. Choose a spot with a clear view south, away from trees and buildings.

You do not need a telescope to find it. At magnitude 1.16, Fomalhaut is visible to the unaided eye under a reasonably dark sky. Let your eyes adjust, then use a star chart or astronomy app set to your location to confirm the position; the star’s southern declination can make it easy to miss from northern sites where it stays low. Binoculars can help you pick it out in a bright or hazy sky, but they are not required.

When you find it, pause before moving on. The point you see is both a dependable seasonal marker and a changing neighbor, edging across the celestial sphere as the years accumulate. A glance at its position tonight may seem no different from the view your grandparents had. Compare careful measurements across generations, though, and Fomalhaut’s quiet journey becomes part of the view.
