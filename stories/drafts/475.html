Alpha Men: A Southern Star on the Move

*In the faint constellation Mensa, this fifth-magnitude sunlike star rewards patient observers with a glimpse of a nearby system—and a reminder that the sky is never quite still.*

**The story**

Some stars announce themselves with brilliance. Alpha Men is quieter: a modest point of light in the southern constellation Mensa, best appreciated when the sky is dark and the air is clear. Its apparent visual magnitude is about 5.1, near the limit of unaided-eye visibility under excellent conditions. From a light-polluted neighborhood, it may vanish into the background; from a dark southern sky, it offers a small, steady target to seek out.

The star is close by astronomical standards. Its measured parallax is about 97.9 milliarcseconds. Parallax is the apparent annual shift of a nearby star against more distant background stars as Earth moves around the Sun. Turning that measurement into distance gives roughly 10.2 parsecs, or 33 light-years. Alpha Men is classified G7V: a G-type main-sequence star, in the broad family of stars that includes the Sun. That classification describes its stellar type, not a claim that it is a twin of our own star.

Alpha Men also has a notable motion across the sky. Its measured proper motion—the apparent drift against the background over time—is about 122 milliarcseconds per year eastward and 212 milliarcseconds per year southward. Combined, that is roughly a quarter of an arcsecond annually. The shift is far too subtle to watch from night to night, but precise measurements across years reveal it. The catalogued radial velocity is +36.1 kilometres per second, motion measured along our line of sight.

**Scientific context**

The star’s coordinates place it deep in the southern sky: right ascension 6 hours 10 minutes, declination −74 degrees 45 minutes (J2000). Declination is the celestial equivalent of latitude. At that far-southern position, Alpha Men is inaccessible from locations north of about 15 degrees north latitude. For observers farther south than roughly 15 degrees south, it stays above the horizon all night, circling near the south celestial pole.

Its position also gives the observing season a useful rhythm. Around December, when the Sun lies opposite this region of sky, Alpha Men is well placed in the evening. The exact time it clears local obstacles depends on your location, so a star chart or planetarium app can help you plan.

**Observing guidance**

Look toward the southern sky and use a chart or app set to Alpha Men’s coordinates: RA 6h 10m, Dec −74° 45′. Because the star is faint, begin from a dark site and allow your eyes time to adapt. Binoculars can make the search easier, though they will show many more stars than the naked eye; use the chart to distinguish Alpha Men from its field. A telescope is not required to see it, and at this brightness the view is a point of light rather than a disk.

If you do find it, take a moment with the idea that the star’s position is not fixed. Its gentle drift is invisible in a single observing session, yet measured across generations of astrometry. Alpha Men is no showpiece by brightness. Its appeal is subtler: a nearby star, far south, moving steadily through the galaxy.
