MCG 8-22-2: A Long Island of Light

*Dek: In the spring sky of Ursa Major, a modest telescope can reveal a distant spiral whose thin glow offers a lesson in scale—and in the patience of looking.*

Spring evenings bring the Great Bear into a commanding place in the northern sky. Its familiar bowl and handle are easy to recognize; harder to notice, but well worth seeking, is the faint galaxy catalogued as **MCG 8-22-2**, also known as NGC 3877.

The galaxy lies at roughly 11 hours 46 minutes right ascension and +47° 30′ declination, in Ursa Major. Its light reaches us as a narrow oval, about 5 by 1 arcminutes across in optical measurements. That slender shape hints at a spiral disk seen at an angle: we look across much of the galaxy’s face, rather than straight down onto it. SIMBAD classifies its morphology as SA, a designation for an unbarred spiral. Its catalogued B-band magnitude is 11.8—not a naked-eye object, but a promising target for a night under dark skies. [SIMBAD: NGC 3877](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%203877)

A galaxy’s apparent brightness is not the same as its surface brightness. Its light is spread across an extended patch, so it can be harder to detect than a star of similar total brightness. That is why MCG 8-22-2 rewards averted vision: instead of staring directly at the galaxy, look just to one side of it. The eye’s more light-sensitive peripheral vision may pick up the faint oval more readily.

The measured redshift listed for NGC 3877 corresponds to a recession velocity of about 893 kilometers per second. At such a relatively small velocity, the universe’s general expansion is only part of the story: the motions of nearby galaxies within their surroundings can also affect the number. So that figure is useful context, not a simple, precise distance ruler. Still, even without assigning the galaxy a distance from that velocity alone, its light is a reminder that the small smudge in an eyepiece is an immense system of stars, gas, and dust—not a cloud inside our own Milky Way.

For an observing session, begin with a finder chart or planetarium app set to NGC 3877; the familiar name may be easier to search than its MCG catalog designation. The coordinates place it in the northern part of Ursa Major, a constellation well positioned for evening observing in spring. Under a dark, transparent sky, try a telescope of about 8 inches or larger, starting with low-to-medium magnification. Give your eyes time to adapt to the dark, keep stray lights out of view, and scan slowly. The galaxy may first appear as a faint streak rather than a sharply edged object. Higher magnification can sometimes help separate an extended target from the bright background, but if the image grows dim, return to a lower power.

Then pause. The galaxy’s long axis may become more apparent as your vision settles. You are seeing not a dramatic spiral photograph but its quiet, integrated glow: light gathered across a disk so large that its visible outline spans several arcminutes in our sky. MCG 8-22-2 asks for no special equipment beyond a little patience—and offers, in return, a small window onto another stellar island.
