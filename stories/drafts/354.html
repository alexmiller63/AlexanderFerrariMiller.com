Blue Racquetball: A Small Nebula with a Big Exit

NGC 6572 is compact enough to resemble a colored bead in a telescope—and it marks a star’s bright, brief transition toward its final form.

Somewhere in Ophiuchus, a dying star has let go of its outer layers. The expanding gas glows around the hot stellar remnant at its center, making NGC 6572 one of the sky’s compact planetary nebulae. Its nickname, Blue Racquetball, suits an object that can look less like a sprawling cloud than a tiny, vivid ball of light.

The name “planetary nebula” is a historical misdirection: these objects are not planets. They are shells of gas cast off by stars nearing the end of their lives. Radiation from the exposed, extremely hot central star energizes the gas, causing it to shine. The shell will continue to expand and fade; the star left behind will eventually become a white dwarf. NGC 6572 offers a glimpse of that short-lived, luminous stage.

The catalog numbers help explain the racquetball comparison. The catalogue lists NGC 6572 at right ascension 18h 12m 06.3s and declination +06° 51′ 13″, with an infrared angular size of about 0.597 by 0.406 arcminutes—roughly 36 by 24 arcseconds. That is tiny beside the Moon, which spans about 30 arcminutes. Its listed visual magnitude is 10.8, though the nebula’s compactness means that brightness is concentrated into a small patch of sky.

To find it, look in Ophiuchus, the large constellation between Hercules and Scorpius. The coordinates above are a useful starting point for a planetarium app or star chart; use a current chart to star-hop from nearby bright stars rather than sweeping blindly. From mid-northern latitudes, Ophiuchus is well placed on summer evenings, and NGC 6572 climbs high enough to observe around its passage across the southern sky. The target is also accessible to observers farther south.

A small telescope can show that something is there under a dark sky, but the nebula’s small apparent size rewards magnification. Start at low power to locate the field, then try roughly 100× or more if the air is steady. At low power, it may resemble a slightly out-of-focus star. Increasing magnification can help reveal its tiny disk. A nebula filter, especially an O III filter, may improve contrast against the background; compare the view with and without it, since filters dim stars as well as the sky.

Expect color to be elusive. The “blue” in the nickname is a useful hint, not a promise of a saturated jewel in every eyepiece. Visual color depends on aperture, sky conditions, magnification and the observer’s eyes; photographs can collect light for much longer than a human observer can. Even without obvious color or detail, spotting this small disk is a satisfying catch: a distant star’s expelled atmosphere, briefly shining before it disperses into space.
