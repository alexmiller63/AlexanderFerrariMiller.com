M28: A Small Glow with a Stellar Crowd

Find this globular cluster beside Sagittarius’s teapot and look for a compact haze that gathers hundreds of thousands of years of cosmic history into one patch of sky.

On a clear summer night, Sagittarius seems to pour its way across the southern sky. The constellation’s familiar teapot is an easy starting point, but the treasure nearby is subtler: M28, a globular cluster tucked close to the star Lambda Sagittarii, at the top of the teapot’s spout.

M28 does not announce itself with the bright, broad glow of its better-known neighbor M22. Instead, it waits about a degree northwest of Lambda Sagittarii, a soft spot that can be mistaken for a blur in the sky. That faintness is part of the pleasure. Once you find it, you are looking at a densely packed community of stars, held together by gravity in a roughly spherical swarm.

The catalogue calls M28 a globular cluster and lists its apparent diameter as about 11.2 arcminutes—roughly a third of the Moon’s apparent width. Its listed coordinates are right ascension 18 hours 24 minutes and declination minus 24 degrees 52 minutes, placing it in the southern part of Sagittarius. These measurements help explain why M28 is a rewarding target but not always an obvious one: it is compact, and from many northern locations it stays low above the horizon.

Globular clusters are among the Milky Way’s most enduring stellar gatherings. Their stars crowd into a gravitationally bound ball, with the densest region concentrated toward the center. In a telescope, that arrangement creates a pleasing contrast: a brighter central glow surrounded by a more delicate fringe. Even when the stars cannot be separated individually, the cluster’s concentrated shape gives away its nature. The light is not a single star, but the combined shimmer of many.

For a first search, let your eyes adjust to darkness and use a star chart or planetarium app to identify Lambda Sagittarii. Sweep slowly about a degree northwest of it with binoculars. Under a dark sky, M28 may appear as a small, dim smudge; steady binoculars and averted vision—looking slightly to the side of the object—can help. A small telescope should show a more definite round haze. With a larger telescope and patient viewing, stars around the outer regions may begin to resolve, while the core remains crowded.

Timing matters. M28 is best placed for evening observing during the Northern Hemisphere summer, when Sagittarius climbs highest. From northern latitudes it remains low, so choose a site with an unobstructed southern horizon. Wait until the cluster is well above rooftops and trees, and avoid nights when haze or bright moonlight washes out faint objects. A chair, a slow sweep, and a few minutes of unhurried looking can make the difference between passing over a blur and recognizing a stellar city.

M28 rewards that second look. Its faintness is not an absence but a gathering: a compact glow, made by stars too numerous to count one by one, shining together across the dark.
