Mirach: The Ember on Andromeda’s Road

*An autumn guide to a bright red giant whose quiet light marks a route across the northern sky.*

On a clear autumn evening, **Mirach** glows like a small ember in the constellation Andromeda. Its light has crossed roughly 197 light-years to reach us: a long journey from a star that, to the unaided eye, looks steady and close at hand.

Mirach is the traditional name of Beta Andromedae, also catalogued as HIP 5447. SIMBAD classifies it as a red giant of spectral type M0 IIIa and lists its visual magnitude as about 2.05—bright enough to pick out without binoculars under ordinary dark skies. Its measured parallax, 16.52 milliarcseconds, gives a distance of about 197 light-years. That distance is calculated from the parallax, rather than measured as a direct journey through space. [SIMBAD: Mirach / HIP 5447](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=HIP%205447)

A red giant is a star in a later stage of its life than the Sun. Its cooler outer surface gives it a warmer hue, though the color can be subtle to the eye and affected by sky conditions. Mirach’s classification tells us about its kind, not its individual future: we should not mistake a broad stellar category for a precise forecast of what this one star will do next.

There is also a small, patient motion beneath Mirach’s apparent stillness. SIMBAD reports a proper motion of about 176 milliarcseconds per year in right ascension and −112 in declination. Those figures describe its changing position against the distant background stars. The shift is far too slight to watch during an evening outdoors; it emerges through careful measurements separated by years.

**Try this:** Go out on an autumn evening in the Northern Hemisphere, after the sky is properly dark. Find the Great Square of Pegasus, then follow the bright chain of stars leading away from it into Andromeda. Mirach is one of the chain’s conspicuous markers. If the constellation is unfamiliar, a star chart or planetarium app can help you identify it; give your eyes several minutes to adjust before relying on fainter stars.

First look with your unaided eyes. Then turn binoculars toward Mirach—not to magnify the star into a visible disk, but to gather more of its surrounding star field. Compare its tint with a nearby white-looking star. The contrast may be delicate; atmospheric haze and personal color perception can change what you see. Keep the binoculars steady, and take a moment to notice how a star that seemed solitary belongs to a wider pattern.

Mirach’s place in that pattern is useful as well as beautiful. It gives the eye a foothold in Andromeda, a constellation best learned one bright star at a time. On another night, return to the same spot and see whether the chain feels more familiar. The stars will appear nearly unchanged—not because the sky is frozen, but because even a star’s measurable drift is small on a human timescale.

For tonight, let Mirach be both destination and starting point: a warm point of light, an evolved giant, and a marker on a road through the stars. Its glow does not announce its age or distance to the naked eye. But knowing those things can deepen the view: the ember overhead is not near, not still, and not merely a pin in a pattern.
