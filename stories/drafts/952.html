Chara, the Quiet Beacon of the Hunting Dogs

An ordinary-looking point of spring starlight, Chara is close enough to study—and a reminder that a bright star need not be a showy one.

On a clear spring evening, the handle of the Big Dipper points toward a patch of sky occupied by Canes Venatici, the Hunting Dogs. There, far less conspicuous than the Dipper’s familiar stars, shines Chara. You won’t see a dramatic color or a dazzling flare. Its pleasure is subtler: this modest point of light is a nearby star, shining steadily from a neighborhood of our own galaxy.

Chara is also cataloged as Beta Canum Venaticorum, or β CVn. The catalogue identifies it as a G0 V star—the “V” indicating a main-sequence star—and gives its apparent visual magnitude as 4.25. That makes it a naked-eye star under reasonably dark skies, though it may be difficult to pick out from a bright city or beneath haze. Its spectral class places it in the broad family of stars that includes the Sun; it is not a twin, but it gives observers a familiar point of comparison.

The measured parallax listed by the catalogue is about 118 milliarcseconds. Parallax is the tiny apparent shift in a star’s position as Earth travels around the Sun. Using that value gives a distance of roughly 8.5 parsecs, or 28 light-years. In cosmic terms, Chara is a near neighbor. The database also lists a substantial proper motion: its position slowly changes against the more distant background stars as it moves through space. That drift is far too small to notice during a night’s observing, but it matters in long-term measurements and in precise star charts.

There is a small caveat in the catalog description: the catalogue classifies Chara as a double or multiple star. That label is useful scientific context, but it does not mean an observer should expect to split it into two points through ordinary binoculars. The database entry alone does not establish what equipment would resolve any companion. For a casual skywatcher, Chara is best approached as one clear, steady star—and as a target whose interest comes from its measured properties rather than a striking telescopic appearance.

How to observe: Try in spring, when Canes Venatici is well placed in the evening sky for many Northern Hemisphere observers. Begin with the Big Dipper’s handle and consult a paper chart or planetarium app to identify the constellation. For a precise search, enter Beta CVn, β CVn or HIP 61317; the catalogue gives its J2000 coordinates as right ascension 12h 33m 44.5s and declination +41° 21′ 27″. Under a dark sky, look first with the naked eye. Binoculars can help if light pollution or a bright Moon makes the field harder to navigate, but they are not required to appreciate the star.

Take a moment to let your eyes adjust. Chara won’t compete with the best-known stars of the Dipper, and that is part of its charm. It is a quiet marker in a quieter constellation: close enough for astronomers to measure its distance and motion, yet still a small, steady light against the immense background of the night.
