Beta Com: A Quiet Star on the Move

*An ordinary-looking point in Coma Berenices offers a useful reminder: the stars that seem fixed are travelling through space.*

In the spring sky, Beta Com is easy to overlook. It is not a brilliant beacon but a modest, fourth-magnitude star in Coma Berenices, the faint constellation between Leo and Boötes. Yet its catalogue numbers tell a story of motion: the star shifts against the background of the sky by more than an arcsecond each year.

Beta Com is about 30 light-years away, close enough that astronomers can measure its distance through parallax: the apparent shift in a nearby star’s position as Earth moves around the Sun. SIMBAD lists a parallax of 108.725 milliarcseconds, corresponding to roughly 9.2 parsecs, or 30 light-years. The same catalogue gives its visual magnitude as 4.25 and classifies it as F9.5V—a main-sequence star, in the broad family of stars that includes the Sun. These figures make Beta Com a familiar kind of nearby stellar neighbor, rather than an exotic object. [SIMBAD: Beta Com](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=HIP%2064394)

Its subtler distinction is proper motion: the apparent movement of a star across our line of sight. SIMBAD records Beta Com’s motion as about 801 milliarcseconds per year in right ascension and 882 in declination. Combined, that is close to 1.2 arcseconds annually. The change is far too small to notice from one night to the next, but accumulated across decades it amounts to roughly an arcminute—about the apparent width of a penny held at arm’s length. The star is also moving along our line of sight: its listed radial velocity is about 5.3 kilometres per second away from the Sun. Proper motion and radial velocity describe different parts of its journey, one sideways across the sky and the other toward or away from us.

**When and where to look:** For northern observers, Coma Berenices is a spring-evening constellation. Look between Leo and Boötes, then use a current star chart or planetarium app to identify Beta Com by its position. SIMBAD gives its J2000 coordinates as right ascension 13 hours 11 minutes 52 seconds and declination +27 degrees 52 minutes. These coordinates are useful for setting a telescope or confirming a chart position; they are not a substitute for a local sky map, since the constellation’s orientation changes through the night and across the seasons.

At magnitude 4.25, Beta Com is generally a naked-eye target under a reasonably dark sky, though light pollution or haze may make binoculars helpful. Give your eyes time to adapt, and sweep the area slowly rather than expecting the star to announce itself. In a small telescope, it will still look like a point of light: its significance lies not in a dramatic telescopic appearance, but in the precision measurements that reveal its distance and motion.

A practical way to observe Beta Com is to note its position relative to nearby stars, then return to the same field on another clear night. You will not see it travel in real time. The pleasure is in knowing that the apparently fixed point has a measurable course—and that patient instruments have turned that minute drift into a portrait of a nearby star.
