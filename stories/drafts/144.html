NGC 559: A small gathering in Cassiopeia

*This open cluster sits high in the northern sky, where a modest telescope can turn a faint patch into a field of individual stars.*

On an autumn evening, Cassiopeia’s familiar zigzag stands above the northern horizon. Away from city glare, sweep a telescope through its star fields and you may find NGC 559: a compact open cluster, a loose family of stars that appears close together because its members occupy the same small patch of sky.

The cluster lies at right ascension 1 hour 29 minutes 32.9 seconds and declination +63 degrees 18 minutes, in Cassiopeia. Its listed optical size is about 9.7 arcminutes across—roughly a third of the apparent width of the full Moon. That modest span makes it a good target for careful, unhurried viewing rather than a showpiece that overwhelms the eyepiece. [SIMBAD identifies NGC 559 as an open cluster and lists its coordinates and angular size.](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%20559)

Open clusters are useful to astronomers because their stars are thought to have formed together from the same cloud of gas and dust. Comparing the stars in a cluster can help researchers investigate stellar ages and evolution. SIMBAD lists a parallax of 0.325 milliarcseconds for NGC 559. A simple inversion gives a distance of about 3,100 parsecs, or roughly 10,000 light-years. That estimate is a guide, not a complete distance analysis: parallax measurements and their uncertainties need careful interpretation, especially when translating a tiny angle into a large distance.

For observers, the most practical starting point is the cluster’s position. Use a planetarium app or a printed finder chart to locate the coordinates, then move from the bright pattern of Cassiopeia toward the target. NGC 559’s declination places it high in the northern sky for observers at mid-northern latitudes; from many such locations, it never sets. Choose a time when it is well above the horizon, and give your eyes time to adjust to the dark.

A finder scope or binoculars can help orient you, but a telescope at low power is a sensible first view. The cluster’s small angular size means it may look like a hazy knot before individual stars come into focus. Try averted vision—looking slightly to one side of the object—to make faint points easier to detect. Then increase magnification gradually. If the sky is bright or hazy, the cluster may appear subdued; a darker site and steady patience can make the difference.

The pleasure of NGC 559 is partly in its scale. The Moon’s broad disk is a familiar measure, yet this cluster occupies only a fraction of that apparent width. Its stars are gathered across a span that looks small from Earth, while the cluster itself lies thousands of light-years away
