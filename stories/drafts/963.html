Alpha Com: One Point of Light, Two Stars

*In Coma Berenices, a modest springtime star rewards a closer look—and offers a lesson in what a catalogue can tell us, and what it cannot.*

Alpha Com is easy to overlook. At magnitude 4.32 in visible light, it is not among the sky’s showiest beacons; to the unaided eye it is a single point in the constellation Coma Berenices. Yet the SIMBAD astronomical database classifies it as a double or multiple star. That quiet mismatch—one point to the eye, more than one star in the catalogue—is the start of its story. ([SIMBAD: HIP 64241](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=HIP%2064241))

The database places Alpha Com at right ascension 13 hours 09 minutes 59 seconds and declination +17° 31′ 46″ (J2000). Its listed parallax is 56.10 milliarcseconds. Parallax is the apparent shift of a nearby star as Earth moves around the Sun; converting that value gives a distance of roughly 58 light-years. SIMBAD also lists a combined spectral classification of F5V+F6V: two main-sequence stars of the F type. The “plus” is a compact hint that the light associated with Alpha Com comes from a pair, rather than a solitary sun. These are catalogue measurements and classifications, not details visible in a casual glance.

For an observer, the first task is simply to find the field. Coma Berenices is a northern-sky constellation, well placed on spring evenings. Use a current star chart or planetarium app to locate Alpha Com by its constellation and position; the coordinates are useful if you are searching with a telescope or setting circles. Under a dark sky, a magnitude-4 star may be visible without optical aid. In a bright suburban sky, binoculars can help pick it out from the surrounding stars.

The second task is to decide what you hope to see. Binoculars should show Alpha Com as a point of light, not as two neatly separated stars. A catalogue’s “double or multiple” label does not, by itself, say how far apart the components appear at a given time. That matters because a telescope’s ability to split a pair depends on their apparent separation, as well as aperture, focus, atmospheric steadiness and the observer’s experience. If resolving the stars is your goal, check a current double-star observing reference before planning the attempt; do not assume that a small telescope will separate them on any given night.

Even if the pair remains merged, Alpha Com is a useful observing exercise. Look first with the naked eye, then with binoculars, and finally through a telescope at low power. Notice how the view changes while the star itself remains in the same place in the field. The instruments do not merely magnify: they gather more light and reveal finer detail, while the atmosphere can blur that detail again.

There is a small pleasure in that progression. The sky offers Alpha Com as one point; measurement and classification invite us to think of it as a system. A careful observer can meet both versions—first the modest star among the spring constellations, then the more complicated object that catalogues describe. The distance, spectral types and double-star designation deepen the view, even when the eyepiece does not visibly divide the light.
