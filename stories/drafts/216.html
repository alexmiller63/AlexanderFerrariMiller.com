NGC 3079: Reading a Galaxy in the Catalog

*Its catalog entry offers a precise position, a narrow outline and a Seyfert 2 classification—but each number describes a different part of the story.*

NGC 3079 does not need a dramatic description to be intriguing. Its entry in the catalogue identifies it as a Seyfert 2 galaxy and records a sky position, an apparent shape and a redshift. Taken together, those details make a compact portrait of the object—and a reminder of how much a catalog can say without telling us everything.

The listed J2000 coordinates are right ascension 10h 01m 57.879s and declination +55° 40′ 47.45″. These numbers locate the galaxy on the sky; they are not a measure of its size or distance. The catalogue also gives an optical angular size of about 10.79 by 2.72 arcminutes, with a position angle of 169 degrees. In other words, as measured in that catalog, the galaxy’s apparent outline is much longer than it is wide. The dimensions describe how large it looks from Earth, not its physical diameter.

That distinction matters. A long, narrow shape on the sky may invite the eye to imagine a thin object, but angular dimensions alone cannot establish the galaxy’s true size. The catalog provides a morphological classification of “S,” indicating a spiral type, alongside the measured outline. These are related ways of describing the galaxy, but they are not interchangeable: one is a classification, the other an apparent angular measurement.

The entry lists an emission redshift of 0.00350451, with an uncertainty of 0.0005. Redshift is a measurement of how light from an object is shifted; it is not itself a distance. Turning it into a distance would require additional assumptions and information not supplied by this single catalog entry. The catalogue also lists a radial velocity of 1,049 kilometers per second, with an uncertainty of 150 kilometers per second. These values belong to the same general picture of motion, but neither should be mistaken for a direct measurement of the galaxy’s physical scale.

The label “Seyfert 2” adds another kind of information. It identifies the galaxy’s active-galaxy classification, not an appearance that the catalog promises an observer will see. The catalogue’s entry gives the category, but does not, by itself, describe the detailed processes in the galaxy’s center or predict how conspicuous that activity would be in a telescope. The classification is a scientific clue, not a visual description.

Brightness figures, too, depend on how they are measured. The catalogue lists a visual V magnitude of 10.86, as well as values in other bands: for example, B magnitude 11.54 and near-infrared J, H and K magnitudes of 8.439, 7.636 and 7.262. The numbers should not be read as competing estimates of one universal brightness. Each belongs to a different filter or wavelength range, and the catalog records them separately.

NGC 3079’s entry is therefore less a single portrait than a set of carefully labeled views. Coordinates say where to point; angular dimensions describe an apparent outline; morphology and Seyfert classification place the galaxy in cataloged categories; and redshift and velocity report measurements of motion.
