Alderamin, a steady beacon with a subtle drift

*The brightest star in Cepheus is easy to spot—but precise measurements reveal that it is moving through the sky.*

On an autumn evening, look north for the familiar zigzag of Cassiopeia. Nearby, Cepheus forms a house-shaped pattern, and its brightest star, Alderamin, marks a corner of the roof. It shines at about magnitude 2.46: bright enough to see without binoculars under a reasonably dark sky, though city lights can make the fainter stars around it harder to pick out.

Alderamin is not a star fixed in place. Like the Sun, it travels through the Milky Way, and its motion has a small effect on its position against the distant background. The catalogue lists its proper motion as about 151 milliarcseconds per year eastward and 49 milliarcseconds per year northward—together, roughly 0.16 arcsecond annually. That change is far too slight to notice from one night to the next. Over many generations, however, the pattern of stars shifts.

The star’s distance can be estimated from its parallax, the apparent wobble in its position as Earth circles the Sun. The catalogue gives a parallax of 66.50 milliarcseconds. Taking the reciprocal of that value in arcseconds gives a distance of about 15 parsecs, or 49 light-years. In stellar terms, Alderamin is a near neighbor.

Its catalogue classification, A8Vn, offers another clue to its nature. The “A” identifies a hot, blue-white spectral type; “V” indicates a main-sequence star, in the long-lived stage of fusing hydrogen in its core. The “n” denotes broadened spectral lines. Alderamin’s catalogue entry also records a radial velocity of about 16 kilometers per second toward us. That motion is along our line of sight, distinct from the sideways motion measured across the sky.

For a useful observing session, start with Cassiopeia and consult a sky chart or astronomy app to locate Cepheus beside it. Once you have the house shape, Alderamin is the bright star that anchors the pattern. Its coordinates—right ascension 21 hours 18 minutes, declination +62 degrees 35 minutes—place it high in the northern sky. From locations north of roughly 27 degrees latitude, it never sets; farther south, it still rises, though it spends part of each day below the horizon. Autumn evenings are especially convenient for many observers in the Northern Hemisphere.

No telescope is needed to enjoy Alderamin. Binoculars can help separate its pattern from a bright background, but the star itself is best appreciated with the naked eye: a clean point of light whose measured drift reminds us that the sky’s familiar figures are not frozen. The constellations are signposts, not cages. Alderamin keeps its place in Cepheus for now, while slowly writing a new position into the future.
