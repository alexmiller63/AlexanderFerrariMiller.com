Phact, the Dove’s Bright Southern Beacon

*An easy-to-spot second-magnitude star, Phact offers a glimpse of a hot, rapidly rotating star and its surrounding gas.*

On a clear winter evening, look below Orion for the small constellation Lepus, the Hare. Continue farther south and you’ll reach Columba, the Dove. Its brightest star is Phact, a blue-white point that can be seen without binoculars from a dark or even moderately lit site. Though it may look like a quiet pinprick, Phact is a hot B-type star whose classification carries a clue to activity around it.

SIMBAD identifies Phact as **Alpha Columbae**, with spectral type B9Ve and a visual magnitude of about 2.65. Its measured parallax—11.4 milliarcseconds—places it roughly 88 parsecs, or 286 light-years, away. These are catalogue measurements, not estimates from its apparent brightness alone. (SIMBAD: https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=HIP%2026634)

The “e” in B9Ve signals emission lines in the star’s spectrum. Phact belongs to the broad class of Be stars: hot stars whose spectra show evidence of gas around them. In the usual picture, a rapidly rotating B star is surrounded by a disk of material. The disk can add emission to particular spectral lines, giving astronomers a way to study gas that is otherwise far too small and distant to see directly. The classification tells us Phact is a Be star; it does not, by itself, describe the disk’s present condition or how it may change.

That is the satisfying scale shift of stargazing. The naked eye sees one steady point. A spectrograph can separate its light into colors and reveal features that hint at moving, glowing gas. The star’s apparent brightness—magnitude 2.65 in SIMBAD’s listed photometry—makes it a practical naked-eye target, while its distance reminds us that even a bright star can be remote.

For observers in the Northern Hemisphere, Phact is best placed in the evening sky around January and February. Find Orion first, then look below it for Lepus; Columba lies farther south. Phact marks the dove’s brighter northern region. At mid-northern latitudes it stays low above the southern horizon, so choose a site with an unobstructed view in that direction. From farther south, it climbs higher and is easier to observe. The constellation is also visible from much of the Southern Hemisphere.

Give your eyes several minutes to adapt to the dark, then try spotting Phact unaided before raising binoculars. Binoculars will show the surrounding star field, but they cannot resolve the star’s gas disk—that takes instruments capable of analyzing starlight. Still, knowing what the spectrum reveals can make the view more vivid: this small bright point is not just a marker in a constellation, but a hot star with a complicated envelope of gas.

Phact’s name may be less familiar than Orion’s, yet it rewards a short detour from the better-known winter patterns. Find the Hare, continue south to the Dove, and let one bright star connect a simple observing session with the physics written in starlight.
