Arkab Posterior: A Quiet Beacon at the Archer’s Heel

*This fourth-magnitude star is a modest naked-eye target—and a reminder that even a familiar point of light is moving through space.*

Not every star in Sagittarius announces itself with the brilliance of a landmark. Arkab Posterior, catalogued as β² Sagittarii, is a subtler prize: a steady point of light near the constellation’s southern reaches, bright enough for unaided eyes under a dark sky but easily lost in a city’s glare. Its name invites a pause. “Posterior” distinguishes it from its neighboring companion in the name pair, Arkab Prior; the star’s catalog identity is HIP 95294. [SIMBAD](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=HIP%2095294) lists its visual magnitude as 4.27.

That brightness places Arkab Posterior within the usual reach of the naked eye, though visibility depends on sky conditions, eyesight and how high the star stands above the horizon. In a dark location, give your eyes time to adjust and scan Sagittarius rather than expecting this star to leap out. Binoculars can help pick it from the surrounding star field; they will not turn it into a disk. At this distance, even a telescope shows a point of light, not a surface.

The star’s position is a useful guide for a chart or planetarium app: right ascension 19 hours 23 minutes 13 seconds, declination −44 degrees 48 minutes, in coordinates for epoch J2000. Sagittarius is best placed in the evening sky during the Northern Hemisphere’s summer, though how high the constellation climbs depends strongly on the observer’s latitude. From far-northern locations it stays low; farther south, it stands higher. Seek a clear southern horizon, and use a current sky chart to identify the Archer before searching for this fainter target.

There is more to Arkab Posterior than its appearance suggests. SIMBAD gives a parallax of 23.3612 milliarcseconds. Parallax is the tiny apparent shift of a nearby star against more distant background stars as Earth travels around the Sun. Turning that measured angle into a distance puts Arkab Posterior about 43 parsecs away—roughly 140 light-years. That conversion is an estimate, not a direct measurement in light-years.

The same database lists the star’s spectral type as F2/3 V. The “V” indicates a main-sequence star, one that generates energy by fusing hydrogen in its core. Its listed proper motion—about 93 milliarcseconds per year eastward in right ascension and 54 milliarcseconds per year southward in declination—records its slow drift across our sky. The motion is far too small to notice while observing, but it accumulates in precision measurements across years. SIMBAD also reports a radial velocity of 13.66 kilometers per second: the measured component of its motion toward or away from us.

For an observing session, start with the broad pattern of Sagittarius, then confirm Arkab Posterior’s position against a chart rather than relying on brightness alone. Once found, take a moment to notice the contrast: to the eye, a nearly still pinprick; to astronomical instruments, a nearby star with a measured distance, classification and motion. That is the quiet pleasure of the Almanack’s less conspicuous targets. They reward patience—and make the sky feel less like a picture than a place.
