HIP 39953: A Wolf-Rayet Star in Vela’s Sails

*Look south for a bright point of light with a tempestuous double-star story: one component is a rare, wind-blasted Wolf-Rayet star.*

On a clear night beneath Vela, the Sails, HIP 39953 is easy to mistake for a steady beacon. It shines at about magnitude 1.8 in visible light—bright enough for the unaided eye. But this designation belongs to γ² Velorum, a star system whose apparent calm hides two very different stars locked together. The SIMBAD astronomical database identifies it as a Wolf-Rayet star and gives its spectral type as WC8+O7.5III-V. [SIMBAD: HIP 39953](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=HIP%2039953)

The letters in that classification sketch a dramatic partnership. “WC8” marks the Wolf-Rayet component as a carbon-sequence star; the accompanying O-type star is classified O7.5III-V. Wolf-Rayet stars are massive stars with powerful winds that carry material away from their surfaces. In the carbon-sequence group, the exposed stellar material is rich in carbon. Such stars offer a glimpse of stellar evolution in an extreme phase: the outer layers have been stripped back, revealing products of the star’s internal life. Beside it, the hot O-type companion adds its own intense light and radiation.

The catalog’s measured parallax is 2.92 milliarcseconds, with an uncertainty of 0.30 milliarcseconds. A straightforward conversion gives a rough distance of about 340 parsecs, or roughly 1,100 light-years. That estimate is approximate: parallax uncertainties matter, and the catalog measurement is not a direct tape measure. Still, it places this bright southern landmark well beyond the familiar stars of our local neighborhood. SIMBAD lists its position as right ascension 8 hours 9 minutes 31.95 seconds and declination −47°20′11.7″ (J2000). [SIMBAD: HIP 39953](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=HIP%2039953)

For observers, the practical challenge is not seeing the star but finding the right patch of sky. γ² Velorum lies in Vela, a constellation best placed for observers in the Southern Hemisphere. From more northerly locations it stays low above the southern horizon, where haze and buildings can dim even a bright star. Use a sky chart or planetarium app to locate Vela, and look for γ² Velorum under that name as well as HIP 39953. A clear, unobstructed southern view will help.

Binoculars can show the star against its surrounding field, while a small telescope offers a closer look at that neighborhood. Don’t expect an ordinary backyard instrument to separate the two stars responsible for the WC8+O7.5III-V classification: the catalog describes a stellar system, not a promise that its components will appear as two distinct points through an eyepiece. The distinction comes from astronomical observations and analysis.

That is part of the appeal of HIP 39953. To the eye, it is simply a bright pinprick in the sails of an imagined ship. The science adds another layer: a rare, carbon-rich Wolf-Rayet star sharing its system with a hot O-type companion, both at a distance measured through the tiny apparent shift of parallax. On your next clear night under Vela, take a moment with the bright point. It is a beacon—and a stellar story in progress.
