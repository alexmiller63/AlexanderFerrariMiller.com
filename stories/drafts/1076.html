Beta Ara: A Southern Beacon in the Altar

Bright enough for the unaided eye, this distant giant marks a rewarding stop in the southern sky.

Beta Ara sits in Ara, the Altar, at a far-southern declination of about 55.5 degrees below the celestial equator. In a dark sky it is an easy naked-eye star: the catalogue lists its visual magnitude as 2.85. Its catalogue position is right ascension 17 hours 25 minutes and declination −55 degrees 31 minutes. Those coordinates put it well south of the familiar northern constellations—and make it a natural target for observers in the Southern Hemisphere.

Ara is sometimes imagined as a small ceremonial altar, but in the sky it is a pattern of stars rather than a sharply drawn object. Beta Ara is one of its bright markers: a point of light to seek out after identifying the surrounding shape on a star chart. Its brightness does not mean it is close. The catalogue reports a parallax of 4.5601 milliarcseconds. Parallax is the tiny apparent shift in a star’s position as Earth travels around the Sun; taking the simple inverse of that value gives a rough distance of about 715 light-years. That estimate is a useful scale, not a precision measurement.

The database classifies Beta Ara as a red supergiant and gives it the spectral type K0/1Ib. Spectral types describe a star’s light and temperature; the “Ib” luminosity class denotes a luminous supergiant. The labels invite a little care: astronomical catalogues may preserve different kinds of classification, and a star’s formal spectral notation is not a promise of a particular color to the unaided eye. To an observer, Beta Ara may look white, warm, or slightly orange, depending on eyesight, altitude, and atmospheric conditions.

What “supergiant” does suggest is scale and stellar evolution. Such stars are highly luminous and evolved, unlike the Sun, which is still in its long, comparatively stable middle age. Beta Ara’s apparent brightness is the combined result of its intrinsic luminosity and its distance; the parallax helps astronomers estimate the latter, while the spectrum offers clues to the star itself. One point of light thus carries two different stories: where it is, and what kind of object shines there.

For a practical observing session, choose a clear, dark night and give your eyes time to adapt. A simple constellation chart or planetarium app can help you trace Ara and confirm Beta Ara’s position; binoculars are not essential, though they can make the surrounding star field more inviting. The star’s southern declination means it rises high for observers at southern mid-latitudes, while from much of the Northern Hemisphere it stays low or never clears the horizon. Around June, when right ascension 17 hours is well placed in the evening sky, observers south of the equator can look for Ara as the night progresses. Check your local sky chart for the best viewing time.

When you find Beta Ara, pause before moving on. It is a steady-looking point to the unaided eye, but its catalogue coordinates, parallax, and spectrum each reveal a different layer of its identity. The Altar’s bright marker rewards not only finding it, but wondering what the light has traveled to reach you.
