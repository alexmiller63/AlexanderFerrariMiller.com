NGC 4889: A Quiet Giant at the Heart of Coma

*In the spring sky, a telescope can reveal one galaxy’s soft glow—and hint at the immense black hole hidden within it.*

The first thing to know about **NGC 4889** is that it will not look like a spiral galaxy in a photograph. There are no sweeping arms to catch the eye, just a broad, softly fading oval of light. But look long enough, and that glow becomes a marker for a place of extremes: a giant elliptical galaxy in the Coma Cluster, hundreds of millions of light-years away.

NGC 4889 lies in Coma Berenices, near the cluster’s crowded center. Its measured redshift is 0.0215, according to the catalogue. The light now entering your telescope began its journey when Earth’s continents, climates and life were very different. The galaxy itself is classified as an E3 elliptical, a shape that is noticeably stretched rather than perfectly round.

The galaxy’s calm appearance conceals a remarkable central object. Astronomers studying the motions of stars in NGC 4889 estimated that its central black hole contains about 21 billion times the mass of the Sun. That number is an estimate, not a direct photograph of the black hole: its gravity is inferred from the way nearby stars move. The galaxy’s bright, extended body gives no obvious visual clue to the darkness at its center. (McConnell et al., *Nature*, 2011)

Finding NGC 4889 is easier if you think of it as part of a pair. Its neighboring giant, NGC 4874, also dominates the Coma Cluster’s core. They are close enough to share the same telescope field at low power, though neither is a showpiece like a bright nebula. The reward is subtler: two concentrated patches of light surrounded by a much larger, fainter realm of galaxies.

For a spring observing session, start with Coma Berenices when it is high in the evening sky. NGC 4889’s listed position is right ascension 13 hours, 00 minutes, 08 seconds and declination +27 degrees, 58 minutes (J2000). A chart or GoTo mount can help you land in the dense cluster field. Under a dark sky, an 8-inch telescope is a useful starting point; a larger aperture can make the galaxy’s diffuse outline easier to distinguish. Use low power to locate the region, then try moderate magnification and look slightly away from the galaxy’s center. Averted vision—looking just to one side of the target—can help reveal faint light.

The catalogue lists NGC 4889 at visual magnitude 11.3, but a galaxy spreads its light over an area, so that number does not mean it will appear like a pinpoint star. Let your eyes adapt to the dark, avoid bright screens, and give the view time. On a clear night, the galaxy may appear as little more than a dim oval. That is enough. You are looking at the combined light of countless stars, with a black hole’s immense gravity hidden in their midst.
