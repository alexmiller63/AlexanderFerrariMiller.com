Beta Vol: An Orange Beacon in Volans

*This southern-sky giant is easy to overlook in a dim constellation, but its brightness and measured motion give observers plenty to look for.*

In the faint southern constellation Volans, Beta Vol offers a useful reminder that a star’s apparent stillness is only an illusion. It shines at about magnitude 3.76—bright enough for the unaided eye under a reasonably dark sky—and lies roughly 108 light-years away. In telescope or binoculars, it is not a dramatic double-star showpiece; the appeal is the star itself, a nearby giant whose catalogue measurements reveal both its nature and its slow drift across the sky.

SIMBAD lists Beta Vol as a K2III star. The “K2” marks a star cooler than the Sun, with the warm, orange hue associated with K-type stars; “III” identifies it as a giant. A giant has expanded beyond the more compact stage of its life, though this classification alone does not tell us its exact age or future. Its apparent brightness is not proof that it is exceptionally close: Beta Vol’s measured parallax is about 30.2 milliarcseconds. Since a parallax of one arcsecond corresponds to one parsec, that measurement places it at about 33 parsecs, or 108 light-years.

The star also has a modest but measurable proper motion: SIMBAD gives components of about −35 and −152 milliarcseconds per year in right ascension and declination. Together, those amount to roughly 0.16 arcsecond of motion across the sky each year. That is far too small to notice during an evening’s observing, but over centuries the change adds up. The database’s designation of Beta Vol as a high-proper-motion star reflects this gradual celestial drift, not a sudden movement.

To find it, use the coordinates listed in SIMBAD: right ascension 8 hours 25 minutes 44 seconds, declination −66°08′. A finder chart for Volans will help, especially because the constellation’s stars are relatively faint. Beta Vol’s magnitude makes it a naked-eye target from a dark site, but binoculars can help pick it out if the sky is bright or you are unfamiliar with the pattern. A small telescope will show a sharper point of light; the star’s orange tint may be more apparent when viewed slightly away from its glare.

Beta Vol is best placed for evening observing in the southern summer, around January and February, though the precise hour depends on date and location. Its far-southern declination makes latitude especially important. At the equator it reaches only about 24° above the northern horizon; at latitude 35° south, it climbs to about 58° above the horizon. Observers north of roughly 24° north cannot see it rise at all. Wherever you observe, choose a clear southern horizon and give your eyes time to adapt to the dark.

There is satisfaction in observing a star that asks for no elaborate equipment: locate Volans, confirm the position on a chart, and let Beta Vol’s steady light be the destination. Its catalogue figures turn that small point into a measurable object—an evolved orange giant, more than a hundred light-years away, changing its place in the sky by a fraction of an arcsecond at a time.
