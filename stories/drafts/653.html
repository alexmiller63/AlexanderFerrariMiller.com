Alpha Hyi: A Southern Star on the Move

*An easy naked-eye target in Hydrus, this F-type subgiant offers a quiet lesson in how stars shift across the sky.*

In the southern constellation Hydrus, Alpha Hyi is bright enough to see without optical aid—and close enough to the celestial pole that it can be hard to reach from much of the Northern Hemisphere. Its light has crossed roughly 63 light-years to meet us. Yet the star’s more surprising story is written not in its brightness but in its motion: Alpha Hyi is a high-proper-motion star, changing position against the more distant background of the sky.

The SIMBAD astronomical database lists Alpha Hyi as spectral type F0IV, with a visual magnitude of 2.84. The “F0” describes its spectral class; “IV” is the luminosity class for a subgiant. In other words, it is not classified as an ordinary main-sequence star, though the catalog entry alone doesn’t tell us its precise age or future. Its apparent brightness makes it a rewarding target for a clear, dark-sky evening, while binoculars can help pick it out when the southern horizon is hazy or washed with light. [SIMBAD: Alpha Hyi (HIP 9236)](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=HIP%209236)

The distance is a calculation from the catalog’s measured parallax: about 51.6 milliarcseconds. Taking the inverse of that value gives a distance near 19 parsecs, or 63 light-years. Parallax is the apparent, tiny shift in a nearby star’s position as Earth moves around the Sun—the same geometric principle that lets astronomers gauge a star’s distance.

Alpha Hyi also has a substantial proper motion: SIMBAD gives components of about 290 and 18 milliarcseconds per year in the two sky directions. Combined, that is roughly 0.29 arcseconds each year. No observer will see it creep across the sky during a night; the change is too small. But over a century, its displacement adds up to nearly 30 arcseconds—about half the apparent width of the full Moon. “Fixed stars” are fixed only on human timescales.

For observers, the practical challenge is its southerly declination, listed as about 61°34′ south. At culmination, the star climbs highest due south. From locations north of roughly 28° north latitude, it remains below the horizon; farther south, it appears, but may skim low in the sky. A low target is viewed through more atmosphere, where haze and light pollution can dim and blur it. Choose a site with an open southern horizon, and wait for a transparent night.

Alpha Hyi’s right ascension is close to 2 hours. That makes late October through December a useful stretch for evening observing: during those months, the star is well placed in the southern sky after dark. Use a current star chart or planetarium app to locate Hydrus and confirm the star’s position; its coordinates are approximately 01h 58m 46s, −61° 34′. From the Southern Hemisphere, it is an unaided-eye object under ordinary clear conditions, but binoculars offer a steadier view and make the surrounding star field easier to explore.

The pleasure of Alpha Hyi is partly its accessibility and partly its scale. A point of light that seems still is moving through space, while Earth’s orbit gives us the geometry to measure its distance. Look south, let your eyes adjust, and give the star a moment. Its journey is slow to watch—but measurable.
