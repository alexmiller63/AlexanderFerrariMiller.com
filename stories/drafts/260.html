NGC 4372: A Southern Cluster to Seek in the Dark

This globular cluster spreads across a patch of sky nearly as wide as the full Moon. Its faint light rewards patient observing from southern skies.

Scientific context: NGC 4372 is cataloged as a globular cluster, a dense, roughly spherical gathering of stars held together by gravity. The catalogue lists its position as right ascension 12h 25m 45.43s, declination −72° 39′ 32.7″, and its cataloged angular size as 24 arcminutes.

On a dark autumn evening in the Southern Hemisphere, the Southern Cross and its neighboring constellations climb into view. Farther south, in Musca, lies NGC 4372: not a bright, compact jewel, but a broad and subtle glow. Its cataloged size—about 24 arcminutes across—approaches the apparent width of the Moon. Yet its listed visual magnitude is 9.85, so that light is spread thinly over a large area. The contrast between its apparent size and faintness is part of the challenge.

Globular clusters are among the Milky Way’s most striking stellar gatherings. Their stars crowd together in a gravitationally bound system, with the densest concentration toward the center. In a telescope, a cluster may look like a soft ball of light; with enough aperture and steady conditions, individual stars can begin to emerge. But the view depends on the instrument, the sky and the observer’s eyes. NGC 4372 is best approached without expecting a brilliant, sharply edged showpiece.

Begin with the sky itself. Give your eyes time to adapt to darkness, and choose a site well away from streetlights. Use a current star chart or planetarium app to locate Musca and then work from nearby, easily identified stars toward the cluster’s listed coordinates. NGC 4372 is at declination −72° 39′, making it a target for observers at southern latitudes; from far northern locations it stays below the horizon. It culminates around midnight during the Southern Hemisphere’s late-autumn observing season, though it can be observed at other times when it is high enough and the sky is dark.

Try binoculars first if you have them, but don’t be surprised if the cluster remains elusive. A telescope at low power can help you identify its broad glow against the surrounding star field. Once you have found it, increase magnification gradually: higher power can darken the background, but too much may spread the cluster’s faint light so thinly that it becomes harder to see. Averted vision—looking slightly to one side of the target—may help reveal a dim haze. If the view seems empty, pause and return to the field after your eyes have adjusted further.

The catalogue lists NGC 4372’s visual magnitude as 9.85 and its angular size as 24 arcminutes; these figures describe a cataloged object, not a promise of what any particular eyepiece will show. The pleasure is in the search: a careful star-hop, averted vision, and then—perhaps—a faint round smudge resolving from the dark. For a southern observer, that modest glow is a reminder that some of the sky’s most rewarding sights ask for patience before they give themselves away.
