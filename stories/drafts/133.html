NGC 188: A Cluster Near the Pole

*Its position and measured motion offer a way to explore how astronomers describe a star cluster—and distinguish catalog data from what a telescope shows.*

NGC 188 lies in a part of the sky that circles close to the north celestial pole. The catalogue classifies it as an open cluster and gives its J2000 coordinates as right ascension 00h 47m 11.5s and declination +85° 14′ 38″. That declination puts it about 4.8 degrees from the pole. For observers north of roughly 5 degrees latitude, an object at this position remains above the horizon as Earth turns.

The coordinates help locate NGC 188, but they do not describe its full appearance. The catalogue lists an optical angular size of 32.6 by 32.6 arcminutes. This is a catalog measurement of the cluster’s apparent extent on the sky—not a claim that the cluster has a sharp boundary or that every star in that patch belongs to it. What an observer sees depends on the stars and observing conditions; the listed size alone does not predict how prominent the cluster will look through a particular instrument.

The distance is not given directly in the catalogue entry, but the database lists a parallax of 0.5053 milliarcseconds. Taking the inverse of that value, expressed in arcseconds, gives an estimated distance of about 1,980 parsecs, or roughly 6,450 light-years. This is a calculation from the cataloged parallax, not a separate distance measurement quoted in the entry. The result shows how a small apparent shift in a star’s position across the year can provide a way to estimate distance.

The catalogue also lists a proper motion of −2.3087 milliarcseconds per year in right ascension (with the cosine-of-declination convention) and −0.9565 milliarcseconds per year in declination. Its listed heliocentric radial velocity is −42.03 kilometers per second. These measurements describe motion across the sky and along the line of sight. Astronomers can use distance and motion, among other evidence, to assess whether stars seen near one another are physically associated or merely appear close from Earth.

That distinction matters when interpreting a cluster. A star chart shows directions, and an image shows light arriving from those directions; neither alone establishes that every nearby-looking star is part of the same group. The catalogue reports 5,835 linked objects in NGC 188’s hierarchy, but its page cautions that this count includes linked objects regardless of membership probability. It should not be read as a confirmed census of cluster members.

For a practical search, use the coordinates with a star chart or planetarium app and look near Polaris, the North Star. NGC 188 is close to the pole in astronomical terms, but its position is still several degrees from Polaris. A finder or chart can help bridge that gap. The catalog values provide a guide to where the cluster lies and how it is measured; they do not guarantee a particular visual impression. NGC 188’s appeal is partly in that difference: a compact set of coordinates points to a broad patch of sky, while parallax and motion measurements help turn a pattern into an object astronomers can study.
