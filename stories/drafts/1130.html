Beta CrA: A Southern Star, Measured in Motions

At magnitude 4, Beta Coronae Australis is a modest naked-eye point. Its catalog details offer a closer look at what one star can—and cannot—tell us.

The first thing to know about Beta CrA is where to look: far south. The catalogue gives its J2000 position as right ascension 19h 10m and declination −39° 20′, placing it in Corona Australis, the Southern Crown. From a dark site, its listed visual magnitude of 4.095 puts it within naked-eye reach, though haze, moonlight and city glow can erase a star this faint.

The second thing is that a star’s name is not its story. “Beta” marks its place in a constellation’s traditional naming scheme; “CrA” abbreviates Corona Australis. In a modern catalog, the star is also HIP 94160. These labels point to one object, but each belongs to a different system for describing the sky.

The catalogue lists a parallax of 5.8645 milliarcseconds. Parallax is the apparent shift of a nearby star against more distant background stars as Earth moves around the Sun. Turning that angle upside down gives a distance of roughly 170 parsecs, or about 550 light-years. That is an estimate, not a tape-measure result: the catalog’s listed parallax uncertainty is about 0.41 milliarcseconds.

The same entry gives Beta CrA a proper motion of about 4.3 milliarcseconds per year eastward and 38.2 milliarcseconds per year southward. These figures describe its slow apparent drift across our sky, not a sudden change visible from one night to the next. Its listed radial velocity—the component of motion toward or away from us—is 2.7 kilometers per second. Together, the measurements remind us that stars are not fixed pins, even when a human lifetime makes their movement difficult to notice.

Its spectrum carries a caution of its own. The catalogue records a classification of K0II/IIICNIb, but marks the entry with quality grade D. Rather than forcing that compact label into a simple claim about the star’s precise stage of life, it is better to treat it as a cataloged description with limited certainty. The color and brightness tell a broader, more useful observing story: Beta CrA is a bright point in a southern constellation, but the data behind a point of light can be complicated.

To find it, start with a current sky chart or planetarium app and search for Corona Australis; use the star’s coordinates if your chart supports them. Give your eyes time to adjust, then look for the constellation from a dark location with a clear southern horizon. Binoculars can help pick out the surrounding star pattern, though the star itself may be visible without them. Your latitude matters: at 35° south, Beta CrA culminates nearly overhead; at 35° north, it rises only about 16° above the horizon at its highest. Farther north, buildings, trees and atmospheric murk can make the search especially challenging.

A small point of light can be both a practical observing target and a lesson in astronomical measurement. Beta CrA’s position helps us find it; its parallax estimates its distance; its motions reveal that it is traveling. On a clear night, the first task is simply to spot it. The rest begins with knowing what the numbers mean—and where their uncertainties remain.
