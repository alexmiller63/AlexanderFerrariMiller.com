Algol: The Star That Winks

*In Perseus, a familiar point of light offers a patient observer a glimpse of stellar motion.*

Algol looks like a steady star—until you watch it closely. High in the northern sky on autumn and winter evenings, this bright point in Perseus changes its light as one star passes in front of another. The effect is subtle to the casual glance, but over several nights the “winking” becomes a small, satisfying experiment in astronomy.

The name in the catalog is β Persei, or Algol. The catalogue identifies it as a spectroscopic binary and lists its spectral type as B8V. Its record gives a visual-band magnitude of 2.12, placing it among the brighter stars visible to the unaided eye under a dark sky. That number is a catalog measurement, not a promise that Algol will look exactly the same every time you check: its changing brightness is the point of the show.

An eclipsing binary is a pair of stars orbiting one another in a plane that, from Earth, lets one cross in front of the other. We do not see the stars’ surfaces in detail. Instead, we detect the changing sum of their light: when one star blocks part of its companion’s light, the system appears dimmer. Algol’s dip is temporary, and the system brightens again as the stars continue along their orbits. The star itself has not switched off; our viewpoint is what makes the eclipse visible.

For a simple observing project, begin by finding Perseus with a printed star chart or a planetarium app. Algol lies among the constellation’s recognizable stars; a chart makes the identification easier, especially if you are observing from a light-polluted location. Give your eyes time to adjust to the dark, then compare Algol’s brightness with nearby stars that appear steady. You do not need a telescope. In fact, the unaided eye is useful here because you can compare the whole pattern of stars at once.

Make a brief note each time: date, time, sky conditions, and whether Algol seems brighter or fainter than your comparison stars. Return on later evenings and look for a repeating change. A single look can’t tell you much—haze, moonlight, or a nearby streetlamp can fool the eye—but a run of observations can reveal the rhythm. Binoculars may help you pick out the surrounding star pattern, though they are not essential.

The best almanac entry is not a perfect measurement. It is the moment when a point of light stops seeming fixed and starts seeming alive: a pair of distant suns, moving through an orbit, briefly changing what reaches your eye. Algol turns a patch of sky into a lesson in geometry, patience, and the difference between a star changing and a star merely appearing to.
