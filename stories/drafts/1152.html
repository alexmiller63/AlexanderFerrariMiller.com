Dabih: A Binary Written in Starlight

*In Capricornus, one modest point of light carries a hidden companion—and a clue to how astronomers read the sky.*

Dabih is easy to mistake for a quiet star. Look south on a clear late-summer or autumn evening, find the small, faint outline of Capricornus, and you’ll find it among the constellation’s brightest points. To the eye, it is a single light. In the catalog, however, the object identified as β¹ Cap is marked as a spectroscopic binary: a system whose paired stars reveal themselves through their changing spectra.

That distinction is a reminder that the night sky has more than one scale. A star can appear as one point because its companions are too close together, or because their light blends at the eye or telescope. In a spectroscopic binary, astronomers detect the system through the light itself. As the stars orbit, their motion shifts the patterns of dark and bright lines in the spectrum. Those shifts can disclose a companion even when the stars cannot be seen separately.

SIMBAD lists β¹ Cap’s spectral type as G9II:+B8p:Si:—a classification that suggests a complicated, unusual stellar system rather than a simple sunlike star. Its cataloged visual magnitude is 3.08, making it a naked-eye object under reasonably dark skies. The same record gives a parallax of 8.3966 milliarcseconds. Inverting that measurement puts the system at roughly 119 parsecs, or about 390 light-years away. Those figures are measurements, not a portrait: from Earth, the system still arrives as one small pinprick. ([SIMBAD: HIP 100345](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=HIP%20100345))

There is a pleasing mismatch between the star’s apparent simplicity and the evidence needed to understand it. The unaided eye gathers all the light into one point; a spectrograph spreads that light into a narrow band, where the stars’ motion leaves its signature. The instrument does not merely magnify the view. It changes what counts as a view.

**How to find it:** Capricornus lies south of Aquila and east of Sagittarius, low compared with the more familiar northern constellations. Dabih’s coordinates are about right ascension 20 hours 21 minutes and declination −14°47′. Use those coordinates in a planetarium app or star chart, then look for the constellation on a late-summer or autumn evening. Around September, the region is well placed for evening viewing; from northern locations, Dabih may sit low in the southern sky, so choose a site with an open southern horizon. Its magnitude makes it a practical naked-eye target away from bright city lights, though binoculars can help pick it out among the surrounding stars.

A telescope is not required to appreciate the puzzle—and a sharper view may not split this particular pair into two points. “Spectroscopic binary” describes how the companionship is detected, not a guarantee that two stars will appear side by side in an eyepiece. Instead, let Dabih remain a single bright point. The invisible motion is the story: two stars orbiting, their light carrying a rhythm across the distance.
