Alpheratz — the Andromeda corner of the Great Square

Find the star anchoring the Andromeda corner of the Great Square—and discover the extraordinary mercury patterns hidden in its light.

Throughout human history, people have gazed upward to find patterns among the stars, often attributing stories and meaning to their shimmering points of light. Among these celestial objects, some stars stand out due to their brightness, serving as landmarks guiding observers through the night sky. One such prominent star is HIP 677, more widely known as Alpha Andromedae, which resides in the constellation Andromeda.

The Nature and Position of Alpha Andromedae (HIP 677)

Alpha Andromedae is located within the constellation Andromeda, a figure rooted in Greek mythology. It is positioned near the "bright corner" of the Great Square of Pegasus, an asterism composed of four bright stars that form a large square shape across the sky. This prominent pattern is easily recognizable and serves as a useful pointer for locating neighboring constellations and celestial objects.

The catalogue identifies HIP 677 with Alpha Andromedae, also called Alpheratz. Rather than an ordinary star of a generic spectral type, its primary belongs to the chemically peculiar mercury–manganese (HgMn) class. Spectra reveal chemical abundances in the visible atmosphere, not a view of the stellar surface. Astronomers have used those spectra to map regions unusually rich in mercury.

How to Observe Alpha Andromedae

For amateur astronomers eager to spot Alpha Andromedae, the star's location provides a straightforward target:

  • Locate the Great Square of Pegasus: This prominent asterism is visible during autumn in the Northern Hemisphere. It appears as a large, nearly square formation of bright stars overhead or slightly eastward in the evening sky.
  • Identify the Bright Corner: Alpha Andromedae can be found near one of the square's corners, which is notable for its brightness.
  • Use Star Charts or Apps: Star maps, astronomy software, or smartphone apps can assist in confirming the star's precise position relative to other stars in the area.
  • Optimal Viewing Conditions: Under dark skies away from light pollution, Alpha Andromedae is visible to the naked eye, but binoculars or a small telescope can enhance the viewing experience.

The best time to observe this star is during the months of September through November when Andromeda reaches its highest point in the evening sky, making it most accessible.

Scientific and Cultural Significance

Alpheratz offers an unusual example of stellar atmospheric changes. In a 2007 study, Oleg Kochukhov and collaborators reported seven years of observations of the mercury absorption lines in Alpha Andromedae. By tracking how those lines varied as the star rotated, they reconstructed maps showing that mercury-rich regions changed over the years. The researchers argued that heavy-element diffusion and atmospheric dynamics could produce evolving chemical structures without the magnetic mechanism familiar from sunspots. These are not literal drops of liquid mercury floating above the star: the word clouds describes concentrations inferred from stellar spectra.

From a cultural standpoint, recognizing the star's position in relation to the Great Square of Pegasus not only assists in navigation but also deepens appreciation for how ancient civilizations and modern stargazers alike use these patterns to explore the universe.

Exploring Beyond Alpha Andromedae

By identifying Alpha Andromedae, amateur astronomers can navigate toward other remarkable targets, such as the Andromeda Galaxy (M31)—a giant spiral galaxy and our nearest galactic neighbor. Recognizing the stars of the Great Square as a starting point transforms a simple observation into a gateway for broader cosmic exploration.
