# Altair — the star whose shape gave away its spin

Altair looks like a sharp white point at the foot of Aquila, the Eagle. But astronomers have measured something the eye cannot see: its equator bulges outward, and its surface is not equally bright everywhere.

For northern observers, Altair is the southern point of the Summer Triangle. Vega and Deneb form the other corners. Find those three bright stars and you have a large-scale guide to the summer sky. Altair also sits between two fainter stars in Aquila, a compact line that helps distinguish it from other bright points. Its place in this familiar pattern makes it an easy target for a beginner and an unusually revealing one for a physicist.

A star is not a rigid ball. It is hot gas held together by gravity, and rotation changes the balance of forces. Altair spins so quickly that material near its equator lies farther from the rotation axis than material near its poles. The resulting shape is *oblate*: wider across the middle than from pole to pole. In a 1999 NASA Jet Propulsion Laboratory account of Palomar Testbed Interferometer observations, researchers reported that Altair's equatorial diameter was at least 14% greater than its polar diameter. They also inferred a rotation period of no more than about 10.4 hours. Those numbers describe the measurements and limits available at the time, not a timeless exact model of the star. Source: NASA JPL, https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/images/pia04204-altair/.

How can anyone measure the outline of a star so far away? An ordinary telescope cannot show Altair as a detailed disk. *Interferometry* combines light received at separated telescopes so that tiny differences in the incoming wavefront reveal structure at angular scales far smaller than a conventional image could resolve. The Palomar measurements found that Altair's apparent size depended on the direction across which it was measured. That was evidence for an elongated stellar disk rather than a round one.

The next surprise was that the star's brightness also varies across its surface. At a rapidly rotating star's equator, the effective gravity is lower than at its poles. The equatorial surface can therefore be cooler and dimmer, a phenomenon called *gravity darkening*. In 2007 John Monnier and colleagues used the CHARA interferometric array to reconstruct a near-infrared image of Altair. The image revealed both the distorted shape and the brightness pattern. Their paper also warned that simple uniformly rotating models did not fully explain the observations; differential rotation, different gravity-darkening laws, or both might matter. Source: Monnier et al., *Science* 317 (2007), 342–345, DOI: https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1143205.

More recent models combine interferometry with spectroscopy and stellar oscillations to refine the picture. A 2020 two-dimensional analysis found an equatorial radius of about 2.008 solar radii and an inclination near 50.7 degrees, with uncertainties specific to that model. These are research estimates rather than quantities a backyard observer can measure directly. Source: *Astronomy & Astrophysics*, https://www.aanda.org/articles/aa/full_html/2020/01/aa36830-19/aa36830-19.html.

Altair has also gathered stories long before astronomers measured its outline. In the East Asian Cowherd and Weaver Girl tradition, Altair represents the cowherd, separated from the weaver represented by Vega. The stars are connected in the sky by the Milky Way, giving the tale an observable setting. Other traditions identified Altair with an eagle. These different cultural interpretations belong alongside, rather than in competition with, the physical account. Source: NASA Night Sky Network, https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/skywatching/night-sky-network/summer-triangle-corner-altair/.

When you find Altair, you will not see a flattened disk or a darker equator. Even a small telescope shows essentially a point. That limitation is part of the story: an easily recognized naked-eye star became a laboratory for techniques capable of measuring stellar surfaces. The Summer Triangle is the observer's entrance; rapid rotation and interferometry are what make Altair extraordinary.
