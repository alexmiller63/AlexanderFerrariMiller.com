Alpha Caeli — the brightest point in a very faint tool

The Engraving Tool's alpha star is an ordinary hydrogen-burning sun whose modest light makes constellation-finding a test of geometry rather than spectacle.

Alpha Caeli (α Cae), permanent object 730, is HD 29875 and HIP 21770. The stored visual magnitude is about 4.44. SIMBAD classifies it F2 V and gives a Gaia parallax of 49.0620 milliarcseconds. Inverting the parallax in arcseconds gives about 20.4 parsecs, or 66.5 light-years. This is an original calculation from the catalogue value and should retain the measurement's uncertainty. Source: SIMBAD, https://simbad.u-strasbg.fr/simbad/sim-basic?Ident=alf+cae.

F2 describes a photosphere hotter and whiter than the Sun's G type. Luminosity class V means main sequence: the star's long-lived energy source is hydrogen fusion in its core. This is a physical classification derived from the spectrum, not a description of visual size. Alpha Caeli is unresolved in an ordinary telescope. Its modest naked-eye brightness comes from the combination of its luminosity and distance, not from a claim that it is a weak or dying star.

The same SIMBAD entry links Alpha Caeli to eight observations by the International Ultraviolet Explorer and to far-infrared observations made for the DEBRIS survey. Archive entries show that an object has been observed; they do not by themselves prove a discovery of ultraviolet activity, dust or planets. This distinction keeps an observing log from turning telescope time into a physical claim. The STScI IUE atlas independently lists HD 29875 as an F2 V star at visual magnitude 4.45: https://archive.stsci.edu/prepds/iuesass_web/iweb/sips_t3.html.

Caelum is the official Latin constellation name; Caeli means “of Caelum.” The IAU's English rendering is the Engraving Tool. It is one of the small southern instrument constellations associated with eighteenth-century chartmaking rather than an ancient mythic figure. Alpha here means the star received the first Greek letter in this constellation. It happens also to be the brightest, but Bayer letters are identifiers, not a universal ranking guarantee. Official name, abbreviation and genitive: IAU, https://www.iau.org/IAU/IAU/Astronomy-FAQs/Constellations.aspx.

Alpha Caeli is catalogued as component A in a multiple-star designation, and SIMBAD cross-identifies it with WDS J04406−4152A. That label preserves a measured pairing on the sky. It should not be used to promise a cleanly separated companion in a small telescope without checking current separation, brightness contrast and orbital evidence. This draft therefore treats the bright primary as the observing target and leaves detailed double-star guidance for a checked finder or specialist ephemeris.

The stored position is approximately right ascension 4h 41m and declination −41° 52′. At the almanac's default 45° N it culminates only about 3.1° above a level geometric horizon; north of about 48.1° N it never rises. At 35° S it passes nearly overhead, reaching about 83.1°. These original calculations ignore refraction, terrain and buildings. A fourth-magnitude star that is easy from southern latitudes may be functionally invisible through northern haze.

The accepted Caelum figure is a short bent line. Start at the brighter northern end of the figure, move south to Beta Caeli, then slightly farther south and west to Alpha; continue toward the final southern point to confirm the bend. Alpha and Beta are separated by only a few degrees, so retaining both in a wide field is more reliable than searching for an imagined engraving tool. The outline is a charting convention, not a physical family.

Use unaided eyes under a dark sky first, then binoculars if the faint line does not cohere. Record whether Alpha itself was seen, whether Beta was seen, and whether the whole figure could be traced; these are different outcomes. Weekly darkness, Moon interference and culmination time belong to the current almanac.
