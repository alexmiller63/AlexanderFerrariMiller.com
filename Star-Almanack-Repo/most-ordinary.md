# The Most Ordinary Day Throughout the World

We set ourselves a peculiar problem:

**What is the most ordinary instant in the year?**

Not merely an ordinary day. Not merely an ordinary hour. We wanted an instant that, as nearly as possible, had nothing special to recommend it.

This turns out to be harder than it sounds.

Calendars are crowded with significance. Months begin and end. Seasons change. Nations celebrate. Religions observe holy days. International organizations designate days of remembrance or attention. The Moon becomes full or new. Planets reach opposition. Meteor showers peak. The Sun crosses boundaries we have chosen to mark.

Human beings are very good at making days special.

So before looking for candidates, we established the rules.

The rules had to come first. Otherwise it would have been far too easy to choose a date we happened to like and then invent reasons why it was ordinary.

We wanted the opposite.

We wanted the criteria to choose the candidates.

## What Makes a Day Ordinary?

The day should not begin a month or end one.

It should not divide a month neatly in half.

It should not fall on a major civil holiday, a major religious observance, or an international observance that makes a strong claim on the attention of much of the world.

That last qualification matters.

We are not searching for a day on which nobody, anywhere, commemorates anything. Such a day may not exist. There are national holidays, local festivals, saints' days, professional observances, anniversaries, awareness days, and commemorations of almost every imaginable kind.

We are looking instead for a day on which **the calendar, taken as a whole, makes no strong claim.**

The heavens must be similarly quiet.

The day should not contain a solstice or equinox.

It should not mark perihelion or aphelion.

It should not contain an eclipse, a major lunar phase, the peak of a famous meteor shower, a conspicuous planetary opposition, a zodiac boundary, or some other astronomical event that naturally invites us to say:

**Mark this day.**

We were looking, in other words, for an instant at which, as nearly as possible, nothing happens.

Not literally nothing.

The Earth continues to rotate.

The Earth continues around the Sun.

The Moon continues around the Earth.

The planets move.

Stars rise and set.

Somewhere it is dawn. Somewhere else it is sunset.

But nothing about the calendar or the heavens should be standing on a chair waving its arms.

After applying those criteria, four dates survived as our **gloriously uneventful candidates**:

- **May 11, 2026**

- **August 17, 2026**

- **October 19, 2026**

- **November 23, 2026**

At that point, further argument would have defeated the purpose.

All four had survived the same rules. We did not want to invent a microscopic distinction merely so that one of them could be declared more ordinary than the others.

So we used chance.

We assigned the four dates to the four possible results of two coin tosses:

- Heads–Heads: **May 11**

- Heads–Tails: **August 17**

- Tails–Heads: **October 19**

- Tails–Tails: **November 23**

Then we tossed an actual quarter.

Heads.

We tossed it again.

Heads.

The date was therefore:

**May 11, 2026.**

## But What Time?

A day was not enough.

The *Star Almanack* deals in instants, and an instant needs a time.

So we repeated the procedure.

The time should not be midnight or noon.

It should not fall on an exact hour, half hour, or quarter hour.

It should not form a cute or conspicuous numerical pattern.

But the time candidates were deliberately **stacked** in one respect.

Whichever time chance selected, we wanted the worldwide demonstration to show what happens at the International Date Line. At the same physical instant, most of our locations should still call the date May 11 while locations sufficiently far east should already call it May 12.

This was not left to chance. It was one of the criteria.

We choose the properties a candidate must possess.

Chance chooses among the candidates that possess them.

The four gloriously uneventful time candidates were:

- **10:17 UTC**

- **10:43 UTC**

- **11:07 UTC**

- **11:37 UTC**

They were assigned to two more tosses:

- Heads–Heads: **10:17 UTC**

- Heads–Tails: **10:43 UTC**

- Tails–Heads: **11:07 UTC**

- Tails–Tails: **11:37 UTC**

The quarter was tossed.

Tails.

And again.

Heads.

The time was therefore:

**11:07 UTC.**

Thus, by a method that was simultaneously wonderfully silly and surprisingly rigorous, we arrived at:

# May 11, 2026 at 11:07 UTC

**The Most Ordinary Day Throughout the World.**

## One Instant Throughout the World

Now comes the reason for choosing an instant rather than merely a date.

Every location below is experiencing **exactly the same physical instant**: May 11, 2026 at 11:07 UTC.

The locations are deliberately mixed geographically rather than being divided into Northern and Southern Hemisphere groups. The Southern Hemisphere has a slight numerical advantage, since it is so often slighted in descriptions of the night sky.

The examples include places observing daylight saving time and places observing standard time. They include ordinary whole-hour offsets as well as some of the wonderfully untidy half-hour and 45-minute offsets that human civil timekeeping has produced.

The list demonstrates something that is easy to say and surprisingly difficult to feel intuitively:

**Every entry describes exactly the same instant.**

When it is 11:07 UTC, clocks in Los Angeles read 4:07 AM while clocks in Kathmandu read 4:52 PM.

In the Chatham Islands it is 11:52 PM.

In Apia, Nukuʻalofa, and Tokelau, midnight has already passed.

And on Kiritimati it is 1:07 AM on May 12.

Nothing astronomical happened at a different moment in any of these places.

Only the civil representation of the moment changed.

## UTC Is King

This raises an interesting question.

If May 11 is our Most Ordinary Day, what happens when someone on Kiritimati quite correctly looks at a calendar and says that it is May 12?

There is an additional irony.

May 12 would itself have been rejected as a candidate for the Most Ordinary Day. May 12 is the **International Day of Plant Health**, established by the United Nations to raise awareness of the importance of healthy plants to food security, ecosystems, biodiversity, and economic development.

Yet in several places around the world, our selected instant has already become May 12.

This does not mean that our rigorous treatment has failed.

Quite the opposite.

This is a book about astronomy, and astronomical instants in the *Star Almanack* are expressed in UTC. May 11 is the date of the instant in UTC. May 12 is a local civil date attached to precisely the same physical instant.

**UTC is king.**

A uniform expression of time is necessary when discussing astronomy. The same astronomical event cannot acquire a different identity merely because observers in different places set their clocks differently. UTC serves as the common reference.

But once that single instant is translated into local civil times throughout a spherical world, a change of date somewhere becomes unavoidable.

That is why an International Date Line is necessary.

UTC gives us **one instant**.

Local time gives us **many clocks**.

The International Date Line allows those clocks to have **different dates without contradiction**.

The difference is not a defect in the system.

It is the phenomenon the system was designed to describe.

This is precisely the problem encountered by the two travelers in *The Parable of the Two Rabbis*.

Nature supplies one instant.

Human beings can attach different dates to it.

Nature gives us the motion.

We supply the conventions that let us describe it consistently.

That is why the *Star Almanack* gives astronomical events in UTC first.

## The Ordinary Becomes Extraordinary

Our result is not important because something remarkable happened on May 11, 2026 at 11:07 UTC.

Quite the contrary.

Its distinction is that, after deliberately searching for reasons to reject it, we found remarkably little reason to notice it at all.

And yet, at that instant, the entire world was there.

Some people were asleep.

Some were working.

Some were eating breakfast, lunch, or dinner.

In some places the Sun was above the horizon.

In others the stars were visible.

Every clock told the story differently.

But every place was experiencing exactly the same instant.

Dates and clock readings are conventions we use to describe where we are in the continuous motion of the Earth. They are enormously useful conventions, but they are not the physical instant itself.

The sky does not contain time zones.

The Earth does not pause at midnight.

Nothing astronomical happens when a calendar page changes.

Nature gives us motion.

We supply the clocks.

And on **May 11, 2026 at 11:07 UTC**, for no particularly important reason whatsoever, we choose to notice.

**One sky. One instant. Many clocks.**
