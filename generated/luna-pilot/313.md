# Pinwheel Galaxy: A Vast Spiral Hiding in Plain Sight

*In the handle of the Big Dipper, M101 spreads its light across nearly three-quarters the apparent width of the Moon. Finding it is easier than seeing its delicate structure.*

On a clear spring evening, the Big Dipper seems to point the way to everything: follow its handle’s curve to Arcturus, or trace the bowl toward the North Star. But just off the handle, beyond the bright stars, lies a less obvious destination: the Pinwheel Galaxy.

Also cataloged as Messier 101 and NGC 5457, this spiral galaxy sits at coordinates near right ascension 14h 03m and declination +54° 21′. SIMBAD classifies it as an intermediate barred spiral and lists its optical dimensions as about 21.9 by 20.9 arcminutes—roughly two-thirds the full Moon’s apparent width, though far fainter. Its near-round outline on the sky gives a hint of a broad, face-on view. [SIMBAD: NGC 5457](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%205457)

That apparent size is part of the observing puzzle. M101’s light is spread over a large patch, so it can be difficult to spot even when the sky looks dark. A bright galaxy can be easy to find; a wide, faint one may dissolve into the background glow. The spiral arms that give the Pinwheel its name are not guaranteed rewards for a small telescope. In many backyard views, the first impression is simply a soft, uneven haze.

To locate it, begin with Alkaid, the star at the end of the Big Dipper’s handle. From there, sweep about five degrees north-northeast—roughly the width of three fingers held at arm’s length. Binoculars can help you search, but use a wide field and move slowly. A small telescope at low magnification is often a better starting point than a high-power view: it gathers more light while keeping the galaxy’s broad glow in frame.

Give your eyes time. Shield yourself from nearby lamps, let your vision adapt to darkness, and try looking slightly to one side of the suspected spot. This “averted vision” can make a faint object easier to notice. A moonless night away from city lights will improve the odds; under brighter skies, the galaxy may remain elusive even when you are looking in exactly the right place.

The best season for a high evening view is spring in the Northern Hemisphere, when the Big Dipper climbs high. If you do find M101, pause before increasing magnification. The view may not resemble a detailed photograph, but that is no failure: photographs accumulate light over time, while your eye takes in a live, fleeting impression. The galaxy’s cataloged angular span tells you that the target is extensive. The challenge is perceiving its faint light against the night.

That contrast—large on the sky, subtle to the eye—is what makes the Pinwheel a rewarding observing goal. First comes the star-hop. Then the patient search. And, sometimes, a small ghostly glow appears where the map promised a galaxy.
