# NGC 188: A Cluster Near the Pole

*Far from the crowded showpieces of the Milky Way, this northern open cluster offers a quiet lesson in how astronomers tell a stellar family from its neighbors.*

At the edge of a winter star chart, where the familiar patterns seem to thin out, NGC 188 keeps close company with the North Star. Its position—right ascension 00h 47m 11.5s, declination +85° 14′ 38″—puts it just a few degrees from the north celestial pole, a patch of sky that turns slowly while the rest of the heavens wheels around it. [SIMBAD](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%20188) classifies it as an open cluster: a group of stars recorded together in the sky, not a single glowing cloud.

That position makes NGC 188 unusually convenient for northern observers. From locations north of about 5° latitude, it is circumpolar: it stays above the horizon as Earth turns. Its height changes through the night, but it does not make the familiar seasonal exit of many deep-sky targets. From farther north, it rides higher; from near the tropics, it skims low, where haze and buildings can make a difficult target harder still.

The cluster’s apparent span is about 32.6 arcminutes—roughly the width of the full Moon. That does not mean it will look like a bright lunar disk. A cluster’s listed angular size describes the area its stars cover, while the view through an eyepiece depends on their brightness, the sky’s darkness and the instrument. Think of NGC 188 less as a dramatic splash and more as a dispersed gathering: one that rewards patience and a careful look.

There is a deeper reason to linger. On the sky, unrelated stars can fall close together by chance. Astronomers test whether a cluster is a genuine stellar association by comparing clues such as distance and motion. SIMBAD lists a parallax of 0.5053 milliarcseconds for NGC 188, which corresponds to a distance of about 2,000 parsecs, or 6,500 light-years, using the inverse-parallax relation. The database also gives proper-motion and radial-velocity measurements. These kinds of data help astronomers distinguish stars traveling together from foreground or background interlopers. The coordinates and measurements are catalog values, not a promise that every star visible in the same eyepiece belongs to the cluster.

To find it, start with Polaris and use a planetarium app or a detailed star chart to locate the cluster’s position. A finder scope helps; at nearly five degrees from Polaris, NGC 188 is close in astronomical terms, but not tucked right beside it.
