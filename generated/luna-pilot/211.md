# NGC 2867: A Small Circle, a Star’s Last Act

*This compact planetary nebula is easy to underestimate. Find it in the southern sky, then give it magnification—and time.*

NGC 2867 is a planetary nebula: a cloud of gas cast off by a star late in its life and made to glow by the stellar remnant at its center. The name can mislead. These objects have nothing to do with planets; early telescopes made them look like small disks. NGC 2867’s measured size is indeed tiny in the sky, but that apparent smallness hides a stellar transformation on a much larger scale.

The SIMBAD astronomical database classifies NGC 2867 as a planetary nebula and lists its radio angular dimensions as about 0.253 by 0.251 arcminutes—roughly 15 arcseconds across. It gives a visual magnitude of 10.3 and records the spectral classification [WO2]. These are catalog entries, not a complete description of what an observer will see through a particular telescope. [SIMBAD’s NGC 2867 entry](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%202867) provides the measurements and classification.

A planetary nebula forms as a star sheds its outer layers near the end of its life. Radiation from the exposed stellar remnant illuminates the expelled gas, producing the nebula’s glow. The glowing shell is a temporary phase: it disperses over time, while the remnant continues its evolution. In NGC 2867, the cataloged angular size offers a sense of how tightly the nebula is packed into our view, even though its true physical dimensions are not given in the cited entry.

To locate it, use SIMBAD’s J2000 coordinates: right ascension 9 hours 21 minutes 25 seconds, declination −58° 18′ 41″. Its far-southern position makes it a practical target for observers in the Southern Hemisphere. From more northerly locations it remains low above the horizon or, at sufficiently northern latitudes, does not rise. A planetarium app or detailed star chart can help place the coordinates among nearby stars; a broad view of the sky is unlikely to make such a compact object conspicuous.

A telescope is the sensible starting point. At magnitude 10.3, NGC 2867 is not an easy naked-eye target, and its small apparent size can make it resemble a star at low magnification. Begin with a low-power view to find the field, then increase magnification gradually. The goal is not necessarily to reveal a large, richly detailed cloud: a planetary nebula this compact may remain a small disk, with its character emerging through comparison with the sharper points of nearby stars.

Give your eyes time at the eyepiece, and try more than one magnification. If the view seems stellar at first, that does not mean the search has failed; the cataloged dimensions explain why the object can be hard to distinguish. NGC 2867 rewards careful looking rather than spectacle. Its modest circle of light marks the aftermath of a star’s shedding of its outer layers—a brief, luminous stage in a much longer life.
