# Hadar, the Blue Beacon of Centaurus

*Brilliant to the unaided eye, this southern star is catalogued as a pulsating variable—but its changing light is subtler than its steady brilliance suggests.*

On a clear southern evening, Hadar is easy to mistake for a fixed point in the sky: a bright, blue-white spark in Centaurus. Its name belongs to Beta Centauri, one of the sky’s conspicuous southern stars. The SIMBAD astronomical database lists it at visual magnitude 0.58 and classifies it as a “beta Cep Variable”—a star in a group of hot stars known for pulsations that cause their light to vary. To the eye, though, Hadar appears steadfast. Its stellar rhythm is a project for instruments and repeated measurements, not a flicker to catch in a casual glance.

The catalogue gives Hadar’s position as right ascension 14 hours 03 minutes and declination 60 degrees 22 minutes south. That far-southern declination shapes the view: Hadar stands high for observers in the Southern Hemisphere, but it stays below the horizon for locations north of about 30 degrees north latitude. In the catalogue, its parallax is 8.32 milliarcseconds. Inverting that measurement gives a rough distance of about 120 parsecs, or 390 light-years; the listed parallax uncertainty means this is an estimate, not a precise cosmic yardstick.

SIMBAD also lists a B1 III spectral type, though it marks that classification with a low-quality flag. In the standard spectral sequence, B stars are hot and blue-white; the “III” denotes a giant luminosity class. That description helps explain why Hadar shines so strongly in our sky despite its great distance. Its catalogue entry also records a radial velocity of about 2.5 kilometres per second, with a comparatively large uncertainty. Such measurements turn a bright point into a physical object: one with a position, a distance estimate, a stellar classification and motion along our line of sight.
