# MCG 9-17-10: A Quiet Galaxy with an Active Heart

*In Ursa Major, a slim smear of light marks a Seyfert galaxy whose most energetic story unfolds beyond the reach of the eyepiece.*

On a clear northern-spring night, turn toward Ursa Major and look for a galaxy that offers no grand entrance. MCG 9-17-10 is catalogued by SIMBAD as NGC 3079: a distant spiral whose light arrives as a modest, elongated glow. Its coordinates are right ascension 10h 01m 58s, declination +55° 40′ 47″, and its listed visual magnitude is 10.86. [SIMBAD](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%203079)

The galaxy’s apparent shape is a useful first clue. SIMBAD lists its optical dimensions as about 10.8 by 2.7 arcminutes, a long, narrow outline against the background sky. Those measurements describe the galaxy’s apparent span, not its true physical size; the catalog lists a redshift of 0.0035, but turning that into a distance requires assumptions about the expansion of the universe and the galaxy’s own motion. Even so, the figures hint at the challenge: this is not a pinpoint star, but a broad, faint object whose light is spread thinly across the eyepiece.

The phrase “Seyfert 2 galaxy” adds a more dramatic layer. NGC 3079 is classified as a Seyfert 2—a galaxy with an active central region, where material falling toward a supermassive black hole can release intense energy. In this class, gas and dust around the nucleus affect what we see from Earth, helping explain why its central activity is not simply a bright point in an amateur telescope. The catalog classification describes the galaxy’s energetic nature; it does not promise a visible flare.

That contrast makes the object rewarding. The observer sees a dim, subdued patch; the science points to a restless center. The glow is a composite of starlight and other emission from a galaxy system, arriving from far beyond the Milky Way
