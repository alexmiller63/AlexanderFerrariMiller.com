# MCG 4-30-22: A spiral with a restless heart

*In the spring sky, a galaxy’s faint glow carries two stories: the slow sweep of its spiral arms and the activity hidden in its core.*

MCG 4-30-22 is cataloged as NGC 4725, a galaxy whose light reaches us as a soft, extended patch rather than a point of fire. That distinction matters at the eyepiece: even a galaxy listed at magnitude 12.44 spreads its light across a broad area, making it subtler than a star of the same brightness. The catalog gives its apparent dimensions as roughly 10.6 by 5.9 arcminutes—about a third of the Moon’s width along its longer axis. [SIMBAD’s NGC 4725 entry](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%204725) lists those measurements, along with its position and classification.

The coordinates are right ascension 12h 50m and declination +25° 30′ (J2000), placing the galaxy well north of the celestial equator. Its right ascension makes it a spring-evening target for northern observers: the sky turns it into a convenient destination as the season advances. Find it with a detailed star chart or planetarium app, using the coordinates to confirm the field. A low-power eyepiece can help you locate the surrounding stars before you increase magnification.

Give your eyes time to adjust, and shield them from nearby white light. Under a dark sky, try averted vision—looking slightly to one side of the galaxy’s position. The eye’s peripheral vision is often more sensitive to faint light. A larger telescope helps, but sky darkness matters greatly; haze, moonlight and urban skyglow can wash out the galaxy’s diffuse glow. Don’t expect a crisp photograph in the eyepiece. Look instead for a faint oval haze, then stay with it. Details may emerge gradually.

The catalog describes NGC 4725 as an SABab spiral. In this notation, “SAB” indicates an intermediate form between a strongly barred and an unbarred spiral, while “ab” places its spiral type between the Sa and Sb classes. It is also classified as a Seyfert 2 galaxy: its central region belongs to the active-galaxy family, where material around a central supermassive black hole can produce intense emission. The “2” is an observational category, not a measure of how powerful the galaxy is. In the standard picture of active galactic nuclei, viewing angle and obscuring material help shape what we see; the label alone does not reveal every detail of this particular galaxy’s core.

There is another scale hidden in the numbers. SIMBAD gives NGC 4725 a redshift of 0.003951 and a heliocentric radial velocity of about 1,182 kilometers per second. Redshift shifts the galaxy’s spectral lines toward longer wavelengths, a sign of its recession. That motion is not visible in the eyepiece. The faint glow is the evidence our eyes can gather directly; the spectrum supplies a different kind of witness.

At the telescope, MCG 4-30-22 asks for patience rather than spectacle. Its light may arrive as little more than a dim smudge. Yet that smudge marks a vast spiral system, with a classified active core, suspended in a spring sky.
