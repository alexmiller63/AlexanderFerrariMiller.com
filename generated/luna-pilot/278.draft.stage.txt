# MCG 5-30-30: A Quiet Spiral in Coma Berenices

*An expansive, star-forming galaxy rewards patient viewing—and a dark sky.*

In the spring sky, MCG 5-30-30 waits in Coma Berenices, a constellation best known not for bright stars but for the galaxies scattered across its northern reaches. Cataloged also as NGC 4559, this large spiral lies far beyond the Milky Way. Its light arrives as a soft, elongated glow: a reminder that a galaxy can be immense and still appear delicate from Earth.

The coordinates listed for NGC 4559 are right ascension 12 hours 35 minutes 57.6 seconds, declination +27 degrees 57 minutes 36 seconds. Its reported optical dimensions—about 7.2 by 3.0 arcminutes—make it broad on the sky, though not necessarily easy to see. The catalog gives a visual magnitude near 10.0, but that figure describes the galaxy’s total light. Spread across its extended face, the light has low surface brightness; a dark, transparent sky matters more than the headline magnitude might suggest.

There is structure behind the faintness. NGC 4559 is classified as an SABcd galaxy: a late-type spiral with an intermediate or weak bar. The “cd” designation points to loosely wound spiral structure, while its cataloged H II-galaxy type signals emission associated with ionized hydrogen. Such regions are linked to energetic young stars and ongoing star formation. In a telescope, however, those details may not resolve into distinct knots. The first impression is more likely to be a pale oval, with its shape emerging as the eye adapts.

The catalog also lists a radial velocity of about 815 kilometers per second. That measured motion is useful context, but it should not be turned casually into a precise distance: for nearby galaxies, the expansion of the universe is not the only influence on their motion. The entry’s parallax is not a meaningful way to measure a galaxy’s distance, either. For observers, the practical truth is simpler: this is an external galaxy, a vast stellar system seen as one modest patch of light.

Try it on a moonless night in spring, when Coma Berenices is well placed in the evening sky. Use a star chart or planetarium app to navigate to the coordinates; don’t expect the galaxy to stand out in a finder or binoculars from a bright location. Under dark skies, binoculars may show a subtle haze, while a small telescope at low power can help frame its extended shape. An 8-inch telescope, steady viewing, and averted vision may reveal a more definite oval. Increase magnification only if the view remains bright and stable; spreading a faint object too much can make it disappear.

Give your eyes time in the dark, shield the eyepiece from stray light, and look slightly to one side of the galaxy rather than directly at it. That indirect gaze can make faint detail easier to detect. MCG 5-30-30 will not deliver the sharp, high-contrast drama of a planet or a bright nebula. Its pleasure is quieter: recognizing that the dim smudge in the eyepiece is a sprawling spiral, with star-forming regions and countless suns, seen across the gulf of intergalactic space.
