# Arp 41: A Spiral on the Southern Sky’s Edge

*The galaxy also catalogued as NGC 1232 spreads across about five arcminutes in Eridanus. Its broad face-on appearance rewards patient viewing—and a dark sky.*

A galaxy can look delicate and still be vast. Arp 41, identified with NGC 1232, is a spiral in Eridanus whose light has travelled across space to reach us. In a telescope, it is not a sharply outlined pinwheel but a broad, low-contrast glow: the sort of object that asks the eye to settle in and look indirectly.

NGC 1232’s listed position is right ascension 03h 09m 45.5s, declination −20° 34′ 45″. SIMBAD gives its optical extent as about 5.1 by 4.8 arcminutes—roughly one-sixth the apparent width of the Moon. The galaxy is classified SAB(rs)c: a spiral with some central bar structure, a ring-like feature near the bar, and relatively open arms. These letters are a shorthand for appearance, not a claim that every structure will be obvious through an eyepiece. The database also classifies it as an emission-line galaxy, meaning that its recorded spectrum includes emission from glowing gas.

There is a useful scale hidden in its spectrum. SIMBAD lists a redshift of 0.005611, corresponding to a recession speed of about 1,680 kilometres per second. That is a measurement of the galaxy’s motion away from us, not a direct mileage reading: nearby galaxies’ motions are influenced by local gravitational flows as well as the expansion of the universe. Still, the number is a reminder that this soft patch is not a cloud in our own neighbourhood. It is a separate star system, with its own stars, gas and history.

For observers, the challenge is contrast. A galaxy’s light is spread over an area of sky, so a bright Moon, haze or nearby streetlamp can wash out the faint outer regions even when the telescope is capable of collecting plenty of light. Let your eyes adapt in darkness, shield them from stray light and begin with a low-power eyepiece. Once the galaxy is located, try averted vision—looking slightly to one side of it—to make faint structure easier to detect. Increase magnification only if the sky remains steady and dark.

The coordinates place NGC 1232 well south of the celestial equator. It is especially well positioned for observers in the Southern Hemisphere; from northern locations it rises, but culminates lower in the sky. For example, at latitude 40° north it reaches only about 30° above the southern horizon. Choose a site with a clear southern view and observe when Eridanus is high, on autumn or winter evenings in the Northern Hemisphere. A telescope around 150–200 millimetres in aperture is a sensible starting point under dark skies, though a smaller instrument may show the central glow.
