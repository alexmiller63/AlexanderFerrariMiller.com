# 42 Orionis: A Young Star in the Sword

*Look beyond Orion’s famous nebula to find a modest naked-eye star whose catalog classifications hint at a more complicated life.*

Orion’s Sword is often treated as a route to one destination: the Orion Nebula, M42. But just north of that glowing cloud, a quieter point of light rewards a closer look. 42 Orionis sits at the edge of the sword, roughly half a degree north of M42. It is bright enough to spot with the unaided eye under a dark sky, yet its identity is less straightforward than its steady appearance suggests.

SIMBAD lists 42 Orionis—also identified there as c Ori—as a young stellar object. The same entry gives it a visual magnitude of 4.59 and a spectral type of B1V+?, a notation suggesting a B-type main-sequence star, with a question mark that signals uncertainty. B-type stars are hot and blue-white. The catalog classifications are useful clues, not a complete biography: they point to a star of interest while reminding us that the details are not settled in this entry. [SIMBAD: HIP 26237](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=HIP%2026237)

The star’s coordinates—right ascension 05h 35m 23s, declination −04° 50′—place it in Orion’s busy central region. There, the sky offers a satisfying contrast: M42’s broad, misty glow and 42 Orionis’s sharp point of light. A star can seem like the simplest thing in the eyepiece, but its spectrum and catalog record carry information that the eye alone cannot reveal. That is part of the pleasure of observing: first find the light, then ask what kind of object is making it.

For a first search, wait until Orion is well above the horizon on a clear evening. Find the three stars of the Belt, then follow the short line of stars hanging below it to the Sword. M42 is the Sword’s obvious hazy patch. From there, scan a little northward—toward the Belt—for 42 Orionis. In binoculars, the crowded Sword is a rewarding field; a small telescope at low power can help separate its points of light. If the sky is bright or hazy, concentrate on locating the star rather than expecting faint surrounding detail.
