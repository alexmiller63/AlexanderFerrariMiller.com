# NGC 663: Reading the Measurements Behind a Star Cluster

*An open cluster’s catalog entry is more than a name and a position. It is a compact record of how astronomers describe an object’s place, apparent size and motion.*

A patch of stars can look like a simple pattern in the sky. In a catalog, however, it becomes a set of measurable properties. The SIMBAD astronomical database classifies NGC 663 as an open cluster and gives its position, angular size, brightness and motion. Each number answers a different question—and each comes with limits that matter when interpreting it.

SIMBAD places NGC 663 at right ascension 1 hour, 46 minutes, 21 seconds and declination +61 degrees, 12 minutes, 42 seconds, using the J2000 coordinate system. These coordinates are a way to specify a location on the celestial sphere, much as latitude and longitude specify a place on Earth. They give an observer or a digital sky chart a precise starting point for locating the cluster.

The database lists an angular size of 18 by 18 arcminutes. That describes how large the object appears on the sky, not its physical diameter. Apparent size depends on both an object’s actual extent and its distance from us. To learn about distance, astronomers can use parallax: the apparent shift in a nearby object’s position as Earth moves around the Sun. SIMBAD lists NGC 663’s parallax as 0.320 milliarcseconds, with an uncertainty of 0.002 milliarcseconds.

Taking the inverse of that parallax gives a rough distance of about 3,100 parsecs, or approximately 10,000 light-years. This is an estimate derived from the cataloged value, not a direct measurement with a physical measuring tape. The small parallax is itself a reminder of the scale involved: the more distant an object is, the smaller its apparent shift.

Brightness requires similar care. SIMBAD lists a visual magnitude of 7.1, but marks the measurement with a low-quality flag. Magnitude is a numerical measure of apparent brightness, with smaller numbers corresponding to brighter objects. The catalog value can help describe the cluster, but its quality flag means it should not be treated as a precise prediction of what an observer will see. The database also lists a B magnitude of 7.78, likewise flagged as low quality.

Motion adds another layer to the portrait. SIMBAD records a radial velocity of −32.3 kilometres per second, with an uncertainty of 1.2 kilometres per second. Radial velocity measures motion along the line of sight; under the usual convention, a negative value indicates motion toward us. The entry also lists proper motion—the apparent movement across the sky—as −1.110 milliarcseconds per year in right ascension and −0.235 milliarcseconds per year in declination. These shifts are far too small to notice in an ordinary glance, but they can be measured by comparing carefully recorded positions.

Together, the figures show how a catalog turns a celestial object into a more complete description. Coordinates tell where to look; angular size describes its apparent span; parallax provides a distance estimate; brightness and motion add further measurements. None of these numbers, taken alone, tells the whole story. Their value lies in how they let observers and researchers describe the same object in consistent, testable terms.

**Observing guide:** To search for NGC 663, enter its name or its listed coordinates—01h 46m 21s, +61° 12′ 42″—into a current sky chart or planetarium program. The 18-arcminute angular size offers a useful scale for identifying the cataloged extent. Treat the listed visual magnitude as approximate, since SIMBAD flags that measurement as uncertain
