# Epsilon Ursae Minoris: A Quiet Companion to the North

*Near the celestial pole, this modest naked-eye star offers a lesson in stellar activity—and a rewarding target for patient observers.*

Polaris gets the attention, but the northern sky has other stars worth seeking. Epsilon Ursae Minoris is one: a fourth-magnitude point in Ursa Minor, close enough to the pole to circle the sky for observers across much of the Northern Hemisphere. It won’t outshine the familiar North Star. Its interest lies in what careful records reveal: this is a variable star, catalogued as an RS Canum Venaticorum system.

The name sounds like an address, and in a way it is. Epsilon marks the star’s place in the traditional naming of Ursa Minor, the Little Bear. In modern coordinates, it lies at right ascension 16h 45m 58s and declination +82° 02′, according to SIMBAD. That far-northern declination keeps it above the horizon all night for locations north of about 8° latitude. From mid-northern latitudes, it traces a compact circle around the pole, though its position in the sky changes with the hour and season.

Epsilon UMi is a G5 giant, a star whose spectrum and luminosity class help astronomers describe its temperature and evolutionary state. SIMBAD lists its apparent visual magnitude as about 4.21: bright enough to see without optical aid under a reasonably dark sky, but easy to lose amid city glare or moonlight. Its measured parallax, about 9.86 milliarcseconds, places it roughly 330 light-years away.

The variable-star classification adds another layer. RS CVn stars are associated with close binary systems and magnetic activity; starspots can change how much light reaches us as the stars rotate. That makes Epsilon UMi more than a fixed pinprick, but don’t expect to watch it visibly pulse from one moment to the next. Changes in variable stars are generally best followed with repeated estimates over time, comparing the target with nearby stars of known or reliably similar brightness. SIMBAD identifies the classification, but the page cited here does not give a period or a brightness range—so don’t infer one from a single night’s view.

For a first look, start with the Little Dipper and use a current sky chart or planetarium app to pinpoint Epsilon UMi. The surrounding stars are not all equally bright, and the dipper can be subtle under light-polluted skies. Let your eyes adapt to darkness, then try unaided vision; binoculars will make the star easier to pick out and add the surrounding pattern, but a telescope is not necessary. For a variable-star session, note the date, time, observing conditions and comparison stars. Several estimates on different nights are more useful than a single impression.

The North Star anchors the sky in our imagination. Epsilon Ursae Minoris offers a quieter reward: a chance to look past the landmark and meet one of the stars that keeps it company.
