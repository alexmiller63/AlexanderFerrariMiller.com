# Cigar Galaxy: A Bright Sliver in the Great Bear

*An easy-to-find northern target, M82 rewards patient viewing—and carries a reminder that a galaxy’s catalog label is only one clue to its nature.*

On a clear spring evening, turn your telescope toward the Great Bear and look for a narrow glow: the **Cigar Galaxy**, also cataloged as M82 and NGC 3034. Its name describes the view. Instead of a round, even disk, the galaxy appears stretched into a small luminous streak, as if someone had drawn a soft pencil line across the dark.

That streak is light from a whole galaxy. Its apparent shape is a matter of perspective: we see M82’s disk from a strongly tilted angle, rather than face-on. In a small telescope, the first impression may be only a faint smudge. Take a little time at the eyepiece, though, and the elongated form becomes clearer. The contrast between that tiny patch and the immense system it represents is part of the pleasure.

There is more to the object than its silhouette. The SIMBAD astronomical database identifies NGC 3034 as M82 and lists it as an active galactic nucleus. That is a classification of the galaxy’s central activity—not a description of how it will look through an amateur telescope. SIMBAD also lists a heliocentric radial velocity of about 219 kilometers per second, with an uncertainty of 27 kilometers per second. Radial velocity measures motion along our line of sight; it is not, by itself, a direct reading of the galaxy’s distance. [SIMBAD: NGC 3034](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%203034)

M82 has a nearby companion in the sky: M81, also known as Bode’s Galaxy. SIMBAD notes the possible association between M82 and NGC 3031 (M81). The pair makes a satisfying observing project: compare M81’s more rounded appearance with M82’s narrow profile, then consider how different angles and structures can produce very different views of galaxies beyond our own.

For a first attempt, let your eyes adjust to the dark and use binoculars or a small telescope under a dark, transparent sky. A star chart or planetarium app can help you locate the M81–M82 area in Ursa Major. In a telescope, begin with a low-power eyepiece and a wide field; once you have the pair centered, increase magnification gradually to see whether M82’s shape becomes more distinct. Use averted vision—looking slightly to one side of the galaxy—if its faint glow seems to disappear when you stare directly at it.

Don’t expect the colors and intricate structures often seen in long-exposure photographs. Your eye is gathering far less light, and the galaxy may remain a subdued gray-white streak. That is not a failed observation. It is the real-time view: a distant system reduced to a delicate trace, while the catalog coordinates give you a precise address for finding it. M82’s narrow profile is both a practical guide at the eyepiece and an invitation to look again. **Sources:** [SIMBAD object record for NGC 3034](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%203034).
