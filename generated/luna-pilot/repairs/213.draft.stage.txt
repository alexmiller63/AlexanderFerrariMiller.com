# MCG 6-22-13: A Quiet Spiral in Leo Minor

*At the eyepiece, this distant galaxy is less a showpiece than a patient reward: a faint oval whose catalog numbers hint at the energetic, rotating system beyond the glow.*

Some galaxies announce themselves with dramatic arms or a bright central blaze. MCG 6-22-13 asks for a different kind of attention. In the eyepiece, it may be no more than a dim, elongated haze—but that small patch of light is a vast spiral galaxy, cataloged as NGC 3003, where stars, gas and dust form a structure spanning several arcminutes across the sky.

The catalog measurements put NGC 3003 at right ascension 9h 48m 35.6s and declination +33° 25′ 18″ (J2000), in Leo Minor. Its listed optical dimensions are about 6.0 by 1.6 arcminutes, and its morphological classification is SAB: a spiral with a weak or intermediate bar. SIMBAD also identifies it as an emission-line galaxy, meaning its recorded spectrum includes bright emission from gas. These labels describe different aspects of the galaxy; neither promises a striking visual display through a backyard telescope. [SIMBAD: NGC 3003](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%203003)

Its measured redshift is 0.004976. In everyday terms, the galaxy’s light is shifted toward longer wavelengths as the universe expands. Turning that redshift into a distance requires care: nearby galaxies can have their own motions, on top of cosmic expansion. A simple estimate using the redshift and a typical value for the expansion rate gives roughly 21 megaparsecs, or around 68 million light-years—not a direct distance measurement, but a useful scale for imagining the object.

That scale changes the view. The galaxy’s apparent length of six arcminutes is only a small fraction of the Moon’s apparent width, yet it represents a stellar system stretching across tens of thousands of light-years. Its cataloged blue magnitude is 12.3, a reminder that an extended galaxy spreads its light over a broad area. It can therefore be harder to detect than a star of similar total brightness.

For observers, the practical invitation is simple: choose a dark, transparent night and look for averted vision to help reveal the galaxy’s faint outline. A six- to eight-inch telescope is a sensible starting point under a dark sky; larger instruments and careful viewing may bring out more of its elongated shape. Begin at low or moderate magnification to locate the field, then increase the power only if the image remains clear. Moonlight, haze and nearby artificial lighting can make the difference between a fleeting suspicion and a steady sighting.

The coordinates put MCG 6-22-13 in the springtime evening sky for northern observers. Use a current star chart or planetarium app to hop from familiar stars to its position; the galaxy itself is too subtle to serve as its own signpost. Once found, give your eyes time to adapt and resist the urge to demand detail. The reward is the encounter: a faint smudge, light that has crossed intergalactic space, and the knowledge that behind that modest oval lies a barred spiral turning far beyond the reach of unaided sight.
