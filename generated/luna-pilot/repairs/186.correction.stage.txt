# Hubble’s Variable Nebula: What a Catalog Can Tell Us

A name can make a promise. “Hubble’s Variable Nebula” suggests a cloud that changes, while “R Mon” points to a star. In SIMBAD’s record for NGC 2261, both names appear: the nebula is listed among the object’s identifiers, and the star is identified as V* R Mon. The same entry classifies R Mon as a Herbig Ae/Be star. These labels give us a starting point—but they are not, by themselves, a full account of what changes in the nebula or why. [SIMBAD: NGC 2261](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%202261)

The catalog locates the object at right ascension 6 hours, 39 minutes, 9.95 seconds and declination +8 degrees, 44 minutes, 9.5 seconds, in the J2000 coordinate system. Those precise numbers are a practical way to point a telescope or mark a chart. They also offer a reminder: astronomical names may be evocative, but coordinates make an object identifiable in the sky. [SIMBAD: NGC 2261](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%202261)

SIMBAD’s record gives R Mon a spectral type of B8IIIe. It also lists measurements in several filters, including a visual-band value of 11.85. That figure is a catalog entry, not a promise of what an observer will see on a particular night. The record gives no error beside that visual value, and it identifies R Mon as a variable star. A number in a database is useful, but it does not erase the difference between a recorded measurement and a changing source. [SIMBAD: NGC 2261](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%202261)

The range of listed measurements hints at how many ways astronomers record light. SIMBAD includes values for U, B, V, R and I filters, as well as measurements in G, J, H and K. Each is a separate entry associated with a particular band and reference. The catalog does not turn these figures into a single, timeless portrait. Instead, it preserves measurements that can be compared and interpreted in context. [SIMBAD: NGC 2261](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%202261)

That distinction matters when approaching an object whose common name includes “Variable.” The name is an invitation to wonder about change; the database supports calling R Mon a variable star. But the listed information alone does not describe a schedule or a cause for that variability, nor does it explain how the nebula’s appearance may change. A careful account should leave those questions open unless other evidence answers them.

There is still something compelling in the catalog’s compact portrait. One entry gathers a nebular name, a stellar identifier, a spectral classification, coordinates and measurements of light. Each detail answers a different question: what the object is called, where it is, how the star is classified, and what brightness values have been recorded. Together they make NGC 2261 more than a striking name, without pretending that a catalog entry tells the whole story.

For an observer, the coordinates offer a route to the field; the listed visual value offers a point of reference. What appears through an instrument, however, is an encounter with the sky, not a guarantee supplied by a number. Hubble’s Variable Nebula remains an apt name to carry into that encounter—provided we let the evidence, as well as the name, guide what we say.
