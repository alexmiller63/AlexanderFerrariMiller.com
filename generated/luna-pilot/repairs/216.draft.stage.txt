# MCG 9-17-10: A Galaxy on the Northern Spring Sky

*An elongated spiral in Ursa Major offers a small-telescope glimpse of a galaxy with an active nucleus.*

MCG 9-17-10 is a rewarding target for patient observers: not a bright beacon, but a faint, stretched glow that asks you to look carefully. Listed in SIMBAD as NGC 3079, it lies at J2000 coordinates 10h 01m 58s, +55° 40′ 47″—a northern-sky position that makes it well placed for observers in northern latitudes. [SIMBAD’s NGC 3079 entry](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%203079) classifies the galaxy as a Seyfert 2 and gives it a spiral morphological type.

That classification adds a deeper story to the smudge in the eyepiece. A Seyfert galaxy belongs to the family of galaxies whose central regions emit unusually strong radiation; “Type 2” is a category of active galactic nucleus. The galaxy’s cataloged redshift is about 0.0035, corresponding to a heliocentric radial velocity of roughly 1,050 kilometers per second. That number describes its motion relative to the Sun; it is not, by itself, a precise distance measurement.

NGC 3079 is broad on the sky but its light is spread thinly. SIMBAD lists an optical size of about 10.8 by 2.7 arcminutes and a V-band magnitude of 10.86. The dimensions suggest an elongated form, while the modest integrated magnitude can be misleading: light dispersed across a large area is harder to detect than the same light concentrated in a point. Under a dark sky, the challenge is less about finding a brilliant object than about allowing a faint shape to emerge.

For a spring-evening search, begin with a planetarium app or printed chart centered on the coordinates above. A low-power eyepiece gives a wider field for locating the galaxy; once you have it, try higher magnification to darken the background and inspect the central region. An 8-inch telescope under dark skies is a sensible starting point, though visibility depends strongly on transparency, light pollution and the observer’s experience. Use averted vision—look just to one side of the target—and give your eyes time to adapt. Avoid observing near a bright Moon if you can.

The first view may be subtle: a narrow haze against the stars, perhaps with a brighter central area. Don’t expect a photograph’s contrast or detail. Galaxies are extended objects, and the atmosphere, telescope and sky all shape what reaches your eye. If the target seems to vanish when you stare straight at it, shift your gaze slightly and hold still. Averted vision can make faint structure easier to notice.

A useful observing note records the date, sky conditions, telescope and eyepiece, along with what you actually saw. Even a simple sketch can help distinguish a real impression from an expectation on a later night. MCG 9-17-10 is a reminder that observing is not always about spectacle. Sometimes the reward is recognizing, in a slim patch of light, another vast galaxy—and knowing that its quiet appearance comes with an active heart.
