# UFO Galaxy: A Cosmic Silhouette

*NGC 2683’s slim, edge-on profile turns a distant galaxy into a rewarding telescope target.*

Some galaxies announce themselves with bright, sweeping arms. NGC 2683 makes a quieter entrance: a narrow streak of light, tilted against the dark. Its popular name, the **UFO Galaxy**, suits the silhouette—though what we’re seeing is no spacecraft, but a galaxy viewed nearly edge-on.

That angle is the key to its appearance. A spiral galaxy is a broad disk of stars, gas and dust. Seen from above, its arms can spread across the sky; seen from the side, the disk is compressed into a long, slender shape. NGC 2683’s cataloged optical dimensions are about 8.71 by 2.57 arcminutes, a noticeably elongated outline. The galaxy’s form is a perspective effect: we see its disk projected against the sky rather than looking down onto it.

The catalog also lists NGC 2683 as an active galaxy nucleus. That classification concerns the galaxy’s central region, not a feature likely to leap out in an eyepiece. Through an amateur telescope, the main attraction is the overall shape—a subtle, extended glow that rewards patient viewing. [SIMBAD’s NGC 2683 entry](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%202683) gives its coordinates as right ascension 8 hours 52 minutes 41 seconds and declination +33 degrees 25 minutes (J2000), along with its measured angular size and catalog classifications.

For observers in the Northern Hemisphere, late winter and spring evenings are a useful time to seek it out. The coordinates are a reliable starting point: enter “NGC 2683” into a telescope’s GoTo system, or use them with a current star chart or planetarium app. The object lies at a high northern declination, making it accessible from many northern observing locations. A chart will help you identify the surrounding star field and distinguish the galaxy from nearby stars.

Use a telescope rather than expecting a naked-eye sighting. Choose a dark, moonless night, allow your eyes time to adapt, and begin at low power to locate the field. Then try moderate magnification. Galaxies have low surface brightness compared with stars, so a dark site and careful viewing matter more than simply turning up the power. Look for a faint, elongated patch; averted vision—looking slightly to one side of the target—may make its shape easier to detect. If the view seems underwhelming at first, give your eyes a few minutes and try again.

The UFO Galaxy is not a dramatic, high-contrast object. Its appeal is more intimate: the chance to recognize a remote stellar disk as a galaxy, not a point of light. With the right conditions, that thin smudge becomes a cosmic silhouette—an entire system of stars seen from an unusual angle.
