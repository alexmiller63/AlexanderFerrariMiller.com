# M4: A Star City Beside the Scorpion’s Heart

*Near the red glow of Antares, this globular cluster gathers countless distant suns into a small, soft blur. A pair of binoculars can find it; a telescope begins to break it apart.*

The first thing you notice is the color: Antares burns orange-red, like an ember held against the dark. Then, just to its west, a fainter smudge appears. That small haze is M4, a globular cluster in Scorpius—an immense gathering of stars bound together by gravity and orbiting our Milky Way.

At first glance, M4 looks modest. But the view is a trick of distance. The cluster’s stars are so far away that their combined light reaches us as a pale patch, even though the patch spans a broad region of sky. SIMBAD classifies M4 as a globular cluster and lists its position as right ascension 16h 23m 35s, declination −26° 31′ 33″. Its cataloged angular size is 26.3 arcminutes—close to the apparent width of the full Moon. That doesn’t mean it looks as bright or defined as the Moon: it means its light is spread across a similar slice of sky.

For a first view, find Antares, the brightest star in Scorpius. M4 lies about 1.3 degrees west of it, a little less than three full-Moon widths away. In binoculars, scan slowly from Antares; M4 should show as a dim, fuzzy spot. Under very dark skies, sharp-eyed observers may glimpse it without optical aid, but binoculars make the search easier. A small telescope adds contrast, and a larger one can begin to resolve stars around the cluster’s outer regions. The central glow remains tightly crowded.

The best view depends less on magnification than on a clear, steady, transparent sky. M4 sits low for many observers in the Northern Hemisphere, so buildings, trees, haze and atmospheric turbulence can soften the view. Wait until Scorpius has climbed clear of the horizon, and give your eyes time to adapt to darkness. If the cluster seems to vanish when you look straight at it, try looking slightly to one side; peripheral vision can be more sensitive to faint light.

There is a quiet pleasure in finding M4 because it sits so near a familiar landmark. Antares is a single bright point; M4 is a crowded system, too remote for the naked eye to separate into individual suns. In the telescope, the blur begins to sparkle at its edges. The change is not just a matter of magnification. It is a reminder that the sky’s simplest shapes—one point, one smudge—can conceal very different kinds of worlds.

M4 is a good target for an unhurried evening: locate Antares, nudge west, and let the small blur settle into view. The cluster will not flash or move. The reward is subtler: a glimpse of a stellar city, gathered into one soft light.
