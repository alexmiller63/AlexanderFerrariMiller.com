# NGC 772: A Galaxy in Company

*In the constellation Aries, a galaxy’s catalogue entry offers a compact portrait: its measured size, position, brightness and a notable detail about its surroundings.*

NGC 772 is listed by SIMBAD as a galaxy in a pair of galaxies. That classification makes its setting part of the story, while also setting a limit on what can be concluded: the label identifies it as part of a pair, but does not by itself describe the relationship or any particular encounter. For a distant object, even a brief catalogue entry can offer a useful starting point—provided its measurements are kept distinct from what they do and do not explain.

SIMBAD records NGC 772’s morphological type as SA. Its optical angular size is about 4.99 by 3.58 arcminutes, with a listed orientation angle of 125 degrees. These figures describe how large the galaxy appears in the sky and the direction of its measured major axis. They are not a physical diameter: the entry’s angular measurement alone does not tell a reader how many light-years across the galaxy is.

The catalogue also gives a V-band visual magnitude of 10.31. That value is one measurement of its brightness, not a complete description of how the galaxy would look through a particular telescope or camera. SIMBAD’s entry includes measurements in several other bands too, including ultraviolet, infrared and optical filters. The variety is a reminder that a catalogue can present an object through multiple kinds of observation, each recorded in its own terms.

To identify the field, the entry provides J2000 coordinates: right ascension 1 hour, 59 minutes, 19.56 seconds and declination +19 degrees, 00 minutes, 27.48 seconds. These coordinates act as a precise address on the sky. They are more useful for locating the target than a broad constellation name, especially when comparing observations or searching a star chart. SIMBAD also lists the position’s measurement quality as A, its highest category in the site’s stated coordinate-quality scale.

There is another numerical clue in the record: an emission redshift of 0.008327, with a listed uncertainty of 0.000010. The entry also reports a radial velocity of 2,486 kilometres per second. These are catalogue measurements, not a narrative of the galaxy’s past. In particular, neither the redshift nor the pair classification tells us, on its own, what has happened between NGC 772 and another galaxy.
