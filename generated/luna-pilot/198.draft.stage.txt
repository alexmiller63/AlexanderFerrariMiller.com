# NGC 2440: A Star’s Last Bright Breath

*This compact planetary nebula marks a dramatic stage in stellar evolution. Find it in the southern sky, then give your telescope time to reveal its small, luminous form.*

NGC 2440 is a reminder that a star’s ending can be a beginning for the material around it. Listed as a planetary nebula in the SIMBAD astronomical database, it lies at right ascension 7h 41m 55s and declination −18° 12′ 30″ (J2000). Those coordinates make it a useful target for observers with a telescope and a star chart or computerized mount. [SIMBAD](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%202440)

Despite the name, a planetary nebula has nothing to do with planets. The term dates to early telescopic observations, when some of these objects looked like small, rounded planetary disks. In fact, a planetary nebula forms when an aging, Sun-like star sheds its outer layers. The exposed stellar core heats the surrounding gas, making it glow. Over time, that gas disperses into space, while the core remains as a white dwarf.

The catalogued dimensions of NGC 2440 are about 0.36 by 0.34 arcminutes—roughly 22 by 20 arcseconds. That is tiny on the sky: a reminder that this is a compact target, not a sprawling cloud. The listed size is a catalog measurement, and the visible boundary can depend on the wavelength and observing conditions. At the eyepiece, patient looking matters. A small, concentrated patch may be easier to recognize than to admire at first glance.

**How to find it:** Start with the coordinates above, using a current sky chart or a telescope’s GoTo system. Its declination places it in the southern celestial sky, but it is accessible from many mid-northern locations as well as the Southern Hemisphere. NGC 2440 is highest when your local sidereal time is close to 7h 42m; check a planetarium app for the best evening window from your site.

Give your eyes time to adjust to the dark, and begin at low power to confirm the field. Then increase magnification—perhaps to around 100×, and higher if the air is steady. Because the nebula is compact, a little more power can help distinguish it from a star, though excessive magnification may make the view dim. Try observing both with and without a narrowband nebula filter, such as a UHC or O III filter; the contrast gain varies with your telescope, filter and sky. Avoid judging the view by vivid images made with long exposures: the eye sees less color in faint objects
