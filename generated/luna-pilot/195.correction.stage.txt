# Reading the Light of NGC 2419

*NGC 2419 is listed in SIMBAD as a globular cluster. Its catalogue measurements offer a useful starting point for finding the object—and for understanding what a number on a star chart can and cannot tell you.*

A sky chart can make a faint object seem simple: a name, a position, a brightness, a small mark on the page. NGC 2419 has all of these, but each describes a different part of the task. SIMBAD gives its position in the sky, identifies it as a globular cluster, and lists an apparent visual magnitude of 10.05 and an angular size of 4.1 arcminutes. Together, those details sketch an observing challenge rather than promise an easy sighting. ([SIMBAD: NGC 2419](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%202419))

For a modern chart, the database gives the cluster’s J2000 equatorial coordinates as right ascension 07h 38m 08.51s and declination +38° 52′ 54.9″. Coordinates are the practical key to locating a target, whether you use a chart, a go-to mount or another navigation method. Check that the chart or equipment is using a compatible coordinate system and epoch; SIMBAD also displays an older FK4 position, which is not identical to the J2000 coordinates.

The magnitude and angular size answer different questions. Magnitude 10.05 is the catalogue’s listed visual brightness; 4.1 arcminutes is the apparent diameter recorded for the cluster. The brightness number alone does not describe how the light is distributed across that apparent area, so it should not be treated as a guarantee of how prominent the object will look in an eyepiece. Actual views depend on the observing setup and conditions. A dark, transparent sky and careful focusing are sensible starting points, while a low-power view can help you examine the field before increasing magnification.

A patient search is often more useful than a hurried one. Find the coordinates first, then compare the surrounding field with a detailed chart. Once you believe you have the right position, pause and look slightly to one side of it as well as directly at it. If the view is uncertain, return to the chart and check the field rather than assuming that a faint patch must be the target. These are observing techniques, not extra catalogue measurements: the SIMBAD entry tells you where to point, but it cannot guarantee what a particular telescope and sky will show.

The record also contains measurements beyond the basics. SIMBAD lists a radial velocity of −20.6 kilometres per second, a proper motion of −0.02 milliarcseconds per year in right ascension and −0.57 in declination, and a parallax of 0.003 milliarcseconds with an uncertainty of 0.017. These figures come from cited studies in the database. The parallax uncertainty is larger than the listed value, so that number should not be mistaken for a precise distance measurement.

NGC 2419’s entry includes several photometric values, too: visual magnitude 10.05, along with infrared K magnitude 7.82 and Sloan g, r, i and z
