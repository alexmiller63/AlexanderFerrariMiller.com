# NGC 4517: The Galaxy Often Mislabeled NGC 4437

*An elongated spiral in Virgo offers a lesson in catalog names—and a rewarding challenge for observers under dark skies.*

Some galaxies arrive with a little baggage. Search for NGC 4517 and you may encounter NGC 4437, a name that can make it seem as if two objects have been confused. The SIMBAD database’s record for NGC 4517 displays NGC 4437 in its basic-data identification and classifies the galaxy as an active galactic nucleus (AGN). That naming tangle is worth sorting out before you point a telescope. ([SIMBAD: NGC 4517](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%204517))

The galaxy lies in Virgo, at J2000 coordinates 12h 32m 45.6s, +00° 06′ 54″. SIMBAD lists its optical dimensions as about 17.8 by 2.3 arcminutes, with a spiral classification. That long, narrow outline suggests a disk seen close to edge-on: instead of looking down onto its spiral arms, we see the galaxy’s broad disk from the side. Its light is stretched across a patch of sky more than half the apparent width of the full Moon, but only a small fraction as tall.

That size can be deceptive at the eyepiece. SIMBAD lists a B-band magnitude of 11.1, but a galaxy’s total brightness is spread across its whole face. NGC 4517 is not a point of light like a star; its glow is diffuse, and the thin disk can be hard to pick out against a bright sky. The catalog’s AGN classification adds another layer of interest. An active galactic nucleus is a galaxy’s unusually energetic central region, generally associated with matter falling toward a massive black hole. The label describes activity in the core, not a promise that the nucleus will look conspicuously bright in a small telescope.

To find the galaxy, center a chart or planetarium app on its J2000 coordinates, then use nearby stars in your finder to guide the final hop. Because it is large on the sky, begin with a low-power eyepiece and a generous field of view; higher magnification may help once you have located the faint streak. Give your eyes time to adapt to darkness, shield nearby lights, and choose a moonless night away from city glow. Averted vision—looking slightly to one side of the target—can make a faint, extended patch easier to detect.

The galaxy is well placed for evening observing during northern spring, when Virgo rises high in the sky. If the view is subtle, that is part of the experience: you are not looking at a bright pinprick, but at a vast disk whose light is diluted across many arcminutes. Keep the NGC 4517/NGC 4437 naming mismatch in mind as you compare charts and databases, and let the elongated shape be your confirmation. A small, hazy slash in the eyepiece is the visible outline of a distant spiral system—and a reminder that catalog names, like galaxies, can have more than one angle.
