# NGC 247: A Faint Ribbon in Cetus

*This broad, low-surface-brightness spiral rewards patient, dark-sky observing more than magnification.*

Some galaxies announce themselves with a bright core. NGC 247 is subtler: a long, faint wash of light in Cetus, best approached with dark-adapted eyes and an unhurried gaze. Its challenge is part of its appeal. Rather than a compact beacon, it is a broad island of stars whose light is spread thinly across the sky.

The galaxy’s position is listed as right ascension 00h 47m 08.6s and declination −20° 45′ 37″. That places it in Cetus, a sprawling constellation in the autumn sky for observers in the Northern Hemisphere. SIMBAD classifies NGC 247 as a low-surface-brightness galaxy and gives its optical dimensions as about 17 by 6 arcminutes—more than half the apparent width of the full Moon, but much dimmer. The catalog also records a spiral classification, SAB(s)d: a late-type spiral with a modest bar and no prominent inner ring. [SIMBAD: NGC 247](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%20247)

That contrast between apparent size and faintness is key to the view. A galaxy’s light may be detectable in total and still be difficult to see when spread over a large area. NGC 247’s classification as low surface brightness signals just such a challenge: its extended glow can blend into a bright or hazy sky. Under dark conditions, however, its elongated shape offers a satisfying target—less a sharp-edged object than a delicate streak emerging from the background.

NGC 247 is also moving away from us. SIMBAD lists a heliocentric radial velocity of about 162 kilometers per second. That figure describes motion along our line of sight; it is not, by itself, a simple distance measure for a nearby galaxy, where local motions also matter. The catalog’s position, structure and apparent size make a more immediate observing story: a large spiral, seen from afar, whose faintness asks the observer to look carefully.

To find it, use a star chart or planetarium app set to the listed coordinates, then scan the surrounding region of Cetus. A low-power eyepiece gives a wider field for locating the galaxy and framing its long axis. From a dark site, try a telescope of moderate aperture; keep magnification modest at first, and use averted vision—looking slightly to the side of the target—to catch the faint outer glow. Let your eyes adapt for at least 20 minutes, shield them from nearby white lights, and choose a moonless night if possible. A transparent sky matters more here than high power.

The view may arrive gradually: first a suggestion of brightness, then a narrow oval, perhaps with a more noticeable central region. Give it time. NGC 247 is not a showpiece that overwhelms the eyepiece; it is a patient observer’s galaxy, a distant spiral that becomes visible by degrees. In Cetus, its faint ribbon reminds us that the night sky is full not only of brilliant points, but also of vast structures whose light asks us to slow down.
