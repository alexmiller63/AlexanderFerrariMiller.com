# OCL 269: A Cluster on Cassiopeia’s Edge

**Dek:** Near the northern sky’s familiar W, this broad open cluster rewards patient sweeping more than high magnification.

Some deep-sky objects announce themselves with a sharp outline. OCL 269, listed in SIMBAD as the open cluster NGC 7789, is subtler: a spread of starlight in Cassiopeia that asks you to slow down and let the field resolve. Its position—right ascension 23h 57m 20.2s, declination +56° 43′ 34″—puts it in a rich northern region of the Milky Way. [SIMBAD’s NGC 7789 entry](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%207789) classifies it as an open cluster.

An open cluster is a gathering of stars associated by their shared origin, rather than a distant galaxy or a compact ball of ancient stars. SIMBAD gives this one an apparent diameter of about 25.3 arcminutes—close to the full Moon’s width on the sky. That breadth is a clue to how to observe it: this is not a target to squeeze into the highest-power eyepiece. Use a wide field, and give your eyes time to notice the small points emerging from the background.

The catalogue also lists a parallax of 0.453 milliarcseconds. Taking the inverse gives a rough distance of about 2,200 parsecs, or 7,200 light-years. That is an estimate from the listed parallax, not a claim that every cluster star sits at precisely the same distance. SIMBAD records a radial velocity of about –55 kilometres per second, measured relative to the Sun. The minus sign means the measured motion along our line of sight is toward us; it does not describe the cluster’s full motion across the sky.

For observing, start with Cassiopeia’s unmistakable W shape and use a current star chart or planetarium app to guide you from its bright stars to the cluster’s coordinates. Because the cluster lies far north, it is well placed for many northern observers, especially on clear autumn evenings. From a dark site, try binoculars first: 7×50 or 10×50 binoculars offer a generous field and can reveal the cluster as a concentrated, grainy patch. A small telescope at low power should make the impression more granular; increase magnification only if the view remains comfortably framed.

SIMBAD lists a V magnitude of 6.7, but flags that photometric value with low quality. In any case, a cluster’s light is spread across its apparent area, so a single brightness number cannot promise a naked-eye sighting. Light pollution, transparency and the observer’s adaptation to darkness matter. Shield nearby lights, avoid looking at a bright phone screen, and let your vision settle for several minutes. If the first view looks like a haze, keep observing: faint stars often appear one by one as your gaze moves gently around the field.

OCL 269’s appeal is not a dramatic silhouette. It is the gradual transformation of a pale patch into a crowd of separate suns—a small, satisfying lesson in what patient observing can uncover.
