# MCG -1-33-13: A Quiet Light in Virgo

*Dek: A compact-looking galaxy with a broad spiral profile, NGC 4699 rewards patient searching under dark spring skies.*

Spring evenings bring Virgo high above the horizon, a sprawling constellation whose best-known treasures are often grouped into the Virgo Cluster. Among its less celebrated sights is MCG -1-33-13, also cataloged as NGC 4699. SIMBAD places it at right ascension 12h 49m and declination −8° 40′, and classifies it as an emission-line galaxy with an SABb spiral form. Its optical span is nearly five arcminutes—about one-sixth the apparent width of the Moon. ([SIMBAD: NGC 4699](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%204699))

That apparent size can be deceptive. A galaxy’s light is spread across its whole disk, so an object that sounds substantial on paper may appear as a faint, soft patch rather than a bright, sharply edged target. NGC 4699’s listed blue magnitude is about 10.8, but that figure collects light over the galaxy’s full extent. Under a bright sky, the glow of its outskirts can vanish into the background.

The designation SABb offers a shorthand for its structure. “SAB” indicates an intermediate or weakly barred spiral, while “b” describes a spiral subtype. Its emission-line classification points to spectral features produced by glowing gas. Such lines help astronomers investigate the physical conditions in a galaxy; they are not a promise that the object will look vivid through an eyepiece. SIMBAD lists a redshift of 0.004673 and a radial velocity near 1,398 kilometres per second. Redshift measures how spectral features are shifted toward longer wavelengths; interpreting a galaxy’s motion also requires care, because local motions complicate a simple conversion into distance. ([SIMBAD data for NGC 4699](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%204699))

For observers north of the equator, the galaxy is accessible, though it stays relatively low from many locations. Its southerly declination means trees, buildings and haze near the horizon can matter more than usual. Look in spring, when Virgo is well placed in the evening sky. Spica, the constellation’s bright star, is a useful landmark: NGC 4699 lies roughly nine degrees northwest of it, within Virgo. A planetarium app or a detailed finder chart can help bridge that gap; the galaxy itself is far too faint to pick out with the unaided eye.

Choose a moonless night and a location with a clear southern view. Let your eyes adapt to darkness, then sweep the charted area with binoculars or a telescope at low power. In a small instrument, expect a faint oval haze, not an obvious spiral pattern. A larger telescope and averted vision—looking slightly to one side of the target—may help reveal its extended glow. Once found, increase the magnification modestly and give your eye time at the eyepiece. The reward is subtle: not a detailed portrait, but the quiet recognition of a far-off galaxy whose light has crossed space to reach your telescope.
