# Collinder 20: A Quiet Gathering in Cassiopeia

*An open cluster in the Queen’s familiar W offers a small-scale lesson in how astronomers turn a patch of stars into a measurable place.*

Cassiopeia’s bright stars draw a W across the northern sky, but the constellation’s quieter sights reward a slower look. Between those familiar points lies Collinder 20, also catalogued as NGC 663: an open cluster whose modest glow marks a much farther gathering of stars.

The SIMBAD astronomical database classifies NGC 663 as an open cluster and gives its J2000 position as right ascension 1 hour 46 minutes, declination +61°12′. Its listed angular diameter is about 18 arcminutes—roughly three-fifths the apparent width of the Moon. SIMBAD also lists a visual magnitude of 7.1, though the entry marks that photometry as uncertain. The number is a useful guide, not a promise of what every observer will see. [SIMBAD: NGC 663](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%20663)

An open cluster is a group of stars associated with one another, rather than a chance pattern scattered across the whole sky. Distance helps give this particular group its scale. SIMBAD lists a parallax of 0.320 milliarcseconds. Parallax is the tiny apparent shift in a star’s position as Earth moves around the Sun; the smaller the measured shift, the farther away the object. A simple conversion puts 0.320 milliarcseconds at about 3,100 parsecs, or roughly 10,000 light-years. That is a rounded distance inferred from the listed parallax, not a ruler-straight measurement.

The same database entry records a radial velocity of −32.3 kilometres per second. Radial velocity describes motion toward or away from us along our line of sight; the minus sign here indicates motion toward the Solar System under the usual convention. The cluster’s listed proper motion—the apparent movement across the sky—is small, too. These numbers are not visible at the eyepiece. They are the quiet measurements behind the view: position, distance and motion distilled from light and careful comparison.

**Observing guide:** Look for Cassiopeia high in the northern sky during the autumn and winter evenings, when the W-shaped constellation is well placed for many northern observers. Use a current star chart or planetarium app to locate NGC 663 at 1h 46m, +61°12′; the cluster lies in the constellation’s rich Milky Way field.
