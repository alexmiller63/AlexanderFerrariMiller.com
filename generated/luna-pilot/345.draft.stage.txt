# Ptolemy’s Cluster: A Southern-Sky Gathering

*An ancient-looking patch of stars gets a modern measure: NGC 6475’s coordinates, motion and distance point observers toward a rich field in Scorpius.*

There is a particular pleasure in finding a star cluster before reaching for a telescope: first a small brightening among the stars, then a gathering that resolves into points. Ptolemy’s Cluster—catalogued as NGC 6475—is an open cluster, a group of stars associated in space rather than a distant galaxy or a cloud of glowing gas. The SIMBAD astronomical database identifies NGC 6475 as an open cluster and gives its position in the southern sky: right ascension 17h 53m 47.3s, declination −34° 50′ 28″. [SIMBAD: NGC 6475](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%206475)

Those coordinates make the cluster’s visibility strongly dependent on where you observe from. At latitude 35° south, it passes almost overhead. At 40° north, it reaches only about 15° above the southern horizon at its highest point, where haze and buildings can easily interfere. The declination is the key: a negative value places the target south of the celestial equator. Choose a site with a clear southern horizon, and observe when the cluster is high for your location.

SIMBAD lists a parallax of 3.5704 milliarcseconds. A simple inversion of that value puts the cluster roughly 280 parsecs away—about 910 light-years. This is an estimate from the parallax, not a claim that every star in the field has been individually weighed or placed at precisely the same distance. The database also lists a radial velocity of −14.7 kilometres per second and a proper motion of about 3.1 milliarcseconds per year eastward and 5.3 milliarcseconds per year southward. Together, such measurements help astronomers distinguish a moving stellar group from stars that merely overlap it by chance in our view.

For a field observer, the numbers are a map rather than a test. Use a current star chart or planetarium app to find Scorpius, then search near the cluster’s listed position. The right ascension is close to 18 hours; the best time to look is when that region of the sky is crossing your local meridian. Let your eyes adapt to the dark, and begin with unaided vision or binoculars. A low-power view is a good first choice: it keeps the wider star field together, while higher magnification can spread a cluster across the edges of the eyepiece.

If you are observing from northern latitudes, don’t wait until the target is slipping toward the horizon. Check its rise and transit times for your location, and look when it is highest. From farther south, the cluster’s steep climb makes it easier to observe for longer. In either case, give yourself a moment to look without equipment before switching to optics. The contrast between a faint sprinkling and a resolved collection of stars is part of the experience.

The catalogue’s precise coordinates and measured motion belong to modern astronomy; the pleasure of recognizing a cluster belongs to the observer. Ptolemy’s Cluster offers both: a target you can locate by its position, and a reminder that stars close together in the sky can also share a history in space.
