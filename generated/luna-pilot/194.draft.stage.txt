# MCG 11-10-7: A Quiet Spiral in the Northern Sky

*NGC 2403 is a nearby-looking island of stars whose catalog numbers point the way to a rewarding night-sky target.*

The name MCG 11-10-7 has the clipped, coded sound of a radio call sign. But it belongs to a vast spiral galaxy: the object also catalogued as NGC 2403, in the northern constellation Camelopardalis. Its light reaches us as a soft, extended glow, carrying the structure of a galaxy far beyond the Milky Way.

The SIMBAD database lists NGC 2403 as an SAcd galaxy—a spiral system without a prominent central bar, with relatively loosely wound arms—and gives its optical span as about 10.2 by 5.9 arcminutes. That is a broad patch on the sky, several times the apparent diameter of the full Moon, though the galaxy’s light is spread thinly across it. The same record gives a heliocentric radial velocity of about 130 kilometres per second. That figure describes motion along our line of sight; for a nearby galaxy, local gravitational motions matter, so the number should not be treated as a simple measure of distance. [SIMBAD: NGC 2403](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%202403)

There is something fitting about encountering a galaxy through a catalogue alias. Astronomers assign objects many names as surveys and observing programs build different maps of the sky. The labels vary; the target does not. In this case, the coordinates in SIMBAD—right ascension 7h 36m 51s and declination +65° 36′ (J2000)—offer a precise address for finding the same faint spiral under any of its names.

For observing, choose a dark site and let your eyes adapt for at least 20 minutes. From much of the Northern Hemisphere, NGC 2403 is high enough to be a practical target; above roughly 25° north latitude, its declination makes it circumpolar, meaning it never sets. Use a current chart or planetarium app to locate the coordinates, then sweep the area with binoculars or a low-power telescope. A small instrument may show a diffuse oval haze rather than a sharply defined disk. Increase magnification only if the view remains bright and steady: the galaxy’s low surface brightness means a darker sky can help more than extra power.

Try averted vision—look slightly to one side of the galaxy rather than straight at it. This can make faint, extended light easier to detect. Under excellent conditions, a larger telescope may reveal uneven texture across the disk, but don’t expect a photograph’s crisp spiral arms in the eyepiece. Our eyes gather light differently from a camera, and the galaxy’s details are subtle.
