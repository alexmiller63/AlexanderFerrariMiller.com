# The Beehive Cluster: a skyful of suns

*In Cancer, a broad, loose gathering of stars rewards binoculars—and a patient look at the dark between them.*

On a clear evening, the Beehive Cluster does not announce itself with a sharp point of light. It arrives as a faint, soft patch in Cancer, a shimmer that can be mistaken for a thin cloud until the stars resolve. Look a little longer, and the patch becomes a crowd: dozens of tiny lights scattered across a generous sweep of sky.

The cataloged object is NGC 2632, classified as an open cluster. SIMBAD gives its position as right ascension 8h 40m 13s, declination +19° 37′ 16″, and lists an apparent angular size of about 118 arcminutes—almost two degrees across. That is roughly four full-Moon widths, so the cluster’s scale is part of the spectacle. It is not a tight knot meant to be squeezed into a high-power view; it is a neighborhood of stars best appreciated with room around it. [SIMBAD: NGC 2632](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%202632)

An open cluster is a loose grouping of stars associated with one another, rather than a distant galaxy or a single star. The Beehive’s apparent breadth has a simple observing consequence: magnification can make the view feel smaller, not grander. Binoculars or a low-power telescope are good companions. If your instrument has a wide field, use it; if not, scan slowly across the cluster and let the stars come into view one by one.

To find it, first locate Cancer, the faint constellation between Gemini and Leo. From northern mid-latitudes, Cancer is well placed for evening observing in late winter and spring. A star chart or planetarium app can help you identify the constellation before you search for the cluster itself.
