# Cor Caroli: A bright point with a peculiar spectrum

*In Canes Venatici, this easy-to-spot star offers a reminder that an apparently steady point of light can hold a more unusual story in its spectrum.*

On a clear spring evening, look north for the Big Dipper. Its familiar outline makes a useful starting point for exploring the smaller constellation Canes Venatici, the Hunting Dogs, where Cor Caroli shines as a bright naked-eye star. You don’t need a telescope to find it; a star chart or planetarium app can help you trace the constellation and confirm the identification.

The catalog entry for Cor Caroli’s brighter named component identifies it as **Alpha² Canum Venaticorum**, or Alpha² CVn. Its coordinates are right ascension 12h 56m and declination +38° 19′ (J2000), useful for entering into a digital sky map. SIMBAD lists its visual magnitude as 2.88: bright enough to stand out under a dark sky, though moonlight, haze and city lighting can make it harder to pick out. [SIMBAD: HIP 63125](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=HIP%2063125)

Give your eyes a few minutes to adapt to the darkness. Then scan the region with unaided vision; binoculars can help you distinguish the surrounding star field, but they aren’t necessary for the star itself. If you’re observing from mid-northern latitudes, spring evenings are a convenient time to look. Use a current sky chart for your location and date, since the stars’ positions shift across the sky through the night and across the seasons.

Cor Caroli’s brightness is only part of the interest. SIMBAD classifies Alpha² CVn as a variable star and gives it the spectral type **A0VpSiEu**. In that notation, A0 describes a hot, A-type star; “p” marks a peculiar spectrum, while “Si” and “Eu” flag silicon and europium features. Those details are found by studying starlight with instruments that separate it into wavelengths—not by looking through an ordinary backyard telescope. To the eye, Cor Caroli appears as a point of light. Its spectrum, however, carries clues to a more distinctive stellar atmosphere.

The catalog’s parallax measurement is 32.7 milliarcseconds. Parallax is the apparent shift of a nearby star against more distant background stars as Earth moves around the Sun.
