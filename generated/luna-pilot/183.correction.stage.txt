# NGC 2194: Reading a Cluster in the Catalog

*The measurements for this open cluster tell a story the eye alone cannot: where it appears, how it shifts, and how its motion is recorded.*

NGC 2194 is easy to describe by its position: right ascension 6 hours, 13 minutes, 45.6 seconds; declination +12 degrees, 48 minutes, 47 seconds. Those coordinates, given for the J2000 reference epoch, place the object at a fixed address on the celestial map. But a catalog entry is more than an address. It is a set of measurements, each describing a different aspect of the object.

SIMBAD classifies NGC 2194 as an open cluster and lists its angular size as 9 by 9 arcminutes. An arcminute is one-sixtieth of a degree; the full Moon spans about 30 arcminutes across. By that comparison, the cluster’s listed diameter is about a third of the Moon’s apparent width. That figure describes how large the object appears on the sky, not its physical size. The apparent width is a two-dimensional measure of a distant object viewed from Earth.

The same distinction matters for distance. SIMBAD lists a parallax of 0.263 milliarcseconds, with an uncertainty of 0.003. Parallax is the tiny apparent shift in an object’s position measured from different viewpoints. For a distant object, that shift is small; in NGC 2194’s case, the cataloged value is less than one thousandth of an arcsecond. It is not something an observer would see simply by looking at the cluster. It is a measurement that helps place the object in three-dimensional space.

The catalog also records motion across the sky. Its proper motion is 0.535 milliarcseconds per year in right ascension, after accounting for declination, and −1.468 milliarcseconds per year in declination. These values describe the change in the cluster’s apparent position over time. The numbers are minute, but they show that the catalog’s portrait is not simply a static point on a map.

A separate measurement concerns motion along the line of sight. SIMBAD lists NGC 2194’s radial velocity as 8.559 kilometers per second, with an uncertainty of 0.535 kilometers per second. Proper motion and radial velocity describe different components of motion: one across the sky, the other toward or away from the observer. Together, they make the catalog entry more than a set of coordinates, even though neither measurement is apparent in an ordinary view.

The precision of these figures should not be confused with a complete account of the cluster. SIMBAD identifies the object’s type and compiles its position, size, parallax and motions, but the measurements do not, by themselves, describe every detail of its stars or history. They are separate clues, with different units and uncertainties, gathered into one record.

That is part of the appeal of NGC 2194 as a subject: a modest angular footprint can be paired with measurements of position and motion that reach beyond appearance. The catalog turns a name and a patch of sky into a measurable object—one whose place and movement can be expressed, even when the numbers are far too small to notice by eye.
