# Mizar, the Handle’s Hidden Company

*An ordinary-looking star in the Big Dipper offers two kinds of double vision—and a reminder that even a point of light can be a crowd.*

Mizar sits in the handle of the Big Dipper, where the familiar pattern seems to invite a quick glance and then send the eye onward. Stay a little longer. Beside it, a fainter star called Alcor makes a naked-eye pair; in a telescope, Mizar itself divides into a close pair. One patch of sky, two different tests of vision.

Find the Big Dipper high in the northern sky on a spring evening, or follow it around the North Star at another season. Mizar is the star roughly midway along the handle, near Alcor. Give your eyes a minute to settle, then look just beside Mizar rather than staring directly at it. Under a reasonably dark sky, many observers can distinguish Alcor without optical aid. If the two blur together, try binoculars: they should make the neighboring points easier to separate.

Now turn a small telescope toward Mizar. Use low or moderate magnification and let the image steady; the close pair should appear as two points rather than one. The experience is a useful lesson in scale. Alcor is the wider apparent neighbor, easily noticed by eye under favorable conditions. Mizar’s pair is much tighter and asks more of your instrument and the air above you.

The star has another identity in astronomical catalogues: **Zeta Ursae Majoris**, or ζ UMa. SIMBAD classifies it as a “double or multiple star.” Its entry gives a parallax of 38.01 milliarcseconds. Parallax is the tiny apparent shift in a star’s position as Earth moves around the Sun; using that measurement gives a rough distance of about 86 light-years.
