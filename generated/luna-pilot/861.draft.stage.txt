# Acubens, the Quiet Claw of Cancer

*At the edge of naked-eye visibility, Alpha Cancri offers a lesson in how a steady point of light can carry a surprisingly detailed stellar record.*

On a clear spring evening, Cancer can seem like a pause between the brighter constellations Gemini and Leo. Its stars are modest, and Acubens is no exception: away from city glare, it is a small, steady point for the unaided eye. But this faint-looking star has a name that anchors it in the Crab—and modern measurements that give the point of light depth and motion.

Acubens is Alpha Cancri, also catalogued as HIP 44066. The SIMBAD astronomical database lists its visual magnitude as 4.249, a brightness that makes it visible to the naked eye under dark skies but easy to lose in light pollution. Binoculars can help bring it out, though they won’t transform it into a showpiece. The pleasure is in finding it: Cancer lies between Gemini and Leo, and a current sky chart can guide you from their more conspicuous stars to the Crab’s subtler pattern. [SIMBAD’s Acubens record](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=HIP%2044066)

For a closer look at where to search, SIMBAD gives Acubens’s J2000 coordinates as right ascension 8 hours 58 minutes and declination +11° 51′. Coordinates are the sky’s version of longitude and latitude; the J2000 label marks the reference epoch, so a modern star chart or planetarium app is useful for locating the star in tonight’s sky. From mid-northern latitudes, Cancer climbs comfortably above the horizon in the evening during spring.

The more surprising part of Acubens’s story is its distance. SIMBAD lists a parallax of 18.3304 milliarcseconds. Parallax is the apparent shift of a nearby star against more distant background stars as Earth travels around the Sun. Converting that measurement gives a distance of about 55 parsecs, or roughly 178 light-years. The star we see tonight has been travelling through space for generations before its light reaches us.

Acubens is not fixed in the Crab’s outline, either. Its listed proper motion—about 42 milliarcseconds per year eastward in right ascension and 31 milliarcseconds per year southward in declination—amounts to a shift of roughly five arcseconds in a century. That’s far too small to notice from one night to the next, but careful measurements across decades can reveal it. SIMBAD also reports a radial velocity of –14.79 kilometres per second: a measurement of motion along our line of sight, with the negative sign indicating approach.

Its spectrum resists a simple label. SIMBAD records the classification “kA7VmF0/2III/IVSr,” a reminder that stellar types can encode more complexity than a single letter-and-number shorthand suggests. Even without unpacking every part of that classification, it points to another way astronomers study stars: not just by their visible brightness, but by the pattern of light spread into a spectrum.

For tonight, though, keep the observing simple. Choose a dark location, give your eyes time to adapt, and use a chart to trace Cancer between its brighter neighbours. Once Acubens comes into view, pause on it without magnification. That quiet glimmer is enough to begin with: a star in the Crab, measured from Earth as a moving point of light, shining across about 178 light-years of space.
