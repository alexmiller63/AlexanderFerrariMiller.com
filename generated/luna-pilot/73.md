# Antares: The Red Lantern at the Heart of Scorpius

*An orange-red supergiant anchors the Scorpion’s familiar summer pattern. Find it after dusk, then pause over a star whose calm appearance conceals an immense scale.*

As twilight drains from the sky, Scorpius rises in the south for observers in the Northern Hemisphere. Its stars trace a hooked body and curling tail; near the middle burns Antares, a bright ember marking the Scorpion’s heart. The name is easy to find once the pattern takes shape: look for the conspicuously ruddy star among the blue-white points of the Milky Way.

Antares is Alpha Scorpii, a red supergiant. SIMBAD lists its spectral type as M1.5Iab, alongside a B2V companion in the system’s combined classification. In other words, the reddish point is not a small, cool ember but a star in a late, expanded phase of stellar evolution. Its apparent steadiness to the unaided eye is no measure of its physical scale. At a distance, even a giant star is reduced to a point of light.

The catalogue places Antares at right ascension 16 hours, 29 minutes, 24 seconds and declination −26 degrees, 25 minutes, 55 seconds (J2000). Its listed visual magnitude is 0.91—bright enough to stand out in a dark sky, though its low altitude for many northern observers can soften its brilliance. The air near the horizon is thicker and more turbulent than overhead; Antares may shimmer, dim, or flicker through shifting colors. That restless appearance is mostly our atmosphere at work, not a sudden change in the star.

**The observing Almanack:** In the Northern Hemisphere, try on a clear evening from late spring into summer, when Scorpius is best placed after sunset. Face south and search for the curved pattern of the Scorpion. Antares sits in its central body, above the tail’s bend. In the Southern Hemisphere, Scorpius climbs higher and can appear especially striking. The exact viewing hour depends on your location and date, so give the constellation time to clear rooftops, trees, or a hazy horizon.

No telescope is needed. Start with the naked eye, letting your vision adjust to darkness, and compare Antares’ warm hue with nearby stars. Binoculars can sharpen the surrounding star field, but the pleasure is in the larger pattern: the reddish beacon set against the crowded glow of the Milky Way. If the star seems to pulse or change color, watch for a few minutes. Atmospheric turbulence often makes bright stars twinkle more dramatically when they sit low.

Then look again at the simple point of light. The catalogue’s coordinates and spectral label are precise, but the view is personal: a star appearing above a neighbor’s roof, over a dark field, or between passing clouds. Antares gives the Scorpion a center, and the summer sky a memorable landmark. You do not need to resolve its distant companion or measure its brightness to observe it well. Find the red star, recognize the pattern, and let your eyes linger.
