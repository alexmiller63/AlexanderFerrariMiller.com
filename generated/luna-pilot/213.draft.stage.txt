# MCG 6-22-13: A Thin Spiral in the Spring Sky

*An extended galaxy offers a lesson in cosmic scale—and a patient challenge for backyard observers.*

Some galaxies announce themselves with bright, unmistakable cores. MCG 6-22-13 is more of a quiet invitation: a broad, slender island of stars whose light has traveled across intergalactic space to reach us. Cataloged as NGC 3003, it lies in Leo Minor, at about right ascension 9h 48m and declination +33° 25′. Its position is well placed for northern observers during spring evenings. [SIMBAD: NGC 3003](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%203003)

The numbers hint at a galaxy with room to spare. Its listed optical dimensions are roughly 6.0 by 1.6 arcminutes—about one-fifth the Moon’s apparent width, but much narrower. A cataloged radial velocity of about 1,488 kilometers per second places it among the galaxies whose recession is measured through the Doppler shift of their light. Applying a simple Hubble-flow estimate gives a distance on the order of 21 megaparsecs, or roughly 70 million light-years. That is an estimate, not a direct distance measurement: at such comparatively nearby cosmic scales, a galaxy’s own motion can complicate the calculation.

SIMBAD classifies NGC 3003 as an emission-line galaxy and lists its morphology as SAB, a category associated with an intermediate or weakly barred spiral form. Emission lines are specific wavelengths made especially prominent when atoms and ions emit light; their presence is a clue from a galaxy’s spectrum, not a promise that those lines will be visible through an eyepiece. The same database lists a blue magnitude of 12.3. Because the galaxy’s light is spread over a large area, that figure can make it sound easier to see than it is.

For an observing plan, start with the coordinates rather than the name: nearby galaxies often have several catalog designations, and a chart or planetarium app can help confirm the field. In spring, look high in the northern sky for Leo Minor, the small constellation tucked between Leo and Ursa Major. A telescope with a few inches of aperture under a dark, transparent sky is a sensible starting point; larger aperture and darker skies improve the odds. Give your eyes time to adapt, and use averted vision—looking slightly to one side of the target can make a faint glow easier to detect.

Begin at low or moderate magnification to locate the field, then try a little more power if the sky is steady. Don’t expect a bright spiral pattern
