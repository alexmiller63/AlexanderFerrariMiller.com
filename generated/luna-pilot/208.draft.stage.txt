# The UFO Galaxy: A Cosmic Craft in Lynx

*NGC 2683 is no spacecraft, but its slim, tilted profile gives this distant spiral an unforgettable silhouette.*

The UFO Galaxy glides across the spring sky—not through space, but through the imagination. Its catalog name is NGC 2683, and its elongated shape can suggest a saucer seen from the side. The resemblance comes from perspective: we see the galaxy’s broad disk at a steep angle, so its spiral structure is compressed into a narrow streak of light.

NGC 2683 lies in Lynx, a faint northern constellation between brighter star patterns. Its position is about 8 hours 52 minutes right ascension and +33 degrees 25 minutes declination. In its catalog entry, SIMBAD lists an optical size of 8.71 by 2.57 arcminutes and a morphological classification of SA, indicating a spiral galaxy. That long-to-short dimension captures the object’s strikingly stretched appearance. [SIMBAD: NGC 2683](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%202683)

The name “UFO Galaxy” is a nickname, not a scientific designation. The formal name NGC 2683 comes from the New General Catalogue, a catalog of nebulae and star clusters compiled in the nineteenth century. Seen nearly edge-on, a spiral galaxy’s disk becomes a thin oval; its stars and gas are spread across an enormous system, while the viewing angle hides much of its face-on structure. In a face-on spiral, we might trace sweeping arms. Here, the disk’s narrow outline takes center stage.

There is more to the galaxy than its silhouette. SIMBAD’s entry identifies NGC 2683 as an active-galaxy-nucleus object, and gives a spectroscopic redshift of 0.001372. These catalog labels describe measurements and classifications, not what an observer will necessarily see through an eyepiece. The listed redshift is small, and turning it into a reliable distance estimate would require care: a nearby galaxy’s motion relative to us can complicate a simple calculation from redshift alone. [SIMBAD: NGC 2683](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%202683)

**How to observe it:** Look for Lynx on clear evenings in the Northern Hemisphere’s late-winter and spring season. The constellation has few bright stars, so use a star chart or planetarium app and search for NGC 2683 by its coordinates. A telescope is the practical choice; under a dark sky, try a 4- to 6-inch instrument or larger. Begin at low power, around 30–50×, to locate the galaxy, then increase magnification
