# Arp 78: A Spiral with Company

*In Aries, a modest patch of light marks a distant galaxy—and a reminder that even cosmic neighbors can matter.*

Look toward Aries and you are looking past the foreground stars, toward a galaxy catalogued as NGC 772 and known as Arp 78. Its light arrives from far beyond the Milky Way, carrying a quiet clue about its surroundings: astronomical databases classify it as a galaxy in a pair. That small note turns a spiral into a story about company.

The catalogue gives Arp 78 a measured optical span of about five by three and a half arcminutes—roughly one-sixth the apparent width of the full Moon. It classifies the galaxy as an unbarred spiral, and lists a visual magnitude of 10.31. Those numbers describe a target that is not a naked-eye sight, but is within reach of amateur telescopes under suitable skies. [SIMBAD: NGC 772](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%20772)

A galaxy’s shape is more than decoration. Stars, gas and dust orbit within a gravitational system; nearby galaxies can tug on one another, shifting material and disturbing orderly patterns. The SIMBAD entry’s “pair of galaxies” classification makes the neighborhood part of the picture, though that label alone does not tell us the full history of any encounter. What it does offer is a useful observing thought: a spiral is not isolated simply because it looks like a single patch of light.

The measurements also give observers a reliable way to find the target. SIMBAD lists coordinates of right ascension 1 hour 59 minutes 19.6 seconds and declination +19 degrees 00 minutes 27 seconds (J2000). Those coordinates place it in Aries. Use a current star chart or planetarium app to locate the field; the galaxy itself will not stand
