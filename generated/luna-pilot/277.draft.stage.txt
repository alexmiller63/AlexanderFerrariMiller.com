# M89: A Quiet Light in Virgo

*An unassuming galaxy with a complicated identity rewards patient spring observing.*

On a clear spring night, Virgo offers a field rich in galaxies. Among them, M89 is easy to overlook: not a bright star or a showpiece nebula, but a small, softly glowing oval. Its understated appearance belies a layered identity. The galaxy is also catalogued as NGC 4552, and SIMBAD lists it as an S0 galaxy and a Seyfert 2 galaxy. [SIMBAD](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%204552)

S0, or lenticular, is a galaxy classification between elliptical and spiral. In a telescope, M89’s light gathers into a smooth-looking patch, without the conspicuous spiral pattern that might make a galaxy immediately recognizable. Its other label points to activity in its center: Seyfert galaxies have unusually energetic nuclei. The catalogue classification adds a hint of drama to what the eyepiece presents as a subdued glow.

That glow is spread across a patch of sky roughly four arcminutes wide. SIMBAD lists a visual magnitude of 9.75, but a galaxy’s light is diffuse: it can be harder to see than a star of similar listed brightness. A dark sky and averted vision—the trick of looking just beside an object rather than straight at it—can help the faint oval emerge.

**The spring search**

M89 lies in Virgo, a constellation well placed for evening observing in spring for observers in the Northern Hemisphere. A star chart or planetarium app is the most reliable way to find it. Set the chart to the J2000 coordinates listed by SIMBAD: right ascension 12h 35m 39.8s, declination +12° 33′ 23″. These coordinates are a map address, not a promise that the galaxy will be visible from every location; local sky brightness and the object’s altitude matter.

A telescope is needed: M89 is not a naked-eye target. Under a dark sky, a small telescope may show it as a faint, roundish haze. Increase the magnification only if the image remains steady and the galaxy is still visible; a wider field can make it easier to orient yourself among nearby stars and galaxies. Give your eyes time to adapt to darkness, and shield them from white lights. If M89 does not appear at once, sweep slowly around the charted position rather than staring directly at one point.

The reward is not a dramatic burst of detail. It is the moment a diffuse patch settles into view and becomes more than a mark on a chart: a distant galaxy with a smooth S0 form and a catalogue classification that hints at activity deep in its core. M89 asks for patience, not spectacle—and gives the spring sky a quieter kind of grandeur.
