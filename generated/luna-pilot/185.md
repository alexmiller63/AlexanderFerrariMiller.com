# NGC 2244: A Cluster Measured in Starlight

*An open cluster with a broad apparent span invites a wide-field view—and offers a way to turn a patch of sky into a map of three-dimensional space.*

Some celestial landmarks announce themselves as shapes: a crescent, a ring, a familiar constellation. NGC 2244 offers a different starting point: a set of measurements. SIMBAD identifies it as an open cluster and lists its position as right ascension 6h 32m 10.8s and declination +4° 54′ 50″, in the J2000 coordinate system. Those numbers are a precise address in the sky. A finder chart can help translate them into a route among the stars. ([SIMBAD: NGC 2244](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%202244))

A catalogue measurement also lets us estimate how far away the cluster is. SIMBAD lists its parallax as 0.617 milliarcseconds, with a reported uncertainty of 0.006 milliarcseconds. Parallax is the apparent shift of a nearby object against more distant background stars as Earth changes position in its orbit. Taking the reciprocal of the listed parallax gives about 1,620 parsecs. Using roughly 3.26 light-years per parsec, that works out to about 5,300 light-years.

This is a calculation from the catalogue value, not a distance directly stated on the SIMBAD page. It depends on treating the parallax as a guide to distance; the small reported measurement uncertainty does not, by itself, capture every possible limitation in interpreting a cluster’s distance. Even as an estimate, it gives a striking sense of scale: the stars’ apparent arrangement on the sky lies thousands of light-years away.

SIMBAD also lists NGC 2244’s angular size as 88 by 88 arcminutes—nearly a degree and a half across. That makes it a broad target on the sky, though an angular-size entry should not be mistaken for a sharply defined edge. If we combine the listed span with the rough distance above, the corresponding physical scale is about 42 parsecs, or 135 light-years. This is a geometric estimate, not a claim that every star inside that span belongs to the cluster or that the cluster ends at a precise boundary.

For an observing session, begin with a sky chart and use NGC 2244’s coordinates to check the location. Binoculars provide a wide field that can make it easier to take in a broad patch of sky at once. If you use a telescope, starting at low magnification is a practical way to preserve more of that field. Give your eyes time to adjust to the dark, and choose a location away from bright lights when possible. If the cluster is difficult to identify, return to the chart and check the instrument’s focus and pointing rather than assuming that a catalogue position will look immediately obvious.

The process of locating NGC 2244 is part of the appeal. First, there is the patch of points in the eyepiece. Then there is the catalogue entry: a position, a parallax and an angular span, each describing a different aspect of the same object. The coordinates tell us where to look; the parallax offers a way to estimate how far away it is; and the angular size connects its apparent breadth to a possible physical scale.

A small number of measurements can therefore change how a star field feels. What begins as a flat pattern becomes a distant object with measurable depth and extent—not because the eye alone reveals those dimensions, but because observation and calculation work together.
