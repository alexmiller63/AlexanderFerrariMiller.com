# Kochab, the North’s Other Beacon

*This orange giant is not Polaris—but on clear nights, it helps give the Little Dipper its shape.*

A familiar mistake is to look at the Little Dipper and assume its brightest star must be Polaris. Look instead toward the bowl, where Kochab shines as a steady point of light. Polaris marks the end of the dipper’s handle; Kochab belongs to the bowl. The distinction matters: one star is famous for pointing near the north celestial pole, while the other is a bright, ancient-seeming landmark nearby.

Kochab is cataloged as Beta Ursae Minoris, in the constellation Ursa Minor. Its measured parallax—an apparent shift caused by Earth’s changing viewpoint as it orbits the Sun—is 24.91 milliarcseconds. That corresponds to a distance of about 40 parsecs, or 131 light-years. Its listed visual magnitude is 2.08, bright enough to see without binoculars under a reasonably dark sky. The star’s spectral classification, K4 III, identifies it as a cool, orange-hued giant: a star that has expanded beyond the main sequence, the long phase in which stars like the Sun spend most of their lives steadily fusing hydrogen in their cores.

Those catalog numbers become something more personal under the sky. Kochab is not a pinpoint of color through the unaided eye, and city lights can mute the smaller stars that outline Ursa Minor. But it is a useful anchor. Find Polaris first, then look for the Little Dipper’s compact bowl. Kochab is one of its conspicuous stars; the nearby Pherkad helps complete the bowl’s outer edge. If the faint dipper stars vanish in haze or light pollution, binoculars can bring the pattern back into view.

**When to look:** For observers north of roughly 16 degrees latitude, Kochab stays above the horizon all night, circling the northern sky as Earth turns. Its height changes with your location: the farther north you are, the higher it rides. In the evening, face north and give your eyes time to adjust to the dark. A star chart app can help you identify the dipper, but dim your screen—or switch it off once you have found the pattern.

Kochab’s presence also offers a quiet lesson in scale. Its light began its journey more than a century ago, long before it reached your eyes. And the “giant” in its classification describes a stage in stellar evolution, not an object that can be seen as a disk from Earth. Even in a telescope, Kochab remains a point of light to ordinary observers; its character is read from the light it sends across space.
