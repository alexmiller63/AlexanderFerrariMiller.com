# Dschubba at the Scorpion’s Brow

*Look south for a bright blue-white star in Scorpius—and a binary whose companion is hidden in its light.*

As twilight deepens, Scorpius climbs into view, its curved tail arcing toward the horizon. At the other end of the constellation, among the stars that mark the creature’s head, Dschubba shines like a pinprick of pale light. Its name belongs to Delta Scorpii, a star cataloged by SIMBAD as a spectroscopic binary. [SIMBAD: HIP 78401 / Delta Scorpii](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=HIP%2078401)

Start your search with Antares, the conspicuous reddish star in Scorpius. From there, trace the constellation upward toward its head: Dschubba is one of the bright points in that compact pattern. The contrast between Antares’ ruddy color and Dschubba’s blue-white glow is striking when both are above the haze near the horizon. In the Northern Hemisphere, Scorpius is a summer constellation, though it stays low in the southern sky for many observers. Farther south, it rises higher.

SIMBAD lists Dschubba’s spectral type as B0.3IV. The “B” class identifies a hot, blue-white star; “IV” indicates a subgiant luminosity class. The same database lists its visual magnitude as 2.32—bright enough to see without binoculars under a reasonably dark sky. That catalog figure is a reference, not a promise of exactly how bright the star will look on a particular night.

“Spectroscopic binary” means the star’s light carries evidence of two stars in orbit, even though an ordinary observer cannot split them into separate points. Astronomers detect such systems by studying changes in the stars’ spectra, including shifts in their lines. To the unaided eye, Dschubba remains a single, steady-looking point. Its hidden companion is a reminder that a sharp-looking star can be a whole system.

For a useful observing session, wait until Scorpius has cleared the horizon and choose a spot with an unobstructed view south. Give your eyes 15 to 20 minutes to adapt to the dark, and keep bright phone screens dim or covered. First find Antares, then use a printed star chart or a planetarium app to pick out the head. Binoculars can make the surrounding star pattern easier to recognize, but you do not need optical aid to see Dschubba itself.
