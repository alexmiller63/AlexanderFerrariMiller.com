# Aludra, the Blue Flame at the Hound’s Tail

*Look beyond Sirius on a clear night and find a bright blue supergiant marking the far end of Canis Major.*

Canis Major has a star that steals the show: Sirius, the brightest star in the night sky. But follow the Great Dog’s outline away from Sirius and you’ll find Aludra, Eta Canis Majoris, shining at the tail. It is not as dazzling as its famous companion, yet it offers its own reward: a bright, blue-white point that marks the dog’s far edge.

Aludra is a blue supergiant. SIMBAD classifies it as B5Ia: “B” identifies its spectral type, associated with hot, blue stars, while “Ia” denotes a luminous supergiant. That classification hints at a star very different from the Sun, even though both appear to us as points of light. Aludra’s recorded visual magnitude is about 2.45—bright enough to see without optical aid under a reasonably dark sky. Its measured position is right ascension 7 hours 24 minutes and declination 29 degrees south, placing it in the southern sky. ([SIMBAD entry for HIP 35904](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=HIP%2035904))

The star’s name is a small invitation to look closely. In the familiar outline of Canis Major, Sirius gleams toward the front of the dog, while Aludra belongs at the opposite end. The constellation is often drawn as a four-legged hound; its lines are not written across the sky, of course, but suggested by the spacing between stars. Finding Aludra is a way of seeing the whole figure rather than stopping at its brightest point.

For observers at mid-northern latitudes, Canis Major is a winter constellation. Look for it in the evening sky during the season, low toward the south; the farther north you are, the lower the dog will ride. From the Southern Hemisphere, the constellation appears higher during summer evenings. Your local sky and horizon matter: buildings, trees or haze can hide a star that is otherwise easy to see.

To find Aludra, first locate Sirius. It is exceptionally bright and hard to mistake. Then use a star chart or a sky-viewing app to trace Canis Major away from Sirius toward the constellation’s tail. Aludra lies at the far end of that pattern. Give your eyes several minutes to adapt to darkness, and choose a spot with a clear view toward the southern sky. If light pollution washes out the fainter stars that shape the dog, binoculars can help you pick out the surrounding pattern—but take them away to check that Aludra itself is visible to the naked eye.

A steady point of light is all you should expect. Even through binoculars, Aludra will not resolve into a disk; stars are too distant for ordinary observing equipment to show their surfaces. The pleasure is in connecting that tiny blue-white glimmer to its stellar identity: a hot, luminous supergiant, set far beyond the familiar geometry we draw around the Great Dog.
