# Castor: Gemini’s Quiet Pairing

*An apparently single winter beacon becomes a lesson in stellar multiplicity—and in the patience of looking closely.*

On a clear evening, Castor looks like one bright point in Gemini. The eye gathers its light and moves on. A telescope asks a different question: is that point really one star?

Astronomers classify Castor as a double or multiple star. SIMBAD lists its spectral type as A1V+A2Vm, describing two A-type stars on the main sequence, where stars spend most of their lives fusing hydrogen in their cores. The catalogue gives the system a visual magnitude of 1.58—bright enough to stand out without optical aid—and a parallax of 64.12 milliarcseconds. That parallax corresponds to a distance of roughly 51 light-years. [SIMBAD: Castor](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=HIP%2036850)

For the observer, the first task is finding Gemini. Castor marks one of the constellation’s two bright “twin” stars, with Pollux nearby. The pair makes a useful naked-eye starting point: look for the two beacons, then turn your attention back to Castor. Under dark skies, it shines readily; from a light-polluted yard, it is still an easy target.

The contrast between what the eye sees and what a telescope can reveal is part of the pleasure. A steady telescope view can show Castor’s close visual pair as two points rather than one. Begin at low magnification, centre the star, and increase power gradually. Atmospheric turbulence can blur or distort the view, so wait for moments when the image settles. If the stars seem to merge, try again when Castor is higher in the sky, where its light passes through less atmosphere.

That small split is only the beginning of the system’s complexity. “Multiple star” is not just a poetic label: stars can be bound together by gravity, orbiting a shared centre of mass. Some pairs are separated enough to distinguish through a telescope; others are so close that their motion is detected by spectroscopy or changes in brightness. Castor’s catalogue classification signals that more than one stellar component is involved, even though a telescope’s view cannot reveal every orbit or companion.

For a simple observing record, note the date, sky conditions, instrument and magnification. Sketch what you see, including the apparent spacing and brightness contrast between the points. Repeat the observation on another night. The stars will not visibly swing around one another over a few days, but the practice of returning to the same target connects a fleeting view to the longer timescales astronomers study.

Castor also offers a reminder about measurement. SIMBAD lists its position for the J2000 coordinate system as right ascension 07h 34m 35.87s and declination +31° 53′ 17.8″—a precise address for a light that has crossed interstellar space before reaching your eye. The catalogue reports a proper motion of about 191 milliarcseconds per year westward and 145 milliarcseconds per year southward. Those shifts are imperceptible in a single observing session, but they register the star’s movement across the sky over time. [SIMBAD: Castor](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=HIP%2036850)

So start with the naked-eye beacon, then let a telescope complicate the picture. Castor rewards both kinds of attention: the quick glance that finds a familiar constellation, and the patient look that discovers the point of light is a system.
