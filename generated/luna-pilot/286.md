# MCG 10-18-74: A Quiet Island in the Northern Sky

*An extended galaxy with a modest catalog number rewards patient searching—and reminds us that a redshift is not always a simple yardstick.*

Some galaxies announce themselves in photographs; others ask you to slow down. MCG 10-18-74 is a good target for the second kind of encounter. In the SIMBAD catalog, the corresponding entry for NGC 4605 describes a galaxy “towards a Group of Galaxies.” Its listed position places it in a richly populated northern sky, but the galaxy itself is the prize: a broad, faint patch of light spread across several arcminutes. [SIMBAD: NGC 4605](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%204605)

The catalog gives its position as right ascension 12h 39m 59s and declination +61° 36′ 33″ (J2000). That far-northern declination is useful at northern latitudes: from 40° north, the galaxy passes nearly overhead. Its listed optical dimensions—about 5.8 by 2.3 arcminutes—are larger than many observers expect from a small catalog entry. Those measurements describe its extent in images, though; they don’t promise a sharply edged shape in the eyepiece.

SIMBAD lists the galaxy’s morphology as “S/Ix,” a classification that suggests a spiral or irregular appearance rather than a tidy, certain label. Such shorthand is a reminder that galaxies can resist neat categories. Their forms are shaped by stars, gas, dust and gravity, while our view depends on the wavelength and detail of the observations. A photograph may show structure that a visual observer sees only as a subtle brightening against the background.

There is another reason to treat the catalog numbers thoughtfully. The listed heliocentric radial velocity is about 145 kilometers per second, corresponding to a very small redshift, roughly 0.00048. Redshift is often used to estimate how far away a galaxy is, but at such a low velocity the expansion of the universe is not the only motion involved. A galaxy’s motion within its local cosmic neighborhood can matter substantially. So this figure is useful scientific context, not a dependable stand-alone distance estimate.

For the observing session, start with the coordinates rather than a visual guess. Use a current chart or planetarium app set to the J2000 position, then identify nearby stars in a low-power eyepiece. Give your eyes time to adapt to darkness; avoid white phone screens, and use averted vision—looking just to the side of the target—to catch faint, diffuse light. A dark, transparent sky will help more than magnification alone. A small telescope may reveal only a soft oval glow, while a larger aperture and careful observing may bring out a brighter central region or uneven texture. Don’t mistake a subdued view for a disappointing one: the galaxy’s cataloged span is several times the apparent width of Jupiter.

The best moment may come when you stop expecting a dramatic spiral and recognize what the faint patch represents: a galaxy whose light has crossed space to meet your eye. MCG 10-18-74 is not a spectacle on demand. It is an invitation to look steadily, compare what you see with the chart, and let a distant island in the northern sky emerge at its own quiet pace.
