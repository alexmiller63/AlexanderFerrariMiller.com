# MCG 3-29-20: A Quiet Beacon in Leo

*This catalog-numbered galaxy is more than a faint oval: its compact, energetic core marks it as a Seyfert 2.*

In the spring constellation Leo, a small telescope can turn up a distant island of stars with a surprisingly active heart. MCG 3-29-20 is also cataloged as NGC 3607. Its light has traveled across space to reach us, carrying clues to a galaxy whose calm-looking glow conceals a powerful central engine.

The galaxy sits at right ascension 11h 16m 55s and declination +18° 03′ (J2000), in Leo. Catalog data list it as roughly 4.1 by 3.6 arcminutes across—several times the apparent width of Jupiter, though its light is spread thinly over that area. Its listed visual magnitude is about 12.8, so it is a telescopic target rather than a naked-eye sight. [SIMBAD’s NGC 3607 record](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%203607) identifies it as a Seyfert 2 galaxy.

That classification points to the galaxy’s nucleus. A Seyfert galaxy is a type of active galaxy: gas falling toward a supermassive black hole can heat up and shine intensely before it disappears from view. In a Seyfert 2, astronomers see strong emission from the active nucleus, but the broad-line region close to the black hole is not directly visible. One explanation is that intervening gas and dust obscure our view of that inner zone. The galaxy’s ordinary starlight remains visible, but the nucleus adds a more energetic chapter to its story.

SIMBAD lists a redshift of about 0.00319, corresponding to a recession velocity near 955 kilometres per second in the record. That figure is not a simple cosmic speedometer: at relatively small distances, a galaxy’s motion within its local region can complicate attempts to infer its distance from redshift alone. For observers, the essential point is that MCG 3-29-20 is far beyond the Milky Way, and its light arrives as a soft, extended patch rather than a sharp point.

To find it, look in Leo during northern spring evenings, when the constellation climbs high in the south. Start with Delta Leonis (Zosma), the star marking the lion’s hindquarters, then sweep about 2½ degrees south and slightly east with a finder or binoculars. A planetarium app or printed chart set to the galaxy’s coordinates can help distinguish it from nearby field stars. Under a dark sky, try a telescope of 6 inches or more; higher magnification is not always better for a diffuse object, so begin at low or moderate power and use averted vision. Let your eyes adapt to darkness, and shield the eyepiece from stray light.

Expect a small, faint glow, not a dramatic spiral with arms. Increase the aperture if you can, but give the view time: the galaxy’s oval outline may emerge gradually against the background. What looks like a subdued smudge is a whole stellar system—and, at its center, a compact source whose activity makes MCG 3-29-20 worth seeking out.
