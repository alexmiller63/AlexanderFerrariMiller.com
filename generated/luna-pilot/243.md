# MCG 9-20-52: A Quiet Island in the Great Bear

*An edge-on galaxy turns its narrow glow toward observers in the Northern Hemisphere—and rewards patient searching.*

Spring nights bring the Great Bear high over the northern horizon. Among its galaxies is MCG 9-20-52, also catalogued as NGC 4026: a remote island of stars in a sky best known for familiar patterns. Its light has travelled across space to reach us, but the galaxy itself is no naked-eye spectacle. It is a target for deliberate observation, and a reminder that the brightest-looking constellations are only signposts to much fainter things.

The SIMBAD catalogue places NGC 4026 at right ascension 11h 59m 25.1s and declination +50° 57′ 42″ (J2000). Its listed optical dimensions are about 4.95 by 1.28 arcminutes—an elongated patch on the sky. The catalogue gives a B-band magnitude of 12.17; that measurement is not the same as a visual magnitude, but it conveys the challenge: this is a galaxy for a telescope and a reasonably dark site, not a casual glance from a bright city street. [SIMBAD: NGC 4026](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%204026)

That long, slim outline offers a hint of orientation. We see the galaxy’s light spread across a narrow strip rather than presenting a round face. In a telescope, however, its shape may be subtle: faint galaxies rarely look like the crisp, high-contrast images made by long-exposure cameras. At the eyepiece, the view can be a soft wisp against darkness, easier to notice with averted vision—looking just to one side of the object so the more light-sensitive parts of the eye can help.

There is useful science behind the catalog numbers. SIMBAD lists a radial velocity of about 940 kilometres per second and a redshift near 0.00314. Redshift describes how the galaxy’s light is shifted toward longer wavelengths; the listed velocity is the catalogue’s measure of its motion relative to us. For a relatively nearby galaxy, that number alone should not be treated as a precise distance: local motions can complicate a simple conversion from recession speed to cosmic distance.

For observers, the coordinates are the surest route. Find Ursa Major, then use a detailed star chart or a telescope’s setting circles or GoTo system to reach the listed position. At nearly 12 hours right ascension and +51 degrees declination, NGC 4026 is well placed for many Northern Hemisphere observers when Ursa Major rides high. Begin with a low-power eyepiece to locate the field, then try a little more magnification once you have the galaxy centered. Give your eyes time to adapt, shield the eyepiece from stray light, and try averted vision rather than staring directly at the faint glow.

A dark, transparent sky matters more than rushing to higher power. If the galaxy does not appear at once, take a moment to check the chart and let the field settle. The reward may be understated: a thin suggestion of light, nearly lost among the stars. Yet that quiet streak is an entire galaxy, its photons crossing the gulf between worlds and arriving, at last, in an observer’s eye.
