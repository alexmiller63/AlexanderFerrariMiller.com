# M86, the Galaxy That Seems to Come Toward Us

*In Virgo’s crowded spring sky, this bright elliptical galaxy offers a quiet visual reward—and a surprising motion measured across the stars.*

A galaxy can look still and distant, yet carry a startling velocity. M86, in the constellation Virgo, has a measured heliocentric radial velocity of about **−306 kilometers per second**: its motion along our line of sight is toward us. That number doesn’t mean the galaxy is rushing straight at Earth through empty space. M86 belongs to the rich Virgo galaxy region, where galaxies move in many directions under one another’s gravity. Its measured approach is one part of that larger, restless scene.

M86 is classified as an **E–E/S0 galaxy**, a description placing it among elliptical and lenticular forms. Rather than the conspicuous spiral arms of galaxies like the Milky Way, it presents a smooth, rounded glow. In a catalogue, that glow spans roughly **9.3 by 6.6 arcminutes**—a little over a quarter of the Moon’s apparent width along its longest axis. Its apparent visual magnitude is listed at **8.90**, bright enough to make it a rewarding target for modest equipment under a dark sky. [SIMBAD’s M86 entry](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%204406) gives its classification, apparent size, brightness and radial velocity.

The telescope view is not a close-up. Even a good backyard instrument shows the galaxy as a soft oval of light, with its central region brighter than its outer haze. The wonder is partly in the contrast: a faint blur in the eyepiece is an immense stellar system, and the light arriving now began its journey long before anyone could point a telescope at it.

## How to find it

M86’s J2000 coordinates are **right ascension 12h 26m 11.8s, declination +12° 56′ 45″**. Those numbers can guide a GoTo mount or a digital chart. For star-hoppers, begin with Virgo on a clear spring evening and use a detailed star atlas to work into the constellation’s rich galaxy fields. M86 lies close to M84, and a low-power eyepiece may show both galaxies in the same field, depending on the telescope and eyepiece. A chart is especially helpful here: the surrounding sky contains many faint galaxies, and the eye can easily mistake one smudge for another.

Start with the lowest useful magnification to locate the field, then increase the power to study the galaxies’ shapes. Let your eyes adjust to the darkness, shield nearby lights, and use averted vision—looking slightly to one side of a faint object rather than directly at it. Under suburban skies, M86 may be subtle; darker skies make its diffuse outer light easier to detect. Binoculars can help sweep the region, but a small telescope is a better choice for distinguishing the galaxy from the background.

There is a particular pleasure in finding M86 among the Virgo galaxies: the view is modest, but the setting is immense. The eyepiece offers a pale oval. The catalogue offers coordinates, dimensions and a negative velocity. Between the two lies the astronomer’s invitation—to look carefully, and to remember that the apparently settled sky is still in motion.
