# Arp 135: A Quiet Galaxy with a Peculiar Name

*An autumn constellation offers a telescope-sized view of NGC 1023, a lenticular galaxy whose catalog identity hints at a more dramatic story.*

Arp 135 sounds like a warning from a science-fiction star chart. It is, instead, a real galaxy: NGC 1023, a bright target in Perseus. Its name comes from astronomer Halton Arp’s catalog of unusual galaxies; its modern catalog description is more restrained. SIMBAD classifies NGC 1023 as a barred lenticular galaxy, type SB(rs)0−, and lists it as a candidate active galactic nucleus. Those labels offer two ways into the view: the galaxy’s smooth, disk-like structure and the possibility of activity in its core.

A lenticular galaxy sits between spiral and elliptical galaxies in the familiar classification scheme. It has a flattened disk, like a spiral, but typically lacks the conspicuous spiral arms and abundant star-forming material that make spirals look busy. The result can be a galaxy that appears serene in a small telescope, even though its stars are moving within a vast system. “Candidate” matters, too: SIMBAD’s listing is a classification, not a promise that an observer will see an active nucleus. At the eyepiece, the galaxy’s core and disk are the show.

NGC 1023 is at right ascension 2 hours 40 minutes and declination +39°04′ (J2000), in Perseus. SIMBAD lists an apparent V magnitude of 9.35. That is the galaxy’s combined brightness, not the brightness of a point; its light is spread across an extended patch of sky. Expect a small, faint oval rather than a star-like beacon. The catalog’s measured position and brightness make useful signposts, but a finder chart will make the final approach easier.
