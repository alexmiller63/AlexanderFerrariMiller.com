# Alpha2 Cru: A blue point at the foot of the Cross

*In the Southern Cross, Acrux offers a bright landmark—and a close pairing to explore with a telescope.*

As dusk deepens in the Southern Hemisphere’s autumn, the Southern Cross climbs into view. Its brightest star, Acrux, marks the Cross’s southern end. Look closely with a telescope and the single bright point resolves into two stars: Alpha1 Cru and Alpha2 Cru. The second of these, Alpha2 Cru, is a blue-white star with a catalogue identity all its own: HD 108249.

The distinction is a useful reminder that stars with nearly the same address can be separate objects. The catalogue identifies HD 108249 as Alpha2 Cru and gives its position as right ascension 12h 26m 36.442s, declination −63° 05′ 58.28″ (J2000). The database lists its visual magnitude as 1.58, bright enough to be seen without optical aid under ordinary conditions. It also assigns the star a B1V spectral type: “B” indicates a hot, blue-white star, while “V” denotes a main-sequence star. The catalogue flags that classification as low quality, so it is best treated as a catalogue description rather than a finely pinned-down measurement.

Alpha2’s brilliance makes it a fine naked-eye guidepost, but its close companion is the real observing invitation. To the unaided eye, Acrux appears as one star. Binoculars may show it still as a single point; a telescope, with a steady view and suitable magnification, can separate the two bright components. Try first at low power, then increase the magnification if the stars remain merged. Atmospheric turbulence can blur a close pair, so a crisp, calm night is more helpful than simply using the most powerful eyepiece.

For a finder, start with the Southern Cross’s familiar kite-like outline. Acrux is the bright star at its lower, southern end. A star chart or planetarium app can help confirm the identification; search for “Alpha2 Cru” or “HD 108249” rather than relying on the naked-eye appearance alone. The coordinates provide another check: Alpha2 lies far south, at declination −63°. From Earth’s geometry, it can rise above the horizon only for observers south of about 27° north latitude. Farther north, the Cross stays below the horizon.

Once you have Acrux in the eyepiece, pause before moving on. The striking effect is not just that one point becomes two: it is that the sky’s familiar signpost turns out to be a small patch of stellar real estate. Alpha2 Cru is bright enough to anchor the view, yet its catalogue entry—one identifier among many—hints at the careful bookkeeping behind modern astronomy. A name in the sky becomes a coordinate, a magnitude, and a tentative spectral label. Then, through the telescope, it becomes a star beside another star.
