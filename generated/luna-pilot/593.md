# Proxima Centauri: The Nearby Star You Can’t See

*Close enough for its motion to be measured from year to year, this faint red dwarf is a rewarding target for patient southern-sky observers.*

Somewhere in Centaurus, a star shines too faintly for unaided eyes. Proxima Centauri is our nearest stellar neighbor, but closeness does not make it brilliant: this small, cool red dwarf glows at about magnitude 11 in visible light. Finding it asks for a telescope, a detailed chart and a little patience.

The numbers make the contrast striking. SIMBAD lists Proxima’s parallax as 768 milliarcseconds. Turning that apparent shift into distance puts the star roughly 1.3 parsecs—or about 4.2 light-years—from the Sun. The same database gives its visible-light magnitude as 11.13 and classifies it as M5.5Ve, a late-type red dwarf. It is a nearby world in astronomical terms, yet a dim point in an eyepiece.

Its position also gives observers a sense of the sky’s scale. SIMBAD’s J2000 coordinates are right ascension 14 hours 29 minutes 43 seconds and declination 62 degrees 40 minutes 46 seconds south. That far-southern declination makes Proxima best suited to observers in the Southern Hemisphere and at low northern latitudes, where it can rise well above the horizon. When Centaurus is favorably placed, use a planetarium app or printed chart to work from brighter stars toward the precise field. Proxima is not a naked-eye or ordinary binocular target; a telescope and a finder chart that reaches faint stars are the practical tools.

The star has another claim to attention: it moves quickly against the background. SIMBAD reports proper-motion components of about 3,782 milliarcseconds per year in right ascension and 769 milliarcseconds per year in declination. Combined, that is nearly 3.9 arcseconds annually—a noticeable shift on the scale of stellar positions, though not something to watch unfold during a single observing session. Compare carefully dated images, and Proxima’s journey becomes apparent.

For a visual observer, the immediate experience is quieter. At the eyepiece, expect a faint point, not a red beacon. In dim light, human vision loses much of its color sensitivity, so the star’s red-dwarf identity is clearer in the science than in its apparent hue. Give your eyes time to adapt, shield the telescope from stray light, and use a chart matched to the eyepiece’s field of view. If the star proves elusive, check focus and field orientation before concluding it is absent.

There is a pleasing paradox in seeking Proxima: it is extraordinarily close by galactic standards, yet hard to pick out from Earth. Its faintness reminds us that proximity and brightness are different things. Its measured motion, meanwhile, turns a seemingly fixed point into a traveler. Find it, and you have not just located a dim star in Centaurus—you have glimpsed a nearby system whose position changes against the vast, slow-looking pattern of the night.
