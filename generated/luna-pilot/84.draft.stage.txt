# Shaula, the Scorpion’s Bright Stitch

*At the end of Scorpius, a brilliant blue-white star marks the turn from summer sky to the deep south.*

The Scorpion’s tail is a hook drawn in stars, and Shaula gleams near its tip. Look for Scorpius low in the southern sky on a summer evening in the Northern Hemisphere; from farther south, it climbs higher. Shaula is bright enough to stand out even where city lights wash away fainter stars. Its name in a star chart is Lambda Scorpii, and its position—right ascension 17h 33m 36.5s, declination −37° 06′—places it far south of the celestial equator. [SIMBAD](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=HIP%2085927)

Begin with Antares, Scorpius’s conspicuous reddish heart. Trace the constellation’s curved body down toward the horizon; the line bends into the sting, where Shaula shines close to its neighbor Lesath. The pair makes a striking marker at the Scorpion’s end, though their apparent closeness is a perspective effect: stars that look like neighbors can lie at very different distances. Give your eyes a few minutes to adjust, then sweep the pattern slowly rather than searching for one star in isolation. Binoculars can help reveal the surrounding star field, but Shaula itself needs no optical aid.

The eye sees a steady point of light, but the catalog record hints at a more complicated object. SIMBAD lists Shaula as a Beta Cephei variable—a hot star whose light can vary as it pulsates. Such changes are subtle to casual observers, not the dramatic blinking of a warning beacon. The same entry records a composite spectral type, B2IV+DA7.9: a B-type subgiant spectrum alongside a DA-type white-dwarf spectrum. That combination is a reminder that a familiar point in a constellation may represent more than one stellar component, and that careful measurement can disclose what unaided vision cannot.

SIMBAD gives Shaula a visual magnitude of 1.63, explaining its prominence. It also lists a parallax of 5.71 milliarcseconds. Inverting that value gives a rough distance of about 570 light-years, though the listed parallax has an uncertainty, so the estimate should not be treated as exact. The light reaching your eye began its journey long before the star became a point on tonight’s chart.
