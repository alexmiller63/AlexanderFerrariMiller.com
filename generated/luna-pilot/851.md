# Beta Pyx: A Quiet Beacon in the Southern Sky

*This fourth-magnitude star’s measured parallax places it nearly 386 light-years away—a useful reminder that even familiar points of light have depth.*

Beta Pyx does not announce itself with a famous name or an especially bright glimmer. It waits in Pyxis, the southern constellation of the compass, among stars that can be easy to overlook if you are looking for a showpiece. But a steady point of light can still tell a substantial story. Catalogued as HIP 42515, Beta Pyx has a measured parallax that lets astronomers estimate its distance, while its spectrum offers clues to its place in stellar life. gathers those measurements.

The star’s parallax is about 8.46 milliarcseconds. Parallax is the tiny apparent shift in a nearby star’s position as Earth travels around the Sun; the smaller the shift, the farther away the star. Converting that measurement gives a distance of roughly 118 parsecs, or 386 light-years. That is an estimate, not a direct journey across space: the number comes from measuring a minute angle and applying geometry.

Beta Pyx shines at about magnitude 3.95 in visible light, bright enough to see without optical aid under a dark sky. The catalogue lists its spectral type as G5 II/III. The G classification places it among yellowish stars, while II and III describe luminous giant and bright-giant categories. The combined notation signals an in-between classification; the catalogue marks the spectral-type entry with a lower-quality flag, so it is best treated as a guide rather than a definitive label. Still, it hints that Beta Pyx is not simply a Sun-like star at a greater distance: its light and classification point to a more evolved, more luminous object.

There is something appealing about the contrast between the star’s understated appearance and the information hidden in its light. To the unaided eye, it is just one point in a pattern. A catalog supplies its coordinates, brightness, spectrum and parallax; each is a different way of asking what that point is and where it belongs. The star does not need to look spectacular for those questions to matter.

**How to find it:** Beta Pyx lies at right ascension 8 hours 40 minutes and declination 35 degrees 18 minutes south (J2000 coordinates). Those coordinates make it a much easier target from the Southern Hemisphere, where it climbs higher above the horizon. From northern locations it remains accessible in principle, but sits low in the southern sky, where haze and buildings can interfere. Look when Pyxis is well placed, and use a current sky chart or planetarium app to identify the constellation rather than relying on Beta Pyx as a signpost: at fourth magnitude, it is not among the sky’s brightest guide stars. Binoculars can help separate the surrounding star pattern, though the star itself should be visible to the unaided eye under reasonably dark conditions.

Give your eyes time to adjust, then find the small, quiet point. Its light began travelling toward us centuries ago. A careful measurement of that light’s apparent shift across the year turns the point into a place: roughly 386 light-years away, in a sky that is still changing as Earth turns beneath it.
