# Larawag: A Steady Light in the Scorpion

*Look south on a winter evening in the Southern Hemisphere—or a summer evening in the north—to meet a nearby orange giant hiding in plain sight.*

As twilight fades, Scorpius takes shape low in the southern sky: a sweeping curve of stars with bright Antares at its heart. Follow the constellation’s outline and you’ll find Larawag, a modest-looking point of light with a remarkable distinction. It is close enough to count among the Sun’s stellar neighbors, yet its warm glow comes from a star in a very different stage of life.

Larawag is the proper name of Epsilon Scorpii, cataloged as HIP 82396. SIMBAD gives it a visual magnitude of 2.29, bright enough to see without binoculars under a reasonably dark sky. Its spectral classification, K1III, identifies it as a K-type giant: cooler in appearance than the Sun, and expanded beyond the compact, stable state of a star like ours. Its golden-orange tint can be subtle to the unaided eye, especially when the star is low and its light passes through more of Earth’s atmosphere.

The SIMBAD record lists a parallax of 51.19 milliarcseconds. Parallax is the apparent shift of a nearby star against more distant stars as Earth moves around the Sun.
