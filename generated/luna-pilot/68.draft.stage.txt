# Zeta Ursae Minoris: A Quiet Marker in the Little Dipper

*An unassuming point of light in the north offers a chance to practise finding your way by stars—and to consider what a catalogue can, and cannot, tell us.*

On a clear night, the Little Dipper can seem to hover in the northern sky, its modest stars easy to overlook beside the Big Dipper’s brighter pattern. Zeta Ursae Minoris is one of those quieter points: not the famous Polaris at the end of the handle, but a star along the same familiar curve. Finding it is a small act of celestial wayfinding.

The catalogue entry gives the star a measured visual magnitude of about 4.27—visible to the unaided eye under reasonably dark skies, though likely lost in a bright urban glow. Its listed parallax is 9.08 milliarcseconds. Parallax is the tiny apparent shift of a nearby star against more distant background stars as Earth moves around the Sun. Inverting that measurement gives a distance of roughly 110 parsecs, or about 360 light-years. That is an estimate derived from the catalogue value, not a direct trip odometer.

SIMBAD identifies Zeta Ursae Minoris as a variable star and lists its spectral type as A3Vn. The “A3” describes a hot, blue-white stellar spectrum; “V” indicates a main-sequence star, still in the long phase of fusing hydrogen in its core. The “n” denotes broadened spectral lines. The variable-star label is intriguing, but it does not, by itself, tell an observer how much the star changes, how often it changes, or whether the variation will be apparent to the eye. A database classification is a signpost for further investigation, not a promise of a visible performance.

That gap between what the sky shows and what instruments record is part of the appeal. To the eye, Zeta is simply a steady-looking pinprick. Its catalogue entry, assembled from measurements, gives it a different kind of presence: a position, a spectrum, a distance estimate, and a hint that its light may not be perfectly constant.

For a first look, go outside when Ursa Minor is well clear of nearby buildings or trees. In much of the Northern Hemisphere, the constellation stays above the horizon all night, though its height and orientation change with season and location. Find Polaris first, then follow the Little Dipper’s handle away from it toward the bowl. A printed star chart or a planetarium app can help distinguish the fainter handle stars; use the chart’s orientation for your location and date rather than assuming the dipper always appears upright. Binoculars can make the pattern easier to pick out, but they are not essential under dark skies.
