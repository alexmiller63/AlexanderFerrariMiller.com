# Epsilon Sagittarii: A Star in the Catalogue and in Motion

*Measurements of brightness, distance and movement turn a point of light into a more precise celestial address.*

A star can appear fixed in the sky and still have a measurable place, distance and motion. Epsilon Sagittarii—identified in SIMBAD as “eps Sgr”—offers a compact example. Its catalogue entry records a bright object in Sagittarius, gives coordinates for locating it, and tracks small changes in its position over time. Each figure answers a different question: how bright it appears, how far away it may be, and how its position shifts against the sky.

SIMBAD lists the star’s V-band magnitude as 1.81. That number describes its recorded brightness in visible light; it is a measurement, rather than a description of how the star will look from every location or under every sky condition. The catalogue also gives coordinates for the J2000 reference frame: right ascension 18 hours, 24 minutes and 10.318 seconds, and declination −34 degrees, 23 minutes and 4.619 seconds. Put more simply, the coordinates specify a position on the celestial grid, with the negative declination placing the star south of the celestial equator. [SIMBAD: HIP 90185 / Epsilon Sagittarii](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=HIP%2090185)

Distance is not stated directly in the entry, but SIMBAD lists a parallax of 22.76 milliarcseconds, with an uncertainty of 0.24 milliarcseconds. Parallax is the angular shift used to estimate a star’s distance; taking the reciprocal of the listed value gives approximately 44 parsecs, or about 143 light-years. That is an estimate derived from the catalogue measurement, not a direct journey through space. The reported uncertainty also matters: the parallax is not an infinitely exact number, and a distance calculated from it should be treated as approximate.

The same record describes Epsilon Sagittarii as a high-proper-motion star. Its listed proper motion is −39.42 milliarcseconds per year in right ascension, adjusted by the cosine of declination, and −124.20 milliarcseconds per year in declination. Both components are measured on the sky. The negative declination value indicates a southward shift; the negative right-ascension component indicates a shift toward decreasing right ascension. These are small annual changes, but they are measurable and give the catalogue a way to describe a sky that is not perfectly static.

The entry also gives a spectral type: B9IVp_lB?, with a quality rating of C and a reference dating to 1975. The notation is part of the catalogue’s record, including its question mark; it should not be mistaken for a complete explanation of the star. Its presence alongside coordinates, brightness, parallax and motion shows how different kinds of observations accumulate into a concise astronomical profile.

For anyone trying to identify the star, the coordinates provide a more reliable guide than a name alone: right ascension 18h 24m and declination about 34 degrees south. A sky chart or planetarium app can use that position to show where to look. The catalogue’s numbers do not replace the experience of seeing the star. They add context: the bright point is Epsilon Sagittarii, its distance is estimated from parallax, and its position shifts gradually over time.
