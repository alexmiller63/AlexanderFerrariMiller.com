# M108: A Long, Quiet Streak in Ursa Major

*An unbarred spiral galaxy with the proportions of a thin brushstroke, M108 rewards patient looking more than a quick glance.*

On a clear spring night, the Big Dipper can feel like a map drawn in bright, familiar strokes. M108 is a subtler mark: a distant galaxy in Ursa Major, not a star but a stretched patch of light. Its catalogue name is NGC 3556. In SIMBAD, its position is given as right ascension 11h 11m 31s and declination +55° 40′ 27″ (J2000), a useful address for finding it on a chart or entering into a digital sky atlas. [SIMBAD: M108 / NGC 3556](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%203556)

**Scientific context**

M108’s apparent shape tells part of the story before a telescope reveals any detail. SIMBAD lists its optical dimensions as about 14.6 by 3.9 arcminutes, and its morphological type as SA—a classification for an unbarred spiral galaxy. Seen as a narrow streak, it invites the impression of a broad disk viewed close to edge-on. That is a visual clue, not a full portrait: the light reaching us blends countless stars and other features into one delicate glow.

The database also lists M108 as an active galaxy nucleus. That label marks a classification of its central region; it does not mean the galaxy will look dramatic through an eyepiece. On the contrary, its appeal is the contrast between its modest appearance and its scale: a galaxy whose light occupies a slender shape in the sky, while its real structure lies far beyond the reach of unaided vision.

M108’s listed redshift is 0.002334. Redshift is a measure of how light has shifted toward longer wavelengths, and astronomers use it as one clue when studying a galaxy’s motion and place in the expanding universe. Here, it’s enough to remember that the faint streak is not a nearby cloud or a mark on the lens. It is another galaxy, seen across an immense interval of space.

**Observing guide**

Begin with a chart of Ursa Major, then use M108’s coordinates to confirm the target; a planetarium app or telescope’s coordinate display can make the search easier. A low-power eyepiece gives you a wider field for locating the galaxy. Once centered, try a little more magnification, and give your eyes time to adjust to the dark. Averted vision—looking slightly to one side of the object—can help make faint light easier to detect.

Choose a transparent, moonless night if possible, and shield your eyes from nearby lights. Don’t expect a bright, detailed view: M108 is best approached as a faint, elongated smudge. Patiently watching can make its orientation clearer, especially when the field is steady and your eyes are dark-adapted. Sketching what you see is a useful way to notice the shape without assuming that every speck in the eyepiece belongs to the galaxy.

The Big Dipper offers an easy beginning to a night’s observing, but M108 asks for a different kind of attention. It is not a showpiece that announces itself. It is a thin, faraway island of stars—quietly present when you know where to look.
