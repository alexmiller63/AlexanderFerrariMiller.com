# NGC 2867: A Small Circle, a Star’s Last Act

*This compact planetary nebula is easy to underestimate. Find it in the southern sky, then give it magnification—and time.*

At first, NGC 2867 may seem like little more than a faint speck. But that tiny patch of light is a planetary nebula: the glowing gas shed by a star late in its life, lit from within by the hot stellar remnant left behind. Its roundness is a reminder that a dramatic transformation can unfold on a remarkably modest patch of sky.

The name can mislead. Planetary nebulae have nothing to do with planets; early telescopes sometimes made them look like small disks. NGC 2867 really is compact. SIMBAD lists its radio angular size at about a quarter of an arcminute across—roughly 15 arcseconds—and gives a visual magnitude of 10.3. It also records the spectral classification [WO2] for the object, a clue to the unusual, highly evolved star associated with its nebular glow. These catalog measurements and classifications are available through [SIMBAD’s NGC 2867 entry](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%202867).

A planetary nebula forms when a low- or intermediate-mass star reaches the end of its red-giant phase and expels its outer layers. The exposed core floods that material with ultraviolet light, causing the gas to shine. The nebula is temporary on cosmic timescales: it expands and fades, while the stellar core cools toward its long future as a white dwarf. In NGC 2867, the compact appearance makes the scene feel almost like a single point—but it is a shell of stellar material, spread across space.

To find it, start with SIMBAD’s J2000 position: right ascension 9 hours 21 minutes 25 seconds, declination −58° 18′ 41″. That far-southern declination makes it a rewarding target for observers in the Southern Hemisphere, where it can climb well above the horizon. From much of the Northern Hemisphere it stays very low, or never rises at all. Use a planetarium app or a detailed star chart to place the coordinates among nearby guide stars; the nebula is too small to stand out in a wide-field view.
