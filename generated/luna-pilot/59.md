# Mizar in the Catalogue: One Star, Several Measurements

*SIMBAD identifies Mizar as a double or multiple star. Its entry also shows how a single catalogue record can gather names, positions and measurements from different astronomical sources.*

A familiar name can make a star seem like a simple point of light. In an astronomical database, that point may have several names and a collection of measurements, each describing a different aspect of its record. Mizar is a good example: SIMBAD lists it as a “Double or Multiple Star” and associates it with the designation Zeta Ursae Majoris, written ζ UMa.

The entry gathers identifiers from multiple catalogues. Alongside Mizar and ζ UMa are HIP 65378 and HIC 65378, catalogue designations, and 79 UMa, another listed name. SIMBAD also includes WDS J13239+5456AB and STF 1744AB among its identifiers. These labels are not different objects merely because they look different; they are ways of locating or referring to the entry in astronomical catalogues.

SIMBAD’s hierarchy section reports two linked child objects. That number is a database relationship count, not a complete description of what an observer would see. The service cautions that linked-object counts depend on the references and links in its records. Its classification—“Double or Multiple Star”—offers a broader description, but the entry alone does not tell the reader how the system appears through a particular telescope.

The record also gives Mizar’s position. In the International Celestial Reference System for epoch J2000, its coordinates are right ascension 13 hours, 23 minutes, 55.54048 seconds, and declination +54 degrees, 55 minutes, 31.2671 seconds. Coordinates are a map address in the sky: the first measures position around the celestial sphere, and the second measures it north or south of the celestial equator. SIMBAD associates this coordinate record with the reference code 2007A&A...474..653V.

Other entries describe motion. SIMBAD lists proper motion of 119.01 milliarcseconds per year in right ascension, with the cosine-of-declination convention, and −25.97 milliarcseconds per year in declination. These are angular changes in position across the sky, not a direct statement of how quickly the star is moving through space. The database separately lists a heliocentric radial velocity of −6.31 kilometers per second, with an uncertainty of 0.38 kilometers per second.

A further measurement is parallax: 38.01 milliarcseconds, with an uncertainty of 1.71. Parallax is the apparent shift in a star’s position used to estimate its distance. Taking the reciprocal of 0.03801 arcseconds gives a rough distance of about 26 parsecs, or 86 light-years. That conversion is an estimate based on the listed parallax, not a separate distance value displayed in the entry.

Each figure answers a different question. Coordinates say where to point; proper motion describes a measured change in sky position; radial velocity records motion along the line of sight; and parallax provides a distance estimate. None, alone, tells the whole story of a multiple star. Together, the catalogue’s names and measurements show how much information can sit behind a short label: Mizar, or ζ UMa.
