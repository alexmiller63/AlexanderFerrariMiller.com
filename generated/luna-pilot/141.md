# NGC 300: Reading a Spiral Galaxy’s Catalog Record

*Coordinates, classification and measurements offer a precise—but limited—portrait of a galaxy in the southern sky.*

Some objects announce themselves with brilliance. A catalog entry for NGC 300 makes a different kind of introduction: a position, a type, an apparent size and a measured brightness. Together, these details describe how the galaxy is recorded and observed, while leaving important questions—such as its physical dimensions—unanswered by the figures alone.

NGC 300 is listed as a galaxy with morphological type Sc. Its coordinates for the J2000 epoch are right ascension 00h 54m 53.4466s and declination −37° 41′ 03.168″. The negative declination places it south of the celestial equator. The coordinates provide a precise point for locating the object, but they do not describe its visible outline. For that, the catalog gives an optical angular size of 20.89 by 13.49 arcminutes, with an orientation angle of 114 degrees. These are apparent dimensions on the sky, not a measurement of the galaxy’s width in space. ([SIMBAD’s NGC 300 entry](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%20300).)

The Sc classification is a compact label for its recorded morphological type. It is useful for identifying the galaxy within a scheme of galaxy forms, but it is not a detailed account of every feature in NGC 300. The catalog lists the classification with a 2012 astronomical reference; the angular dimensions come from a different, 2003 reference. Those dates matter: a catalog page brings together measurements and descriptions drawn from separate sources, rather than presenting one single observation made at one time.

Brightness is another entry, and one that needs to be read with care. SIMBAD lists a V-band magnitude of 8.13, alongside values in several other filters, including U, B, R, J, H and K. These are measurements in different parts of the spectrum, not competing estimates of one identical quantity. The V value is an apparent magnitude: it records how bright NGC 300 appears in that band from Earth. On its own, it is not a measurement of the galaxy’s total energy output or physical size.

The entry also lists a heliocentric radial velocity of 146 kilometres per second, with an uncertainty of 2 kilometres per second, and a spectroscopic redshift of 0.000487. These figures describe measured motion and spectral shift, not a direct distance measurement. Keeping the quantities distinct helps prevent a catalog’s concise numbers from being asked to say more than they do.

NGC 300’s record is therefore less a complete portrait than a set of carefully labeled clues. Its coordinates identify where to point; its optical dimensions describe the apparent span; its Sc type supplies a broad morphological classification; and its magnitudes and velocity report other measured properties. Each figure is useful, but each belongs to a particular category and source. Read together, they give a structured account of the galaxy without turning a handful of catalog values into a full description of its physical nature.
