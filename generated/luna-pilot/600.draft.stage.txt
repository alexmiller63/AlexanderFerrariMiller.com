# Zubenelgenubi: A Bright Star with a Hidden Orbit

*Look for Libra’s steady point of light—and remember that its calm appearance conceals motion revealed by starlight.*

Zubenelgenubi is a fine reminder that a star can look solitary while telling a more complicated story. Listed by astronomers as **α² Librae**, it shines at about magnitude 2.75 in visible light, bright enough to pick out with the unaided eye under reasonably dark skies. Yet the catalog entry for this star identifies it as a **spectroscopic binary**: its paired nature is detected not by separating two points of light, but by studying the changing patterns in its spectrum. ([SIMBAD: HIP 72622](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=HIP%2072622))

That distinction is a small lesson in how astronomy works. A telescope may show a star as a single point, but a spectrograph spreads its light into a band marked by dark absorption lines. As stars orbit one another, their motion toward and away from Earth shifts those lines through the Doppler effect. Repeated measurements can reveal a companion’s gravitational tug even when the stars cannot be visually separated. The catalog classifies Zubenelgenubi as a spectroscopic binary; it does not mean an ordinary backyard telescope will show two distinct stars.

The same listing gives a parallax of 43.03 milliarcseconds. Parallax is the tiny apparent shift of a nearby star against more distant background stars as Earth travels around the Sun. Converting that measurement gives a distance of roughly 23 parsecs, or about 76 light-years. The catalog also records a spectral classification of kA2hA5mA4 IV–V—a technical shorthand for features in the star’s spectrum and its place between luminosity classes IV and V. These measurements add depth to what, at first glance, is simply a bright point in Libra.

## How to find it

Libra lies between Virgo and Scorpius, along the zodiac. From the Northern Hemisphere, look toward the southern sky on spring and early-summer evenings; from farther south, the constellation climbs higher. The best viewing time depends on your location, horizon, and date, so use a current sky chart or planetarium app to confirm Libra’s position.

Start with your unaided eyes from a spot away from bright streetlights. Zubenelgenubi’s reported visual magnitude makes it a practical naked-eye target in a dark or moderately dark sky. If you are new to the constellation, binoculars can help you trace its pattern, but they are not required to spot the star. Give your eyes time to adjust to the dark, and avoid looking at a phone screen at full brightness.

For a useful observing exercise, look first without optics, then return with binoculars and compare how the surrounding star field changes. A small telescope may sharpen the view, but don’t expect it to expose the spectroscopic companion. The binary’s evidence lies in the measured shifts of spectral lines, not in a dramatic split visible at the eyepiece. Zubenelgenubi rewards two kinds of attention: the simple pleasure of finding a bright star, and the scientific habit of asking what its light can reveal.
