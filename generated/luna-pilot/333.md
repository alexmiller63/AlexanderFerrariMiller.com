# M10: A Lantern in Ophiuchus

*An old star cluster spreads its light across a patch of sky much wider than a full Moon. Here’s how to find it—and what to look for when you do.*

On a clear summer evening, point your attention south, toward Ophiuchus. There, beyond the bright patterns that make a constellation easy to recognize, lies M10: not a single star, but a crowded company of them. In the eyepiece, it may first appear as a soft, pale glow. Stay with it. The longer you look, the more the cluster seems to gather itself from the darkness.

Astronomers also know M10 as NGC 6254. SIMBAD classifies it as a globular cluster: a compact, roughly spherical swarm of stars bound together by gravity. Its cataloged position is right ascension 16 hours 57 minutes and declination −4 degrees 06 minutes, and its listed apparent diameter is about 15 arcminutes. That’s half the apparent width of the Moon—large enough to reward patient viewing, though the cluster’s light is spread thinly across the sky.

SIMBAD lists M10’s visual magnitude as 4.98. That number describes the combined light of the cluster, not how bright every part looks through the atmosphere. A faint, extended glow can be harder to spot than a star of the same total magnitude, especially under light-polluted skies. Dark adaptation and averted vision—looking just to one side of the target—can help.

To find M10, begin with a current sky chart or planetarium app and search for M10 or NGC 6254. Ophiuchus is best placed for evening observing around northern summer, when the constellation is well up in the southern sky for many Northern Hemisphere observers. Use the chart to work from recognizable nearby stars toward the cluster; its coordinates are useful for a telescope’s setting circles or a computerized mount. Because its declination is close to the celestial equator, M10 can be observed from a wide range of latitudes.

Binoculars may show it as a small, misty patch. In a telescope, start at low power to locate the cluster and take in its full, rounded shape. Then try a moderate magnification. Under a dark sky, with steady seeing and a telescope capable of gathering enough light, some of the outer stars may begin to separate from the glow. The central region can remain a concentrated haze: a reminder that a cluster can be made of individual suns and still look, at first, like one quiet light.

Give your eyes several minutes to adapt before deciding what you can see. Keep the view steady, and try looking slightly away from the center; faint stars often become easier to notice outside direct vision. If M10 refuses to resolve into points, that is no failure. You are still seeing the combined light of a vast stellar gathering, set against the deep background of the Milky Way.
