# Beta Centauri: A Bright Star with a Variable Classification

*Its catalogue entry records a conspicuous point of light, a measured position and distance estimate, and a classification that hints at changes too subtle to judge by appearance alone.*

Beta Centauri is among the bright stars of the southern sky. In the SIMBAD astronomical database, its visual magnitude is listed as 0.58, and its object type is given as “beta Cep Variable.” The first figure describes how bright the star appears from Earth; the second is a classification, not a claim that its light visibly flickers from one moment to the next. A casual glance can show a bright point, but it cannot establish the details of any variation. That takes repeated measurements.

The classification is a useful starting point, but the database entry does not provide a period or amplitude for changes in brightness. It therefore tells us how the star is categorized, not how much its light changes or how quickly. This distinction matters: a catalogue label can point to a kind of behavior without making that behavior apparent to an observer watching the sky briefly.

SIMBAD places Beta Centauri at right ascension 14 hours, 3 minutes, 49.4 seconds and declination 60 degrees, 22 minutes south, in coordinates for the J2000 reference epoch. The declination explains its strongly southern position. From locations north of roughly 30 degrees north latitude, the star does not rise above the horizon; farther south, it can be seen when local conditions and the time of observation permit. Coordinates turn “southern star” from a general description into a precise position that observers can use to locate it.

The same database lists a parallax of 8.32 milliarcseconds, with an uncertainty of 0.50 milliarcseconds. Parallax is the apparent shift in a nearby star’s position against more distant background objects as Earth changes position in its orbit. Inverting the listed value gives a rough distance of about 120 parsecs, or around 390 light-years. Because the measurement has an uncertainty, that distance should be treated as an estimate rather than an exact value.

SIMBAD also records a spectral type of B1 III, but marks the classification with a low-quality flag. The notation is part of the catalogue’s description of the star; the flag is a reminder that the classification is not presented as especially secure. The entry also gives a radial velocity of 2.52 kilometres per second, with an uncertainty of 2.96 kilometres per second. Radial velocity measures motion along the line of sight, and here the uncertainty is larger than the reported value. The number is useful as a record of a measurement, but it does not support a precise account of the star’s line-of-sight motion.

These details—brightness, position, parallax, spectral classification and radial velocity—describe different aspects of the same object, and they come with different levels of certainty. The database also lists Beta Centauri’s proper motion, its apparent movement across the sky: −33.27 milliarcseconds per year in right ascension (adjusted for declination) and −23.16 in declination.
