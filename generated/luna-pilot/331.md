# M12: A Compact Globular Cluster in the Catalogue

*Its catalogue entry gives M12 a name, a position and an apparent size. Those numbers offer a way to locate the object—and a reminder that a point of light in a database can represent something larger than a single star.*

M12 is also known as NGC 6218. SIMBAD classifies it as a globular cluster, and lists its position for the J2000 sky as right ascension 16h 47m 14.18s and declination −1° 56′ 54.7″. These coordinates act like an address on the celestial sphere: a precise way to identify the object, even when its appearance depends on the observing conditions and equipment. [SIMBAD: M12 / NGC 6218](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%206218)

A second number describes how broadly the cluster appears on the sky. SIMBAD lists an angular size of 14.5 by 14.5 arcminutes. This is an apparent measurement: it describes the span seen from Earth, not a physical diameter in kilometres or light-years. Nor does it mean the cluster has a sharp circular edge. The catalogue value gives an observer a useful sense of scale, while leaving the detailed view to the instrument and the sky.

For a practical search, enter the listed coordinates into a star chart or planetarium app and use them to guide the telescope. Begin at low magnification, then adjust as needed to find a view that is comfortable and clear. Binoculars or a telescope can be used to explore the region around the target; a patient observer can take time to compare what is visible with the chart. These are observing suggestions, not extra measurements of M12: the catalogue identifies the object, but it cannot promise what any particular sky or instrument will show.

SIMBAD also records several brightness measurements. Its entry lists a V magnitude of 6.07, a K magnitude of 4.26, and values in the g and z bands of 7.691 and 6.653, respectively. These are measurements in different filters, so they should not be treated as a single set of directly interchangeable brightness readings. Their value is in documenting how M12 is represented in different parts of the catalogue’s photometric record.

Other entries describe motion and distance-related measurements. SIMBAD lists a radial velocity of −41.2 kilometres per second, with an uncertainty of 0.2 kilometres per second, and a parallax of 0.208 milliarcseconds, with an uncertainty of 0.011. The database also gives proper-motion components of −0.15 and −6.77 milliarcseconds per year. Each number is tied to a measurement and its reference; together they make the entry more than a name on a chart.

The catalogue’s many values do not replace the experience of finding M12. They give that experience a framework. A position helps point the way; an angular size describes its apparent extent; brightness and motion measurements add other ways of studying it. At the eyepiece, an observer may see more or less detail than expected, but the identity remains fixed: M12, NGC 6218, classified as a globular cluster. Its modest catalogue entry opens onto a larger task—looking carefully, comparing views and discovering what the sky will reveal that night.
