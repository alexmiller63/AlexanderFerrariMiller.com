# Epsilon Tauri: A Star in the Numbers

*SIMBAD’s entry for Epsilon Tauri brings together a position, measurements of motion and light, and a classification—each a different way to describe the same point in the sky.*

A star’s catalogue entry can look like a collection of abbreviations and decimals. For Epsilon Tauri, also listed under the identifier HIP 20889, those details offer a compact account of how astronomers record an object’s position and properties. SIMBAD classifies it as a double or multiple star and gives its coordinates, parallax, proper motion, radial velocity, spectral type and brightness. [SIMBAD: Epsilon Tauri](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=HIP%2020889)

Its J2000 coordinates are 4 hours, 28 minutes and 37 seconds in right ascension, and about +19 degrees, 10 minutes in declination. In simpler terms, the coordinates specify where to locate the object on the celestial grid. SIMBAD reports the position with high-quality astrometric data, and also lists a proper motion: 107.526 milliarcseconds per year in right ascension, with the declination component at −36.200 milliarcseconds per year. Proper motion describes a star’s measured movement across the sky over time.

The entry also gives a parallax of 22.3654 milliarcseconds, with a listed uncertainty of 0.1721 milliarcseconds. Parallax is the apparent shift of a nearby object against more distant background objects as the observer’s position changes. Converting the listed value gives a distance of roughly 44.7 parsecs, or about 146 light-years. That distance is an estimate derived from the parallax measurement, rather than a separate number quoted in the entry.

SIMBAD records Epsilon Tauri’s radial velocity as 38.420 kilometers per second, with a listed uncertainty of 0.0004 kilometers per second. The database identifies the value as an optical measurement and gives it in the heliocentric frame. Together, the proper-motion and radial-velocity figures describe two different components of motion: one across our line of sight and one along it.

The spectral type in the entry is G9.5IIICN0.5. It is a compact classification, not a complete description of the object. SIMBAD’s record attributes it to a published reference and gives the classification a quality flag of B. The entry’s object-type field separately identifies Epsilon Tauri as a double or multiple star.
