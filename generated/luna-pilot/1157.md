# Rotanev: A Quiet Beacon in the Dolphin

*This modest star in Delphinus is bright enough to find without a telescope—and catalogued as part of a multiple-star system.*

On a clear summer evening, look for the little dolphin leaping through the dark: a compact pattern of stars southeast of Altair. One of its points is Rotanev, a name that sounds like a character from an old sea tale. Its light has crossed roughly 101 light-years to reach us, making this small constellation’s bright point feel, for a moment, like a signal sent across a vast black ocean.

Rotanev is also known as Beta Delphini and HIP 101769. The catalogue lists it as a double or multiple star, with a spectral type of F5IV and a visual magnitude of 3.63. That spectral classification describes an F-type star classed as a subgiant. Its measured parallax—32.33 milliarcseconds—provides the distance estimate: the smaller the apparent shift in a star’s position as Earth moves around the Sun, the farther away the star lies. Converting that parallax gives about 31 parsecs, or 101 light-years. These are measurements, not a picture of the system: the database’s multiple-star classification doesn’t mean a companion will be easy to pick out at the eyepiece.

Rotanev’s brightness makes it a rewarding naked-eye target under a reasonably dark sky, though city glow can wash out the smaller stars around it. Begin by finding the Summer Triangle, the broad pattern of Vega, Deneb and Altair. From Altair, scan southeast for Delphinus’s small, distinctive grouping. Its four-star outline is often likened to a diamond or a tiny kite, with a short tail trailing away. Rotanev marks one of the brighter corners. If the pattern doesn’t emerge immediately, give your eyes time to adapt, shield them from nearby lights and try again without binoculars first.

Binoculars can help separate the dolphin from the surrounding star field, but they are not a promise of a split view of Rotanev’s multiple-star system. For a first observing session, the constellation itself is the prize: a small, unmistakable figure set against a much larger sky. A telescope is optional. If you use one, start at low magnification and take in the surrounding field before centering the star.

For a more precise search, the catalogue gives Rotanev’s J2000 coordinates as right ascension 20h 37m 32.94s and declination +14° 35′ 42.3″. These coordinates are useful with a planetarium app or a telescope’s setting circles; they’re not a substitute for checking where Delphinus stands in your sky on the night you observe. The constellation is a northern-sky target, best placed for evening viewing in northern summer and early autumn.

There is something fitting about Rotanev’s quiet role: a star with a catalogued complexity, set within a constellation small enough to miss if you hurry. Find the dolphin, trace its outline, then pause on the bright point. Its apparent simplicity is only the first impression; careful looking turns a passing glance into a measured encounter with a distant stellar system.
