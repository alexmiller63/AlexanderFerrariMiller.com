# Alcyone, the Pleiades’ bright heart

*In a familiar winter star cluster, one blue-white point offers a lesson in stellar life—and a fine reason to take binoculars outside.*

Alcyone is the brightest star in the Pleiades, the close-knit-looking group that catches the eye in Taurus. On a clear evening, the cluster can seem almost like a tiny handful of stars spilled across the dark. Alcyone sits among them as a steady beacon: easy to spot, but more than a point of light.

Astronomers catalogue Alcyone as Eta Tauri. The SIMBAD database identifies it as a B7III star and a Be star, with a visual magnitude of 2.87. Its measured parallax is 8.09 milliarcseconds. Turning that angle into a distance gives roughly 124 parsecs, or about 400 light-years. These measurements translate a familiar patch of sky into a physical place: the light reaching your eye began its journey centuries ago. [SIMBAD: Eta Tauri / HIP 17702](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=HIP%2017702)

The classification hints at what the eye cannot see. “B” marks a hot, blue-white kind of star; “III” is its luminosity class. “Be” indicates a B-type star whose spectrum shows emission lines. Such stars are associated with gas around the star, often arranged in a disk. That material is not something a backyard telescope will resolve. Instead, Alcyone’s brightness and color are the visible invitation; spectroscopy supplies the deeper story.

For a first look, find the Pleiades without equipment. In the Northern Hemisphere, the cluster is a welcome evening sight during autumn and winter. Look for a compact knot of stars in Taurus, not far from the brighter orange star Aldebaran. In a city, you may pick out only the strongest points. Under a darker sky, the group opens up, and Alcyone stands out as one of its brightest members.

Binoculars are often the best next step. Brace your elbows or rest the binoculars on something steady, then sweep slowly across the cluster. The wider view keeps the stars together as a pattern and reveals more of the surrounding field than a high-powered telescope usually can. Give your eyes several minutes to adjust to the darkness; avoid looking at a bright phone screen while you wait.

Try this small observing exercise: first look at Alcyone with the naked eye, then through binoculars. Notice how the star remains a single point while its companions multiply around it. You are seeing the difference between unaided vision and a modest instrument—not a change in the stars themselves.
