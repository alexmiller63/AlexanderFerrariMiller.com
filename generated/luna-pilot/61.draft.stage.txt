# Alkaid, the Dipper’s Far-Flung Spark

*At the end of the Big Dipper’s handle, a blue-white star offers an easy spring target—and a reminder that even familiar patterns are in motion.*

On a clear spring evening, the Big Dipper can look almost too familiar to notice. Its seven bright stars make a ladle-shaped figure in the northern sky, and Alkaid marks the handle’s outer tip. Look a little longer: this is not merely the end of a pattern, but a hot, distant star whose measured position slowly shifts against the background.

Alkaid is also cataloged as Eta Ursae Majoris, or Eta UMa. SIMBAD lists it as a B3V star—a hot, blue-white star on the main sequence—and gives its apparent visual magnitude as 1.86, bright enough to see without optical aid under ordinary dark skies. Its measured parallax, 31.38 milliarcseconds, places it about 32 parsecs away, or roughly 104 light-years. Parallax is the tiny apparent change in a nearby star’s position as Earth travels around the Sun; the smaller the measured shift, the farther away the star. [SIMBAD’s Alkaid entry](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=HIP%2067301) provides the catalog data.

That distance gives the familiar point of light a different scale. Its photons began their journey long before they reached your eyes. And Alkaid is not perfectly fixed in the sky: SIMBAD reports a proper motion of about 122 milliarcseconds per year across the celestial sphere. That motion is far too small to notice from one night to the next, but across a century it adds up to roughly 12 arcseconds—still less than the apparent width of many stars in a telescope.

For a simple observing session, begin with the Big Dipper itself. In the Northern Hemisphere’s spring evenings, the pattern is prominent in the northern sky and often stands high above the horizon. Find the bowl, then follow its three-star handle outward. Alkaid is the last star at the tip. No telescope is needed; binoculars can help you pick out the pattern if the sky is hazy or you are learning the field, but Alkaid remains a point of light even at higher magnification.

Give your eyes a few minutes to adapt to the dark, and try comparing Alkaid with the handle stars closer to the bowl. The brightness and color impressions may vary with sky conditions and individual vision. Its B3V classification tells us what instruments and stellar models reveal more securely than unaided eyesight: Alkaid is a hot main-sequence star, not a small white lamp pinned to the night.

A star map can make the Dipper feel like a diagram. Alkaid makes it a place to pause. Start with the easy outline, then remember the deeper measurements behind it: a star about a hundred light-years away, moving slowly across our line of sight, visible from Earth as a bright point at the end of a familiar handle.
