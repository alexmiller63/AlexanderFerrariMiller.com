# Beta Equ: A Quiet Star on the Move

*In the faint constellation Equuleus, a fifth-magnitude star offers a useful lesson in how astronomers measure distance and motion.*

Beta Equ is not a showpiece. It does not blaze like a brilliant planet or announce itself with a striking color. Yet this modest star, listed in astronomical databases as **bet Equ**, carries a story written in the precise measurements of its position, brightness and motion.

Its apparent brightness is about magnitude 5.15 in visible light—near the threshold of unaided-eye visibility under dark skies. In a bright neighborhood, you may need binoculars or a small telescope. The star lies in Equuleus, the small constellation often called the Little Horse. To find it, use a current sky chart or planetarium app to locate Equuleus, then look for Beta Equ among its plotted stars. SIMBAD gives its J2000 coordinates as right ascension 21 hours 22 minutes 53.6 seconds and declination +6 degrees 48 minutes 40 seconds. [SIMBAD: HIP 105570](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=HIP%20105570)

Those coordinates are a celestial address, not a promise that the star will stand at exactly the same point forever. SIMBAD reports a proper motion of about 53.8 milliarcseconds per year eastward in right ascension (with the usual cosine-of-declination adjustment) and 10.7 milliarcseconds per year northward in declination. That amounts to roughly 55 milliarcseconds per year across the sky: far too little to notice in a single night, but measurable in careful comparisons separated by years.

The motion is a reminder that the stars are not fixed jewels on a sphere. They lie at different distances and travel through space in different directions. For Beta Equ, the measured parallax—an apparent shift caused by viewing the star from different points in Earth’s orbit—is 10.1648 milliarcseconds. Inverting that angle gives a distance of about 98 parsecs, or roughly 320 light-years. At that distance, the star’s slow drift across our sky corresponds to a much larger journey through space.

SIMBAD classifies Beta Equ as A3V. The first part identifies its spectral type; “V” denotes a main-sequence star, one that is generating energy through hydrogen fusion in its core. A-type stars are hotter than the Sun. The database also lists a radial velocity of −12.11 kilometers per second. The negative sign indicates motion toward the Solar System along our line of sight; proper motion describes the separate, sideways component of its apparent movement.

For observers, the best approach is simple: choose a moonless night, get away from bright lights if possible, and give your eyes time to adapt. Beta Equ’s listed visual magnitude makes it a rewarding target for a dark-sky naked-eye search, though binoculars can make the star easier to pick out and help you match the field to a chart. Don’t expect to see its motion—or its distance. Those are revealed not by a dramatic view, but by precise measurements gathered over time.

That is the quiet appeal of Beta Equ. A small point of light can be both a practical observing challenge and a reminder that the sky is three-dimensional, dynamic and measurable.
