# Cave Nebula: A Quiet Glow in Cepheus

*Sh 2-155 is an H II region whose catalogued radio outline spans only a few arcminutes. Find it in the northern autumn sky, then give your eyes time to work.*

Cepheus is a constellation of familiar zigzags and modest stars, but its faint treasures reward a slower look. Among them is the Cave Nebula, catalogued as Sh 2-155 and LBN 110.11+02.44. Its name suggests a dark hollow; its catalogue classification points instead to glowing ionized gas. SIMBAD identifies it as an H II region—a region of hydrogen ionized by energetic radiation—and records coordinates of right ascension 22h 57m and declination +62° 38′ (J2000). [SIMBAD: Sh 2-155](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=Sh%202-155)

The nebula is a useful reminder that a named object is not always a sharply defined shape. SIMBAD lists a radio angular size of 5.25 by 3.60 arcminutes, while its coordinates come from a mid-infrared measurement. These figures describe catalogued measurements, not necessarily the precise edge a visual observer will see. The catalogue also gives a local-standard-of-rest velocity of −14 kilometres per second from radio data. That number describes the gas’s motion along our line of sight relative to a standard frame; it is not a distance measurement.

For observers, the practical challenge is contrast. An H II region’s light is spread across a patch of sky, so it can be much harder to detect than a star of similar apparent brightness. A dark, transparent sky and patience matter more than magnification. The object’s northern declination makes it well placed for observers in much of the Northern Hemisphere; north of about 28° latitude, it is circumpolar in the geometric sense, never passing below the horizon. Its right ascension also brings it into convenient evening skies as autumn progresses.

**Finding it:** Use a star chart or planetarium app to locate Sh 2-155 in Cepheus, then work from nearby stars rather than trying to point directly from the catalogue coordinates. The position can help confirm the target, but a finder chart is more practical at the eyepiece. Start at low power to establish the star field, then increase magnification if needed. Give your eyes 20 to 30 minutes to adapt to darkness, shield them from white light, and observe when the Moon is absent or well away from the target. A nebula filter may improve contrast under some conditions; compare the view with and without it rather than assuming it will help.

Do not expect a bright, high-contrast spectacle. Try averted vision—looking slightly to one side of the suspected glow—and pause between checks. If the nebula does not appear, that is useful information about the night’s sky, your equipment, or your observing conditions, not a failure. The Cave Nebula is the kind of target that invites repeated visits: first to find its place among the stars, then to notice whether the sky has offered up a faint trace of the gas recorded in the catalogs.
