# MCG 5-29-85: A Spiral in Spring’s Northern Sky

*An unassuming galaxy in Coma Berenices rewards patient observing—and offers a glimpse of how astronomers describe faraway worlds.*

Some galaxies announce themselves with dramatic arms in a telescope view. MCG 5-29-85 is more reserved. Also known as NGC 4414, it lies in Coma Berenices, among the spring constellations. Its light has crossed the space between galaxies to reach us, carrying clues about both its shape and its motion.

The SIMBAD record for NGC 4414 lists its spiral classification as SAc and its apparent dimensions as about 5 by 3.2 arcminutes. That’s a modest patch of sky: roughly one-sixth the Moon’s apparent width along its longest axis. The catalog also gives a visual magnitude of 10.12. But a galaxy’s light is spread across its face, so that number doesn’t mean it will look like a bright star. In the eyepiece, expect a faint, extended glow rather than a showpiece. ([SIMBAD: NGC 4414](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%204414); [SIMBAD lookup: MCG 5-29-85](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=MCG%205-29-85))

There’s another story in the galaxy’s spectrum. SIMBAD reports a redshift of 0.002388 and a heliocentric radial velocity of about 715 kilometres per second. Redshift describes how much a galaxy’s light has been stretched toward longer wavelengths; radial velocity expresses its motion along our line of sight. These measurements are related, but a galaxy’s motion also reflects local gravitational influences. Its measured velocity alone should not be treated as a precise distance.

That distinction is a useful reminder: astronomy often turns faint light into evidence, but interpreting the evidence takes care. A classification such as SAc is a compact way to describe a spiral galaxy’s structure. A redshift is a measurement, not a complete map of where the galaxy sits in the cosmic neighbourhood.

## Finding and observing it

MCG 5-29-85’s J2000 coordinates are right ascension 12h 26m 27s, declination +31° 13′ 25″. (Coordinates and other catalog data: [SIMBAD](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%204414).) Use those coordinates in a planetarium app, star atlas or telescope’s GoTo system to locate the field. In northern mid-latitudes, the galaxy climbs high in the sky and is a good target for spring evenings.

For the best chance of seeing it, choose a moonless night, get away from bright lights and give your eyes time to adapt to darkness. A modest telescope may reveal the galaxy as a soft oval haze; a larger aperture and darker sky can help bring out its extent. Start at low or medium power to take in the whole object, then increase magnification if the view remains steady. Don’t be discouraged if the galaxy looks subtle: diffuse light is easy to lose against a bright sky, and averted vision—looking slightly to one side of the target—can help.

Its catalog number may sound less evocative than its familiar NGC name, but either designation points to the same quiet destination. Find the coordinates, settle into the dark, and let your eye gather the light. The reward is not a brilliant spectacle, but the realisation that a faint smudge is an entire spiral galaxy, measured through the traces its light leaves behind.
