# Perseus A: A Quiet Pinprick with a Restless Heart

*At the center of the Perseus Cluster, NGC 1275 gives visual observers a faint target—and astronomers a window on an active galactic nucleus.*

On a clear winter evening, Perseus rides high in the northern sky. Its brightest stars make a familiar zigzag, but the prize in this region is far less showy: a small, faint galaxy called NGC 1275, also known as Perseus A. In an eyepiece it may look like little more than a smudge. Yet this galaxy is anything but quiet.

NGC 1275 is classified as a cD galaxy—a large, luminous type often found near the centers of galaxy clusters—and as a blazar, an especially active kind of galactic nucleus. Those labels hint at two scales of activity: a galaxy occupying a central place in its surroundings, and a compact core releasing energy. Astronomers associate such active nuclei with matter falling toward a supermassive black hole. The light we see from the galaxy’s core is only one part of its story; radio observations reveal why it is known as Perseus A.

The galaxy’s recorded redshift is about 0.0168, according to the SIMBAD astronomical database. Redshift describes how much the universe’s expansion has stretched light on its journey to us. It is a clue astronomers use to study objects beyond our own Milky Way—not something an observer can detect by eye at the telescope. SIMBAD also gives NGC 1275’s J2000 coordinates as right ascension 3 hours 19 minutes 48 seconds and declination +41 degrees 30 minutes. [SIMBAD’s NGC 1275 entry](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%201275)

For observers, the challenge is modest but satisfying: finding a faint galaxy among many faint galaxies. Start with a star chart or planetarium app set to those coordinates, and look for NGC 1275 in the direction of the Perseus Cluster. A wide-field view can help orient you before you increase magnification. A dark, transparent sky matters more than chasing extreme power; stray light and haze can wash out the low-contrast glow. Give your eyes time to adapt, and use averted vision—looking slightly to one side of the target—to make a dim patch easier to detect.
