# The Splinter Galaxy: A Thin Brushstroke in Draco

*NGC 5907’s stretched outline gives observers a glimpse of a spiral galaxy seen nearly edge-on—and a reason to linger over a faint patch of sky.*

Some galaxies announce themselves with bright cores and sweeping arms. The Splinter Galaxy makes a quieter impression: a long, narrow smudge, suspended among the stars of Draco. Its catalog name is NGC 5907, and its slender shape is the first clue to the view we have of it.

The galaxy lies at right ascension 15h 15m 53.7s and declination +56° 19′ 44″, according to the SIMBAD astronomical database. SIMBAD lists its optical dimensions as about 15.5 by 1.7 arcminutes—a strikingly elongated profile—and classifies it as a spiral. Those measurements suggest a disk viewed at a steep angle, close to edge-on. We see the galaxy’s broad, flat structure largely from the side, rather than looking down onto its spiral pattern. [SIMBAD: NGC 5907](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%205907)

That perspective turns the galaxy into a narrow line of light. The line is not a solid streak, of course, but the combined glow of countless stars, softened by the immense distance between them and us. A spiral galaxy’s disk also contains gas and dust; seen edge-on, that material lies across our view of the disk rather than being arranged in a face-on whirl. The apparent simplicity of the shape is a matter of orientation: turn the galaxy, and its structure would present a different face.

SIMBAD records a heliocentric radial velocity of about 667 kilometres per second and a redshift of 0.002227. The positive velocity tells us that NGC 5907 is receding in that measurement. It is a useful reminder that the light entering an eyepiece has traveled across space while the galaxy and the Milky Way move relative to one another. The number alone, however, is not a precise distance estimate; nearby galaxies can have motions influenced by their surroundings as well as the expansion of the universe.

For observers in midnorthern latitudes, Draco is well placed during the warmer months. NGC 5907’s coordinates offer a reliable starting point for a planetarium app or telescope’s setting circles. Because the galaxy is faint and spread across a sizeable patch of sky, expect a subtle target rather than a bright showpiece. Under a dark, moonless sky, let your eyes adapt, begin with a low or moderate eyepiece power, and look for a dim, elongated glow. Averted vision—looking just to one side of the target—may make the faint outline easier to notice. Larger apertures and transparent skies help, but patience matters too.

The catalog’s B-band magnitude is 11.4, a measurement in a blue-light band rather than a promise of how bright the galaxy will look through a particular telescope. Its light is distributed along that long profile, so the view can seem subdued even when the object is well placed. Give it time: the Splinter Galaxy rewards careful looking, not a glance. What first appears to be a trace of haze becomes a whole distant stellar system, presented to us edge-on, like a fine mark drawn across the dark.
