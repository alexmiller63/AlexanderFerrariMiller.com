# Collinder 400: A Small Gathering in the Fox

*At the edge of a summer Milky Way field, this open cluster rewards a careful search—and a little patience at the eyepiece.*

Not every star cluster announces itself with a bright splash. Collinder 400, also known as NGC 6802, is a compact gathering in Vulpecula, the Fox: a subtle target whose appeal lies as much in the act of finding it as in the view itself. Its position is close to the rich star fields of the Milky Way, where the background can make a small cluster easy to overlook.

The SIMBAD database lists NGC 6802 at right ascension 19h 30m 36.2s and declination +20° 15′ 43″ (J2000). Its recorded angular diameter is about 3.34 arcminutes—small enough that a wide-field view may not immediately separate the cluster from its surroundings. SIMBAD also lists a parallax of 0.309 milliarcseconds. A simple inversion gives a distance of roughly 3.2 kiloparsecs, or about 10,500 light-years; that estimate is a useful scale, not a precise distance determination. The same record gives a radial velocity of about 12.6 kilometres per second. Together, such measurements help astronomers distinguish a real stellar grouping from a chance alignment: stars that share a distance and motion may be travelling together through the Galaxy.

Open clusters are families of stars formed from the same broad reservoir of gas and dust. Over time, gravitational encounters and the pull of the Milky Way can loosen their bonds, scattering members into the surrounding field. A cluster’s present-day appearance is therefore a moment in a longer story. Collinder 400’s modest apparent size reminds us that a stellar family can be physically extensive yet occupy only a small patch of our sky.

For observers in the Northern Hemisphere, late summer is a convenient season to seek Vulpecula in the evening sky. Use a current star chart or planetarium app to guide you to the coordinates; in a crowded Milky Way field, a precise chart is more dependable than trying to identify the cluster by eye alone. Start at low power to orient yourself, then increase magnification gradually. At higher power, the narrower field can help tease a compact grouping from the surrounding stars. Give your eyes time to adapt, and try more than one magnification: the best view may be the one that makes the cluster’s pattern easiest to recognize, rather than the one that shows the most stars at once.

SIMBAD lists a visual magnitude of 8.8, but flags that photometric entry as low quality. Treat it as a rough catalog value, not a promise about how bright the cluster will look through your telescope. Sky darkness, aperture, transparency and magnification all matter. If the field seems merely busy at first, pause before moving on. Let the stars settle into view, and the little patch may begin to look less like a random Milky Way backdrop and more like a gathering.
