# 47 Tucanae: A River of Stars in the Southern Sky

*One of the sky’s brightest globular clusters, this crowded stellar city rewards a glance with the unaided eye—and a closer look through binoculars or a telescope.*

On a clear, dark night in the Southern Hemisphere, 47 Tucanae can look like a small, soft star. Stay with it, though, and the blur becomes an invitation: a dense gathering of stars, bright against the deep southern sky. Also cataloged as NGC 104, it is classified as a globular cluster—a vast, roughly spherical collection of stars bound together by gravity.

The [SIMBAD astronomical database](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%20104) gives the cluster’s coordinates as right ascension 00h 24m and declination −72° 04′. Its listed visual magnitude is 4.09, bright enough to glimpse without optical aid under dark skies, though it may be difficult to distinguish from a star at first. SIMBAD lists an apparent diameter of about 43.8 arcminutes—larger than the Moon’s apparent width. That figure describes the cluster’s broad extent, not a sharply edged disk: its stars thin gradually toward the outskirts.

The database also lists a parallax of 0.232 milliarcseconds. Turning that value into a rough distance estimate places 47 Tucanae about 4,300 parsecs away, or roughly 14,000 light-years. At that distance, the cluster’s apparent spread corresponds to a structure spanning well over a hundred light-years. The calculation is an estimate from the listed parallax, not a direct measurement with a tape measure.

What appears as a haze to the eye is a populous system of individual stars. Binoculars begin to make the cluster’s texture more apparent, while a small telescope can reveal more of its crowded appearance. A larger instrument, used at modest magnification, can help separate stars across a wider portion of the cluster. The view depends on aperture, sky darkness, steadiness of the air, and the observer’s experience; no single eyepiece will show every star at once.

**How to find it:** Aim for the far-southern sky and use the coordinates as a guide: 00h 24m, −72° 04′ (J2000). A star chart or planetarium app can translate that position into your local sky. Observers in the Southern Hemisphere will have the easiest view; from northern locations, the cluster stays very low or never rises. Choose a site away from bright lights, let your eyes adapt to the dark, and scan slowly with binoculars before switching to a telescope. If the cluster looks stellar at first, use averted vision—look slightly to one side of it—and give your eyes a few moments to notice the surrounding glow.

The first impression may be only a fleck. But 47 Tucanae’s beauty lies in that change of scale: a point of light becoming a crowded realm, each visible glimmer part of a much larger congregation. On a patient night, the southern sky doesn’t merely offer a target. It offers a perspective.
