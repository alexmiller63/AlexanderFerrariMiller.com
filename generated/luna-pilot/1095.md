# Eltanin, the Dragon’s bright eye

*An orange giant in Draco offers an easy naked-eye target—and a reminder that a star’s apparent place in the sky can hide its true distance.*

On a clear northern night, look north of brilliant Vega for a warm point of light in the head of Draco. That is Eltanin, also known as Gamma Draconis. It is bright enough to see without a telescope, but its steady glow comes from a star far larger and more evolved than the Sun.

Draco’s winding pattern can be tricky to trace, but its head is a useful starting point. Eltanin is one of the prominent stars there, near Rastaban, or Beta Draconis. From many northern locations, the Dragon circles the sky around Polaris rather than dipping below the horizon. North of about 39 degrees latitude, Eltanin itself is circumpolar: it never sets. On a summer or autumn evening, use Vega as a guide, then scan northward with your eyes. Binoculars can help pick out the surrounding star pattern, though Eltanin needs no optical aid.

There is more to the point of light than its familiar place in a constellation. SIMBAD lists Eltanin as a K5III star. The “K” marks a relatively cool, orange-hued stellar type; “III” identifies it as a giant. Such stars have expanded beyond the stage the Sun is in now. Eltanin’s catalogue entry gives it an apparent visual magnitude of 2.23—bright by naked-eye standards—and a parallax of 21.14 milliarcseconds. ( [SIMBAD: HIP 87833 / Gamma Draconis](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=HIP%2087833) )

Parallax is the tiny apparent shift a nearby star makes against more distant background stars as Earth moves around the Sun. The measured angle places Eltanin about 47 parsecs away, or roughly 154 light-years. That is close enough, in cosmic terms, for its light to reach us in a human lifetime many times over, yet distant enough that the star’s position is measured through a shift far smaller than the width of a grain of sand seen from across a room.

For an observer, Eltanin’s appeal is its combination of accessibility and scale. You can find it with unaided eyes, then remember that the orange point is a giant star, not a nearby ember. Its catalogue coordinates—right ascension 17 hours 56 minutes, declination +51 degrees 29 minutes—also explain why it sits so high in northern skies. At latitude 51 degrees north, it passes almost overhead.

Give your eyes a few minutes to adjust, and let the constellations emerge around it. Draco is not as instantly recognizable as the Big Dipper or Orion; its long, looping body takes patience to follow. But Eltanin rewards that slower look. It is both a practical signpost in the Dragon’s head and a distant stellar sun whose light has crossed space to become a small, steady mark in our night.
