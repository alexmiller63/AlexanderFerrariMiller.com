# NGC 457: A Cluster in the Catalog

*Coordinates and measurements give this open cluster a precise address in the sky—and a reminder of what a catalog can, and cannot, tell us.*

An astronomical catalog can make a distant object feel close at hand. For NGC 457, it supplies a name, a classification, a set of coordinates and several measurements. Those details do not replace looking at the sky, but they give observers a reliable starting point and help distinguish what is recorded from what is merely suggested by a view.

SIMBAD, the astronomical database maintained by the Centre de Données astronomiques de Strasbourg, classifies NGC 457 as an open cluster. It gives the cluster’s position as right ascension 1 hour 19 minutes 32.9 seconds and declination +58 degrees 16 minutes 41 seconds, in coordinates for the J2000 reference epoch. In practical terms, these coordinates act like an address on a celestial map. They can be entered into a planetarium program or used with a star chart to locate the catalogued object. [SIMBAD: NGC 457](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%20457)

The database also lists an apparent angular size of 15 by 15 arcminutes. That is a measurement of how large the object appears across the sky, not its physical diameter. The distinction matters: an angular size describes an observation from Earth, while a physical size would require information about the object’s distance and the conversion between apparent angle and actual scale.

SIMBAD’s entry includes other measurements, too. It lists a parallax of 0.318 milliarcseconds, a proper motion of −1.586 milliarcseconds per year in right ascension and −0.661 milliarcseconds per year in declination, and a radial velocity of −11.57 kilometres per second. These numbers describe different kinds of measured motion or position information. Proper motion records a change in an object’s apparent position across the sky; radial velocity describes motion along the line of sight. A parallax is an apparent positional shift used in measuring distance.

Such figures can make a catalog entry seem definitive, but they need to be read with care. SIMBAD presents them as measurements associated with NGC 457, each with its own reference and quality information. A catalog listing is not, by itself, a detailed account of every star that may appear in the same field of view. Nor does a coordinate or motion value show what the object looks like through a particular instrument.

That is where observing adds a different kind of knowledge. A chart or planetarium program can guide a viewer to the coordinates; the view through binoculars or a telescope can then be compared with the catalog’s classification and angular extent. Equipment and viewing conditions shape what an observer can make out, while the catalog provides a consistent set of data to check against. Neither experience substitutes for the other: one is a view, the other a record of measured properties.

NGC 457’s entry is therefore more than a label. Its coordinates tell a reader where to begin; its classification identifies the type of object SIMBAD records; and its measurements invite further questions about position, scale and motion. But the entry also marks a boundary. It tells us what the database lists for NGC 457—not what every nearby point of light is, or what any observer must see. That careful distinction is part of reading the sky well.
