# MCG 7-21-37: A Quiet Spiral in the Great Bear

*An unhurried spring-night target, this faint galaxy rewards patient viewing more than magnification.*

Some galaxies announce themselves with brilliant cores or dramatic dust lanes. MCG 7-21-37 is subtler: a broad, pale glow that asks the observer to slow down. It is identified here with NGC 3184, a spiral galaxy in Ursa Major. Its measured position—right ascension 10h 18m 17s, declination +41° 25′ 28″—places it high in the northern sky during spring evenings. [SIMBAD: NGC 3184](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%203184)

The galaxy spans roughly six by five and a half arcminutes in visible light, several times the apparent width of Jupiter. But its light is spread across that area, so the total brightness recorded in a catalog can be misleading at the eyepiece. SIMBAD lists a blue magnitude of 10.4: a useful number for comparing objects, but not a promise that the galaxy will look like a bright star. In practice, its low-contrast glow is more vulnerable to light pollution and poor transparency than a compact object of similar total brightness.

SIMBAD classifies NGC 3184 as “SA,” a designation for an unbarred spiral form. Spiral galaxies are vast rotating systems of stars, gas and dust. Their arms are regions where material becomes more concentrated, helping foster the formation of new stars. At the eyepiece, however, the full structure may remain elusive. A small telescope may show only a soft oval haze; larger instruments and dark skies can make the galaxy’s uneven texture easier to explore.

The catalog also gives a heliocentric radial velocity of about 590 kilometres per second and a spectroscopic redshift of 0.00197. Redshift describes how much the galaxy’s spectral features are shifted toward longer wavelengths; in this case, the value is small. At such low redshifts, a simple conversion from velocity to distance can be uncertain because a galaxy’s own motion within its local cosmic neighbourhood affects the measurement. The figures are best treated as clues to motion, not as a precise yardstick.

To find it, begin with Ursa Major, the familiar Great Bear. Use a detailed star chart or planetarium app to navigate from nearby naked-eye stars to the galaxy’s listed coordinates; the surrounding field is more useful as a stepping-stone than a single dramatic pointer. From northern locations, spring is a convenient season, when the constellation is well placed in the evening sky. Let your eyes adapt to darkness, shield the eyepiece from stray light, and use averted vision—looking slightly to one side of the target can help faint detail register.
