# Xamidimura: A Southern Star with a Double Life

*In Scorpius, this modestly bright point of light carries the signatures of a hot star and a complicated companion.*

On a clear evening, Scorpius gathers low in the southern sky, its curved pattern anchored by ruddy Antares. Among its stars is Xamidimura, catalogued as mu.01 Scorpii. It does not need to blaze to hold attention: to the unaided eye, it is a quiet point of light, while the catalogues reveal a more intricate story.

The SIMBAD astronomical database identifies mu.01 Sco as a Be star and also lists it as an eclipsing and spectroscopic binary. Its recorded spectral classification is B1V+B. That label points to a hot, blue-white star and a companion; the binary designations tell us that astronomers have evidence of two different kinds of interaction. In a spectroscopic binary, the stars’ motion can be detected through shifts in their spectral lines. In an eclipsing binary, their alignment lets one pass in front of the other from our viewpoint, changing the system’s measured brightness.

“Be” describes a B-type star whose spectrum shows emission lines. Such stars are associated with surrounding gas, often understood as material in a disk. The classification is a clue to the system’s physics, not something an ordinary backyard view can reveal. Through binoculars or a small telescope, Xamidimura remains a point of light; its changing spectrum and eclipses belong to careful measurement.

For a night under the stars, start with Scorpius. Find Antares, then trace the constellation’s familiar hooked shape. Xamidimura lies at about right ascension 16h 51m 52s, declination 38° 02′ 51″ south, according to SIMBAD’s J2000 coordinates. A planisphere, astronomy app or star chart can help place it in the pattern. SIMBAD lists a visual magnitude near 3, making it a naked-eye target under a reasonably dark sky, though its displayed photometry is flagged as low quality. Light pollution and haze near the horizon can make the hunt harder.

Scorpius is best placed for evening observing when it is up in the southern sky during the warmer months of the year in the Northern Hemisphere. From farther south, it climbs higher and is easier to appreciate. Give your eyes time to adapt to darkness, and try to observe when the constellation is well clear of rooftops and trees. Binoculars can help you pick out the star field, but no magnification is required to meet Xamidimura.
