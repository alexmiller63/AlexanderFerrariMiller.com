# Nekkar, the Quiet Beacon of Boötes

*Look beyond the familiar arc of Arcturus and you’ll find a golden giant whose measured motion and distance turn a modest point of light into a world of its own.*

On a clear spring evening, the Big Dipper’s handle seems to swing across the northern sky and point toward Arcturus, the bright heart of Boötes. Follow the constellation farther north, and Nekkar waits—not nearly as brilliant, but easy to overlook only until you know where to look. Its light has crossed a great stretch of space to arrive as a steady, third-magnitude spark.

Nekkar is Beta Boötis, also catalogued as HIP 73555. In the SIMBAD astronomical database, its listed visual magnitude is 3.52, bright enough to see without optical aid from a reasonably dark site. The same record gives a parallax of 13.878 milliarcseconds, which puts the star roughly 235 light-years away. Its spectral classification, G8IIIaFe-0.5, identifies it as a giant star—not a twin of the Sun, but a star in a later stage of stellar life. (The “G8” describes its spectral type; “III” is its luminosity class.) [SIMBAD: HIP 73555](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=HIP%2073555)

That distance gives Nekkar’s apparent brightness a different meaning. Its light looks modest from Earth, but the star is intrinsically more luminous than a nearby star of similar apparent brightness would be. Its G-type classification also places it on the cooler side of the familiar yellow-white stars like the Sun; to the eye, it may look faintly golden, though atmospheric conditions and individual perception affect star color.

The catalog’s measurements reveal motion, too. SIMBAD lists a proper motion of about 51 milliarcseconds per year across the sky, as well as a radial velocity of −18.40 kilometers per second relative to the Sun. These figures describe a star in motion, not a fixed pin in a celestial picture. But the change in its position from one year to the next is far too small to notice by eye; the constellations shift only over long spans of human history.

**Finding Nekkar:** Start with the Big Dipper’s curved handle and follow its arc to Arcturus, the bright orange star in Boötes. Use Arcturus as a landmark, then look northward within the kite-shaped outline of Boötes for Nekkar. The constellation is well placed in northern-hemisphere evening skies during spring and summer. Nekkar is visible to the unaided eye under dark skies; binoculars can help pick it out in light pollution, but they’ll also crowd the view with many more stars.

For a precise chart search, Nekkar’s J2000 coordinates are about right ascension 15h 01m 57s and declination +40° 23′ 26″. Coordinates are useful when using a planetarium app or star atlas, while the Big Dipper’s arc is often the more satisfying route outdoors.

Give your eyes a few minutes to adapt to the dark, then pause on Nekkar. It may seem like a small destination after the sweep to Arcturus. Yet its measured distance, temperature class, and motion all belong to a real, evolving star—one whose quiet light makes the northern spring sky feel a little deeper.
