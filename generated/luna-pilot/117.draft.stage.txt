# Scheat, the Variable at Pegasus’s Corner

*An easy naked-eye beacon in the Great Square is also a cool, evolved star whose light does not stay put.*

On autumn evenings, a bright star marks the northwest corner of the Great Square of Pegasus. It is Scheat, also cataloged as Beta Pegasi: a familiar point of light with a less familiar identity. Astronomical databases classify it as a long-period variable, a star whose brightness changes over time.

Scheat lies at right ascension 23h 03m and declination +28° 05′ (J2000), placing it well north of the celestial equator. SIMBAD lists a parallax of 16.64 milliarcseconds. Converting that measurement gives a distance of roughly 60 parsecs, or about 196 light-years. Its listed spectral type, M2.5 II–III, identifies it as a cool, reddish star between the giant and bright-giant luminosity classes. In other words, this is not a small, steady sun like our own, but an evolved star with a greatly expanded outer atmosphere.

The same database gives Scheat a visual magnitude of 2.42—but that is a cataloged measurement, not a promise of how bright it will look on any particular night. The variable-star classification is the invitation to keep watching. Long-period variables change brightness over extended intervals, so a single glance can’t tell the whole story. The supplied record does not give a period or a range of brightness; it’s best to observe Scheat directly and make your own notes rather than expect a particular schedule or dramatic change.

Finding it is straightforward. In the Northern Hemisphere, Pegasus is a signature autumn constellation, though the star can be seen at other times when it is above the horizon. Look for the Great Square, a large, tilted outline of four stars. Scheat is its northwestern corner. At magnitude 2.42 in the catalog, it is readily visible to the unaided eye under a reasonably dark sky. Binoculars will make the surrounding star field more apparent, but Scheat itself does not require optical aid.

For a simple observing project, check in on Scheat every few weeks. Record the date, time, sky conditions and your impression of its brightness. To make comparisons, look at nearby stars that appear similar in brightness; variable-star observers use such comparisons to judge whether their target has changed. A printed or digital star chart with comparison-star magnitudes can make estimates more consistent. Keep the same method from session to session, and don’t be discouraged if the difference is subtle. Human eyesight and sky conditions both affect what you see.

Scheat’s appeal is that it joins two kinds of astronomy in one easy target. It is a landmark that helps you recognize Pegasus, and a changing star that rewards repeated attention. Its catalog values offer a useful starting point, but the night sky supplies the ongoing experiment: find the northwest corner, take a careful look, and come back again.
