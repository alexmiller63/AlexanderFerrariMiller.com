# Bowtie Nebula: A quiet bow in Cepheus

*NGC 40 is a compact planetary nebula with a dying star at its heart. Find it in Cepheus, then give your eyes time to pick out its faint glow.*

Some celestial objects announce themselves with sweeping arcs or brilliant color. The Bowtie Nebula is subtler: a small, dim patch in the northern constellation Cepheus, easy to pass over unless you know where to look. Its catalog name is NGC 40. The SIMBAD astronomical database identifies it as a planetary nebula and places it at right ascension 00h 13m, declination +72° 31′ (J2000)—a useful pair of coordinates to enter in a planetarium app or telescope computer. [SIMBAD: NGC 40](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%2040)

The name “planetary nebula” is a historical misnomer: these objects are not planets. They are glowing shells of gas cast off by stars late in their lives. At the center of NGC 40, the exposed stellar remnant illuminates the surrounding material. SIMBAD lists the central star’s spectral type as [WC8], a classification associated with an unusual, carbon-rich stellar atmosphere. The nebula is a brief chapter in stellar evolution, not a permanent ornament: its gas expands and disperses into space while the central star grows fainter.

The database gives NGC 40 a visual magnitude of 11.46. That number makes clear why it is a telescope target rather than a naked-eye spectacle. Even through a telescope, expect a modest sight, not the vivid, high-contrast colors seen in long-exposure photographs. The Bowtie nickname offers a hint of its form, but the first impression may be simply a tiny, soft glow against the stars.

**Finding it:** Cepheus lies among the northern constellations, and NGC 40’s high declination means it is well placed for many observers in the Northern Hemisphere. Start with a chart or app set to the object’s J2000 coordinates, then use the telescope’s finder to work into the surrounding star field. A medium-sized telescope under a dark sky is a good starting point; greater aperture can help reveal the nebula’s faint outline. If it seems stellar at low power, increase magnification and look slightly to one side of it—averted vision can make dim, diffuse detail easier to notice. Give your eyes several minutes to adapt to the dark, and avoid nearby white light.

On a crisp night, the pleasure here is partly in the contrast: a small, fragile-looking cloud that marks a star’s final transformation
