# M32: The Small Glow Beside Andromeda

*An autumn target with a compact shape and a deep-galaxy story: look closely, and this little elliptical offers a different scale of wonder.*

On a clear autumn night, find the great Andromeda Galaxy first: a long, faint smudge in the northeast for northern observers. Then look near its bright central region for a much smaller glow. That is M32, a compact elliptical galaxy and a close apparent companion to Andromeda. It is easy to overlook beside its grand neighbor, but its concentrated light makes it a rewarding target in its own right.

M32’s catalog coordinates are right ascension 00h 42m 41.8s and declination +40° 51′ 55″, placing it in Andromeda. SIMBAD classifies it as cE2—a compact elliptical galaxy—and lists a visual magnitude of 8.08. Its near-infrared apparent size is about 3.4 by 2.7 arcminutes. Those figures help explain the view: M32 is not a broad, delicate spiral spread across the sky, but a small, rounded patch of light. (SIMBAD: https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%20221)

For an observer, the best introduction is often a low-power view that takes in M31 and its surroundings. Once you have found Andromeda’s bright core, shift your attention to the nearby compact glow. Binoculars may show it under a dark sky, though it can be difficult to separate from the surrounding brilliance. A small telescope gives it more definition; increase the magnification only after locating it, since a wider field makes the search easier. The contrast between the broad, hazy galaxy and M32’s tighter oval is part of the pleasure.

Give your eyes time to adjust to the dark. Shield a nearby lamp, avoid staring at a bright phone screen, and use averted vision: look just beside the object rather than directly at it. M32’s appearance will vary with sky transparency, light pollution, and telescope aperture, so don’t expect a photograph’s detail. In a modest instrument, its light may remain a smooth, softly edged glow. That is not a failure of the view; it is the view.

The compactness that makes M32 easy to distinguish is also what makes it scientifically intriguing. Its classification sets it apart from the familiar spiral form of Andromeda. The pair also invites a useful reminder about scale: two galaxies can appear close together in the same small patch of sky while each remains an enormous system of stars. A telescope gathers their combined story into a single field, but the eye still has to learn to notice the quieter member.

Try returning to M32 after a few minutes of observing M31. Once your gaze has settled, the smaller galaxy may seem less like a stray blur and more like a distinct destination—one with its own compact outline. Sketch what you see, even with a few pencil strokes: the bright core of M31, the surrounding haze, and M32’s little oval nearby. The drawing need not be precise. Its purpose is to make you look carefully.

M32 rewards that patience. It does not fill the eyepiece or announce itself with dramatic detail. Instead, it offers a small, steady light beside one of the sky’s most celebrated galaxies—a reminder that even the faintest-looking neighbor can hold a world of its own.
