# MCG 1-32-100: A Far Island in Virgo

*An elongated galaxy hides in plain sight among the spring constellations. Find it with a telescope, and its faint glow becomes a reminder that even a small patch of sky can hold a whole galaxy.*

Somewhere in Virgo, a soft oval of light waits beyond the reach of unaided eyes. Catalogued as MCG 1-32-100 and also known as NGC 4526, it offers no dramatic flash or sharply defined spiral arms in a small telescope. Its appeal is quieter: a distant galaxy, broad enough to span several arcminutes, set against a sky crowded with other members of the Virgo galaxy region.

NGC 4526’s coordinates are about right ascension 12h 34m and declination +7° 42′ (J2000). The SIMBAD database describes it as a galaxy toward a cluster of galaxies and lists an optical size of roughly 9.6 by 3.3 arcminutes. That elongated outline gives observers a useful expectation: look for a small, diffuse oval, not a point of light. SIMBAD lists its B-band magnitude as 10.6; that measurement is in blue light, so it should not be treated as a direct prediction of how bright the galaxy will look through an eyepiece.

The database gives a measured redshift of 0.001876, corresponding to a heliocentric radial velocity of about 562 kilometres per second. Redshift is the stretching of light as an object moves away; astronomers use it to study the motions and distances of galaxies. But nearby galaxy clusters complicate a simple distance estimate from that number: galaxies also move within the cluster, so their individual velocities do not reflect only the expansion of the universe.

**Finding the faint oval**

Virgo is a rewarding spring target for Northern Hemisphere observers, when it climbs high in the evening sky. A GoTo mount can be aimed at the coordinates above. If you star-hop, begin with a detailed atlas or planetarium chart: the galaxy lies in a rich region where several faint galaxies can appear close together, and a chart helps distinguish the target from its neighbours.

Give your eyes time to adapt to darkness, and keep stray light away from the eyepiece. A telescope of about 6 inches (15 centimetres) or more, under a reasonably dark sky, is a good starting point. Use low power first to locate the field, then try moderate magnification to darken the background and examine the galaxy’s shape. Averted vision—looking just to one side of the object—may make its faint glow easier to detect. On a hazy or light-polluted night, it may remain elusive; that is a reason to try again, not a sign that the coordinates are wrong.

Once found, pause before moving on. The oval may seem modest, but its light has travelled across intergalactic space to meet your eye. MCG 1-32-100 is not a single star or a nearby cloud: it is a galaxy, one more island in Virgo’s deep celestial crowd. A steady gaze, a dark sky and a little patience are the equipment that turn its faint patch into a destination.
