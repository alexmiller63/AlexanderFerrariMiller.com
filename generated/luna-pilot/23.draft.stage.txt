# Ain: A Steady Light in Taurus

*Follow the V-shaped face of the Bull to a modest star with a giant’s story—and a place in one of the sky’s best-known clusters.*

On a clear winter evening, Taurus rises with a quiet promise: look beyond the familiar brilliance of Aldebaran, and you can find Ain, a smaller point of light set among the stars of the Hyades. Its name means “eye,” an apt companion to the Bull’s bright, watchful face. Ain may not dominate the scene, but it rewards a closer look.

Ain is Epsilon Tauri, listed in the astronomical database SIMBAD as a double or multiple star. Its coordinates—about 4 hours 28 minutes in right ascension and +19 degrees in declination—place it in Taurus. SIMBAD gives it a visual magnitude of 3.53: bright enough for the unaided eye under reasonably dark skies, though less conspicuous from a brightly lit neighborhood. The database also lists a parallax of 22.3654 milliarcseconds. Parallax is the apparent shift of a nearby star against more distant background stars as Earth moves around the Sun; that measurement puts Ain roughly 146 light-years away. [SIMBAD: Epsilon Tauri](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=HIP%2020889)

Its spectral classification, G9.5 III, offers another clue to Ain’s nature. “G9.5” describes a relatively cool star compared with the Sun, while “III” identifies it as a giant: a star that has expanded after using up much of the hydrogen fuel in its core. In other words, the steady-looking point above the rooftops is in a later stage of stellar life than our own star. A classification is not a portrait, but it gives astronomers a useful shorthand for interpreting a star’s light.
