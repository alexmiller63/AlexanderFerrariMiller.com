# Algieba: Two Suns in the Lion’s Sickle

*An easy-to-find star in Leo offers a small telescope a rewarding glimpse of a multiple-star system—and a chance to think about what starlight can reveal.*

On a clear spring evening, the Lion climbs into view, its outline drawn in stars. Follow the curve of the Sickle, the backwards question mark that marks Leo’s head and mane, and pause at Algieba. To the unaided eye it looks like one steady point. A telescope reveals a more intricate sight: a close pair of stars, shining together against the dark.

That change—from one point to two—is part of Algieba’s quiet magic. It does not need a grand nebula or a streaking meteor to make the night feel larger. Instead, it asks you to look a little more carefully. A star that seems solitary may be a system, and the telescope’s job is not only to magnify, but to separate what the eye blends together.

Algieba is Gamma Leonis. The SIMBAD astronomical database classifies it as a “double or multiple star” and lists its combined spectral classification as K1-III + G7IIIb. The Roman numeral III marks giant stars: stars that have expanded beyond the main-sequence stage of their lives. In other words, Algieba’s paired light comes from stars with a different story from our Sun’s present-day one. (SIMBAD: [HIP 50583 / Gamma Leonis](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=HIP%2050583))

The same database gives Algieba a parallax of 25.07 milliarcseconds. Parallax is the tiny apparent shift in a star’s position as Earth moves around the Sun; astronomers use it to estimate distance. Turning that value into a distance puts Algieba roughly 130 light-years away. That is close enough, in cosmic terms, for its light to reach us from a time when the world was very different—but far enough that the two stars’ combined glow arrives as a small, bright pinprick. The distance estimate is calculated from the SIMBAD parallax.

For a first visit, start with your eyes. Find Leo’s Sickle, then look for Algieba within its curved pattern. A star chart or sky app can help confirm the shape, especially if city lights wash out fainter stars. Give your eyes a few minutes to adapt to darkness, and choose a clear view with as little haze as possible. The constellation is easiest to enjoy when it has climbed well above the horizon, where Earth’s turbulent air is less disruptive.

Binoculars will make the starfield around Algieba richer, but the pair itself is a telescope target. Use a steady mount if you have one, begin at low power to centre the star, then increase magnification gradually. On a night of steady air, look for two distinct points close together. If they shimmer or seem to merge, try again when the atmosphere settles; a still image in the eyepiece can matter more than adding magnification.

Take a moment before moving on. The Sickle is an ancient pattern, but its stars are not fixed decorations: Algieba has a measured proper motion
