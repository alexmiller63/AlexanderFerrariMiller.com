# Blue Snowball: A Small, Bright Mystery in Andromeda

*NGC 7662 is a planetary nebula with a wintry nickname. Its compact size rewards patient telescope viewing—and a little care in finding it.*

The Blue Snowball is no snowball, and no planet: it is NGC 7662, a planetary nebula in Andromeda. The name hints at the cool, colored bead many observers hope to see against the dark. But this object’s real story is one of stellar endings: a shell of gas cast off by an aging star, glowing as radiation from the remnant at its center energizes the expelled material.

The label “planetary nebula” is a historical misdirection. These objects can look like tiny disks in a telescope, but they have nothing to do with planets. They mark a late stage in the lives of stars like the Sun. After such a star swells and sheds its outer layers, its exposed, hot core can make the surrounding gas shine. The Blue Snowball offers a chance to contemplate that process in a small patch of sky.

The astronomical database SIMBAD classifies NGC 7662 as a planetary nebula and gives its J2000 coordinates as right ascension 23 hours 25 minutes 53.8 seconds, declination +42 degrees 32 minutes 6 seconds. Its cataloged radio angular size is about 0.381 arcminute—roughly 23 arcseconds. That small scale is a useful observing clue: at low magnification, the nebula may resemble a star rather than an extended cloud. [SIMBAD: NGC 7662](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=NGC%207662)

For observers in the Northern Hemisphere, autumn evenings are a good time to seek it, when Andromeda is well placed. Use a star chart or a telescope’s coordinate system to work toward the listed position; the coordinates refer to the standard J2000 epoch, so a modern chart or app can handle the adjustment. Give your eyes time to adapt to the dark, and choose a night when the Moon is not washing out faint detail. A telescope is the practical tool for this compact target. Start at low power to locate the field, then increase magnification if the image remains steady and sharp.

Don’t judge the view only by color. Under a dark sky and with enough aperture, some observers may perceive a blue-green tint, but color is subjective and can be subtle in a small telescope. At modest magnification, the more dependable impression may be a neat, softly glowing disk. If it seems stellar at first, try averted vision—looking slightly to one side of it—and compare its appearance with nearby stars. A nebula’s steadier, less sharply focused look can help distinguish it.

The pleasure of the Blue Snowball is partly in the contrast between its nickname and its scale. It is a tiny target by apparent size, yet its glow comes from a vast, changing envelope of gas, seen across interstellar space. Let the view settle before moving on. In that little disk, a star’s final act becomes an observing destination.
