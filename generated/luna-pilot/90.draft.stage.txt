# Kaus Australis: The Southern Bow’s Bright Beacon

*An easy-to-find star in Sagittarius offers a bright point of entry into the science—and the slow motions—of the southern sky.*

As evening settles, Sagittarius rises like a small piece of celestial scenery: a bow, a teapot, a cluster of stars that can look almost drawn against the dark. Among them, Kaus Australis catches the eye. Its light has crossed interstellar space to reach us, but the star itself is no distant blur. It shines plainly to the unaided eye, a bright landmark in a constellation best known for pointing toward the crowded heart of the Milky Way.

Kaus Australis is also catalogued as Epsilon Sagittarii. SIMBAD gives its visual magnitude as 1.81, bright enough to stand out under ordinary dark-sky conditions. The same database lists a parallax of 22.76 milliarcseconds. Inverting that measurement gives a distance of about 44 parsecs, or 143 light-years: close enough, by stellar standards, for this beacon to feel like a neighbor rather than a remote speck. Its catalogue coordinates are right ascension 18h 24m 10.3s and declination −34° 23′ 05″ (J2000). [SIMBAD: Epsilon Sagittarii](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=HIP%2090185)

The star’s catalogue classification, B9IVp_lB?, hints at a hotter, blue-white star, while the question mark signals uncertainty in the listed type. The letters are shorthand, not a complete portrait: stellar classifications group stars by the patterns in their light, which reveal clues about temperature and other properties. Even without a telescope, Kaus Australis offers a reminder that a point of light can be measured, classified and placed in three-dimensional space.

There is a second, slower story in the catalogue. The star’s measured proper motion is about 39 milliarcseconds per year westward and 124 milliarcseconds per year southward. Those shifts are too tiny to notice from one summer to the next. Across many generations, however, stellar positions change. The chart is not the sky itself, but a record of a sky in motion.

**How to find it:** Choose a clear evening when Sagittarius is above the horizon. In northern mid-latitudes, the constellation is most conveniently placed during summer evenings; farther south, it climbs higher. Start with the familiar Teapot pattern, or use a sky chart or planetarium app, then confirm the star by its position at about 18h 24m right ascension and 34° south declination.
