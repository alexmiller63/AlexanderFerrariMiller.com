# Zubeneschamali: A Blue-White Beacon in Libra

*One steady point of light carries the measurements of a hot star—and a slow drift across the sky.*

On a clear late-spring or summer evening, look toward Libra, the zodiac constellation between Virgo and Scorpius. There, Zubeneschamali offers a quiet reward: a star bright enough to see without optical aid, yet subtle enough to invite a second look. Its name is striking; its light is more restrained. To the unaided eye it appears as a point, not a spectacle, and its color can be difficult to judge against the dark.

Zubeneschamali is Beta Librae, listed in the catalogue as HIP 74785. Its measured visual magnitude is 2.62—bright enough for naked-eye viewing under ordinary conditions, though haze, moonlight and urban lighting can make it harder to pick out. The catalogue gives its position as right ascension 15 hours 17 minutes and declination −9 degrees 23 minutes (J2000). That southerly declination does not put it out of reach for northern observers: from mid-northern latitudes, it climbs high in the sky. ****

The catalogue also gives a parallax of 17.62 milliarcseconds. Parallax is the apparent shift of a nearby star against more distant background stars as Earth moves around the Sun. Inverting that value gives a distance of roughly 57 parsecs, or about 185 light-years. The light arriving tonight began its journey when the nineteenth century was nearing its end.

Zubeneschamali’s listed spectral type is B8Vn. The “B” class identifies a hot star; “V” marks it as a main-sequence star, still producing energy through hydrogen fusion in its core. The “n” indicates broadened spectral lines. The catalogue classifies it as a high-proper-motion star, too: its listed motion across the sky is about 100 milliarcseconds per year in right ascension and 20 in declination. That movement is imperceptible in a night’s observing, but over centuries it changes a star’s place against the constellations.

To find it, first locate the familiar sweep of Scorpius, then search just westward for Libra on a star chart or a sky map. Libra’s stars are less showy than Scorpius’s bright pattern, so begin with binoculars if the sky is washed out; once you have the field, try again with the unaided eye. Zubeneschamali should stand out as a modest, steady point. Give your eyes several minutes to adapt to the dark, and avoid looking at a phone screen between attempts.

For a simple observing note, record the date, time and sky conditions, then sketch the surrounding stars as you see them. Return to the same patch in a month. The pattern will appear to have shifted west as Earth travels along its orbit, while Zubeneschamali’s much slower motion remains hidden from casual view. That contrast is part of the pleasure: the sky changes nightly, and the stars change too—just on a scale that asks for patience.
