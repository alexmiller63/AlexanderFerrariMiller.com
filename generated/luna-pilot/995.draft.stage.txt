# Izar: A Small Light with a Long Journey

*In Boötes, a steady orange-white point offers a glimpse of stellar motion, distance and the quiet work of looking up.*

On a clear spring evening, follow the curve of the Big Dipper’s handle to brilliant Arcturus, then look northeast toward Boötes. There, amid the constellation’s scattered stars, Izar holds its place like a small lamp in the dark. Its name is easy to overlook; its light is not. At magnitude 2.45 in visible light, it is bright enough for the unaided eye under ordinary dark skies.

Izar is also catalogued as Epsilon Boötis, or HIP 72105. The SIMBAD astronomical database gives its position as right ascension 14 hours 44 minutes, declination +27 degrees 04 minutes, and lists a spectral classification of K0-II-III. That classification places it among comparatively cool stars, with a luminosity class between bright giant and giant. The catalog reports a parallax of 13.8267 milliarcseconds. A simple conversion—one parsec for each arcsecond of parallax—puts Izar roughly 72 parsecs away, or about 236 light-years. The light reaching your eye tonight began its journey long before many events in human history.

Distance is not the only story written in the catalog. SIMBAD lists Izar’s proper motion as about 51 milliarcseconds per year westward and 21 milliarcseconds per year northward. That shift is far too small to notice from one night to the next, but over generations it changes a star’s position against the more distant background. Its measured radial velocity is −16.6 kilometres per second; the negative sign indicates motion toward the Sun along our line of sight. Together, these measurements remind us that the sky is not a fixed backdrop. It is a view of objects moving through space, seen from a moving planet.

For observing, start with your eyes and a sky map or astronomy app. In the Northern Hemisphere, Boötes is a familiar spring constellation, rising into the evening sky as the season advances. Use the Big Dipper’s handle as a guide to Arcturus, then sweep toward Boötes and consult a chart to identify Izar. Its coordinates place it well north of the celestial equator, so it is especially well positioned for observers at northern latitudes. From a dark site, Izar should be readily visible without optical aid; binoculars can help pick it out among nearby stars.

A modest telescope offers a closer view of the star’s concentrated light and its colour. Give your eyes time to adjust to the dark, steady the telescope on a tripod, and observe when Izar is high above the horizon, where Earth’s turbulent air has less distance to blur its image. Try returning to it on another night. The star will look unchanged, but the act of finding it again connects a familiar point of light to a measured place, distance and motion in the Galaxy.

Izar asks for no elaborate equipment or special event. It asks only for a clear patch of sky and a little patience. Its light has travelled for centuries; a few quiet minutes spent watching it are a way of meeting that long journey halfway.
