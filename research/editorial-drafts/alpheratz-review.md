# Alpheratz — the fourth corner of the Great Square

**Dek:** Find the star that links Pegasus to Andromeda, then discover how astronomers traced changing concentrations of mercury in its atmosphere.

On a clear autumn evening, look for four bright stars outlining a broad square in the evening sky. This is the Great Square of Pegasus. Three corners belong to Pegasus. The fourth, at the northeastern corner on conventional north-up charts, is **Alpheratz**—Alpha Andromedae (α Andromedae), also catalogued as HIP 677.

That shared corner makes Alpheratz an excellent starting point for exploring Andromeda. From the star, follow the chain of brighter stars extending away from the square toward Mirach and Almach. The constellation's name recalls the princess Andromeda of Greek mythology; the star pattern is a modern observing guide, not a physical grouping of neighboring stars.

## A star with an unusual atmosphere

Alpheratz looks like a steady point of light to the unaided eye. Its spectrum tells a less ordinary story. The brighter component of this stellar system is classified among the chemically peculiar **mercury–manganese (HgMn)** stars, whose atmospheric abundances differ strikingly from those of more typical stars.

A spectrum separates starlight into wavelengths. Dark absorption features can reveal which elements are present in the star's visible atmosphere. In Alpheratz, astronomers found that the strengths and shapes of mercury lines vary as the star rotates. By following those changes, they reconstructed the distribution of mercury-rich regions across the star's surface-facing atmosphere.

A study by Kochukhov and colleagues reported that these patterns evolved over seven years. Their work points toward processes involving chemical diffusion and atmospheric dynamics. The 'mercury clouds' described in this research are **not droplets of liquid mercury**: they are regions with different inferred abundances of mercury in stellar gas.

## Find it for yourself

The Great Square is easiest to recognize during Northern Hemisphere autumn evenings, though its altitude and direction depend on the observing date, time, and location. Alpheratz is visible to the naked eye under ordinary clear-sky conditions; you do not need a telescope to identify the square.

First find the four-cornered outline. Then identify the corner where a line of Andromeda's stars continues outward. That is Alpheratz. A finder chart is useful for checking orientation, especially because the square can appear tilted as it moves across the sky.

Once you have located Alpheratz, you can use Andromeda's star chain as a guide toward the **Andromeda Galaxy (M31)**. The galaxy is a vastly more distant object; sharing a constellation on our sky does not mean it is physically associated with Alpheratz.

The reward is twofold: a naked-eye landmark that helps organize the autumn sky, and a reminder that even a seemingly unchanging star can have a dynamic, chemically intricate atmosphere.
