# Schedar — the warm light in Cassiopeia

**Dek:** A bright orange giant helps trace Cassiopeia's W and offers a glimpse of a star's later life.

Find Cassiopeia's five-star W on a northern autumn or winter evening. One of its brighter points is **Schedar**, Alpha Cassiopeiae (α Cas; HIP 3179). Use a finder chart rather than “left” or “right” to identify it: the W rotates around the pole through the night, so descriptions tied to the horizon quickly become misleading.

Schedar has a warm tint compared with some of Cassiopeia's whiter or bluer stars. That color reflects its relatively cool visible surface. Schedar is an **orange giant**—a star that has expanded beyond its earlier main-sequence state.

A giant star can be cooler at the surface than the Sun yet radiate much more light overall because its emitting surface is so large. That combination makes Schedar a useful example of how color, size and luminosity fit together in stellar evolution.

You can identify Schedar without optical equipment. Binoculars may help you compare its color with neighboring stars, but a telescope will not show the star as a resolved disk in ordinary amateur observations.
