# Caph — the turning star of Cassiopeia

**Dek:** Find Beta Cassiopeiae in the familiar W and discover why its light is not quite steady.

The five bright stars of Cassiopeia make one of the northern sky's easiest patterns to recognize. Depending on the hour, the W may stand upright, tip sideways or turn into an M. **Caph**, Beta Cassiopeiae (β Cas; HIP 746), marks one end of that zigzag.

Start by locating the W, then use a chart to identify its two ends. Caph is the end nearest the Great Square of Pegasus on many autumn sky maps. Because Cassiopeia circles the north celestial pole for observers at sufficiently high northern latitudes, its apparent orientation changes dramatically through the night.

Caph is not an orange K-type giant, as the recovered pilot draft claimed. It is a relatively hot, yellow-white **F-type star**, commonly described as an evolved subgiant or giant. Its light also changes subtly: Caph is a **Delta Scuti variable**, a star that pulsates on short timescales. The variations are small enough that casual naked-eye observers usually notice the constellation's geometry rather than the star's changing brightness.

That is part of Caph's appeal. It serves as a reliable-looking guidepost while its outer layers oscillate. With a camera and careful comparison-star photometry, observers can investigate a change that is easy to miss by eye.
