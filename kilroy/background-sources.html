Reference apparatus · Historical background

Sources & Notes

The history distinguishes documented evidence, plausible reconstruction, disputed origin claims, and folklore. These sources provide the documentary foundation.

  1. U.S. National Park Service. “Kilroy Was Here,” World War II Memorial, National Mall and Memorial Parks. The NPS describes the graffiti’s wartime use, its likely combination of a British cartoon tradition with an American shipyard inspector’s name, the superspy legend, and the two hidden memorial engravings. Read at NPS.
  2. Charles Skilling. “Kilroy Was Here,” Western Folklore 22, no. 4 (1963): 276–277. A short but influential folkloric account of James J. Kilroy and the wartime phrase. The citation and its role in Kilroy scholarship are also documented by Virginia State Parks. See the Virginia State Parks references.
  3. Virginia State Parks. “Finding Kilroy in the CCC archives.” Archival investigation of Kilroy-like figures on Civilian Conservation Corps architectural drawings from the 1930s and early 1940s, including an inventory by park and analysis of work associated with architects Thomas B. Staley and Edward Meyers Jr. Read the archival study.
  4. Australians at War Film Archive. Oral-history transcript recalling “Foo was here” graffiti and a bald, long-nosed figure peering over a wall in Australian wartime service culture. Read the transcript.
  5. International Encyclopedia of the First World War. “Graffiti.” Discusses the Australian slogan “Foo was here,” accompanied by a long-nosed face peering over a wall, as soldiers’ graffiti during the First World War and notes its later reappearance. Read the encyclopedia article.
  6. Library of Congress, Moving Image Section. Catalog record for Kilroy Was Here (1947), documenting the rapid movement of the phrase into postwar popular culture. View the record.
  7. U.S. National Park Service. “The World War II Memorial in Washington DC: An Interpretive Guide.” Notes that two “Kilroy Was Here” engravings are tucked into the Atlantic and Pacific sides of the memorial as a tribute to the generation that served. Read the interpretive guide.
  8. Tim O’Gorman. “Kilroy Was Here,” Army History, no. 30 (Spring 1994): 19–21. Published by the U.S. Army Center of Military History; an important modern historical treatment of Kilroy in American wartime culture. Read at JSTOR.

Additional documentary evidence

A Historic American Engineering Record study of the Quincy shipyard summarizes the 1946 American Transit Association investigation and its conclusion that Fore River inspector James J. Kilroy marked inspected areas with the phrase. Library of Congress HAER record.

The Library of Congress Veterans History Project also preserves oral testimony from World War II veterans who remembered encountering “Kilroy was here” repeatedly during service. Such testimony is especially useful for establishing ubiquity even when it cannot settle the question of first origin.

Method

The origin of a folk symbol is unusually difficult to prove. Repetition destroys provenance: once thousands of people copy a phrase or drawing, later witnesses often remember where they first saw it but not where it began. The chapters therefore give greater weight to dated archival material, contemporary accounts, institutional records, and carefully sourced scholarship than to colorful later anecdotes.

Editorial rule“Documented” means supported by identifiable historical evidence. “Plausible reconstruction” marks an inference that fits the evidence but is not directly recorded. “Folklore” identifies stories important to Kilroy’s cultural history that should not be mistaken for verified events.

This bibliography is intended to grow. Future additions may include digitized contemporary newspapers, wartime photographs, archival shipyard material, British Chad sources, and further folklore scholarship as suitable primary or authoritative sources are located.
