Historical background

Kilroy Was Here

A three-word inscription and a face over a wall became one of the most recognizable pieces of American military folklore of the Second World War.

In its familiar form, “Kilroy was here” accompanies a bald or nearly bald man with a long nose peering over a wall, usually with both hands visible beside his face. During World War II the inscription appeared wherever American servicemen traveled: aboard ships, in barracks and latrines, on bridges, military equipment, and in both major theaters of war. The National Park Service describes it as a sign that American troops had passed through a place and left their mark.1

The joke depended on repetition. A serviceman reaching an unfamiliar or supposedly inaccessible place could discover that an anonymous predecessor had apparently arrived first. The next man could repeat the mark somewhere still farther away. Kilroy therefore had no fixed author, headquarters, or official distribution system. He was reproduced because people recognized the joke and wanted to continue it.

DocumentedThe combined phrase and peering figure were widely used by American troops during World War II, and the form was familiar enough to become part of the war’s shared servicemen culture.

One symbol, several histories

The modern image tempts us to assume that the name, phrase, and drawing were invented together. The evidence points in a different direction. The National Park Service cautiously describes the familiar American form as most likely combining a British cartoon tradition with the name of an American shipyard inspector.1

That distinction is the key to the historical problem. The phrase “Kilroy was here” has one set of origin claims. The peering figure has another history, with documented prewar examples. British Chad and Australian Foo add still older or parallel traditions. During the war these strands appear to have converged into the American Kilroy now remembered as a single icon.

This history therefore does not begin by asking only, “Who invented Kilroy?” It asks several narrower questions: Where did the name come from? Where did the phrase come from? How old is the drawing? Which claims are contemporary, which are later reconstructions, and which belong to folklore?

The answer may be less tidy than a single inventor story, but it is historically richer. Kilroy was not merely a doodle. He was a cultural form assembled, copied, and transformed by thousands of people moving through a global conflict.
