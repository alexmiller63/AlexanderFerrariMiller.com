Historical background

Kilroy Remembered

The unauthorized graffiti of wartime servicemen eventually became part of the official architecture of American remembrance.

The World War II Memorial

At the National World War II Memorial in Washington, D.C., two Kilroy engravings are deliberately tucked into corners of the memorial, one on the Atlantic side and one on the Pacific side. The National Park Service describes them as a “tip of the hat” to the generation that served and to a catchphrase that announced the presence of American troops overseas.7

The placement is unusually apt. Wartime Kilroy was encountered unexpectedly; the memorial versions are likewise not monumental centerpieces. Visitors have to notice them. The act of discovery remains part of the joke.

Documented commemorationTwo Kilroys are intentionally incorporated into the National World War II Memorial, one associated with each major theater represented by the memorial.

From graffiti to monument

The transformation is striking. Kilroy began as an unofficial mark placed on military and civilian surfaces without institutional sanction. Decades later, the same figure was intentionally engraved into one of the nation’s principal World War II memorials.

That journey says something about what the symbol came to represent. Kilroy was not a general, president, or named hero. He was anonymous. That anonymity made him suitable as an Everyman figure: a stand-in for countless ordinary servicemen whose movements, jokes, boredom, fear, ingenuity, and presence rarely entered official records individually.

What remains

The exact first author of the complete Kilroy symbol may never be known. The historical evidence instead reveals a convergence of traditions: an American phrase, a peering figure with a prewar history, British Chad, Australian Foo, shipyard lore, and wartime military culture.

What can be known with much greater confidence is what Kilroy became. He was a compact assertion of presence that could be reproduced by anybody and belong to nobody. In a war characterized by movement on an unprecedented scale, that made him the perfect anonymous traveler.

The message needed only three words:

KILROY WAS HERE
