Chapter 4 · Historical background

Before Kilroy

The peering face did not begin with the familiar American phrase. Archival evidence places closely related drawings before the United States entered World War II.

The Virginia CCC drawings

Virginia State Parks preserves an unusually important body of evidence in architectural drawings created for parks built by the Civilian Conservation Corps. Across plans dating from the 1930s and early 1940s, small bald figures peer over page edges with a long nose and fingers hanging down in a form immediately recognizable as Kilroy-like.3

The agency’s inventory found the figure repeatedly in work associated with architects Thomas B. Staley and Edward Meyers Jr., especially from 1938 through 1940. At Douthat State Park, 37 of 481 scanned materials contain a version of the figure; similar examples appear in plans for Fairy Stone, Hungry Mother, Seashore—now First Landing—and Westmoreland state parks.3

DocumentedThe Virginia drawings predate American entry into World War II and contain the peering figure, but not the phrase “Kilroy was here.”

What the drawings prove—and what they do not

The plans demonstrate that the visual motif was already circulating in American drafting culture before Pearl Harbor. They therefore make it very difficult to maintain that James J. Kilroy or a wartime serviceman invented the complete drawing-and-phrase combination from nothing.

They do not prove that Staley or Meyers created the later wartime character. Their drawings may themselves reflect a preexisting doodling tradition, technical-school joke, or visual convention. The absence of the words “Kilroy was here” is equally important: the image and phrase still appear separate at this stage.

A deeper visual ancestry

Simple faces peering over boundaries are easy to invent independently, and related figures appear in other countries under other names. That makes the search for a single first drawing especially hazardous. What historians can establish more securely is that the peering-man motif existed before the American wartime Kilroy became standardized.

The CCC evidence is therefore not a solution to the origin puzzle. It is something more useful: a firm chronological anchor showing that the visual side of Kilroy has a prewar history of its own.
