Chapter 5 · Historical background

Chad and Foo

Kilroy belonged to an international family of peering figures and “was here” jokes rather than an isolated American invention.

Mr. Chad

In Britain, a closely related long-nosed figure became known as Chad or Mr. Chad. Chad commonly appeared with captions beginning “Wot! No …?”, followed by some scarce commodity or absent amenity. Wartime shortages made the format endlessly adaptable.

British evidence places versions of the peering figure before the American wartime Kilroy became famous. The exact genealogy remains disputed, but the important point is secure: the drawing had an independent life in Britain and did not require the American name to be intelligible.

Documented patternBritish Chad and American Kilroy share a striking visual form while carrying different captions and cultural jokes.

Foo was here

Australian military culture preserves another close relative: Foo. Oral histories remember “Foo was here” accompanied by a bald or nearly bald figure with a large nose peering over a wall. An Australian wartime witness described seeing the graffiti repeatedly around service huts and recalled it as a familiar local phenomenon.4

The International Encyclopedia of the First World War goes farther, placing “Foo was here” and its long-nosed peering face in Australian soldiers’ graffiti during the First World War and noting its reappearance roughly 20 years later.5

Influence or convergence?

It is tempting to draw a straight line from Foo to Chad to Kilroy, but surviving evidence does not support such a simple family tree. Soldiers, sailors, draftsmen, and cartoonists moved between countries and institutions; jokes could travel, merge, be renamed, or be independently reinvented.

What the parallels establish is broader and more significant. The themes of anonymous presence, boundary-peeking figures, and repeated inscriptions were already available in military and popular culture. Kilroy was therefore part of a larger vernacular tradition in which ordinary people announced, humorously and often anonymously, that someone had been there before.

Still uncertainThe precise lines of influence among Foo, Chad, the American peering figure, and the phrase “Kilroy was here” cannot presently be reconstructed with confidence.
