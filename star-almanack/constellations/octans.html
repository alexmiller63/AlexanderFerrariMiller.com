Octans

An instrument around the pole

Octans names a navigator’s tool in the faint southern sky surrounding the point about which the stars appear to turn.

{% capture constellation_story %} The south celestial pole has no brilliant star to announce it. Where northern observers find the conspicuous guide of Polaris, southern observers face a much quieter center of rotation. Around that point lies Octans, the Octant. Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille placed this modern instrument in the southern sky in the eighteenth century. The choice suited both his work and the location. His constellation commemorated a device that helped people connect measurements of the heavens with their position on Earth. An octant is named for an arc equal to one eighth of a circle. Using reflecting surfaces, the instrument brings two images together—for example, the Sun and the horizon—so that the observer can measure their angular separation. That measurement can contribute to finding latitude at sea. John Hadley presented his version to the Royal Society in 1731. Thomas Godfrey independently developed a reflecting instrument in the same period. The history belongs to the continuing effort to make celestial measurements more practical on a moving vessel. The constellation therefore preserves an invention that was relatively recent when Lacaille made his southern map. It is an eighteenth-century counterpart to the older sky’s farming tools, weapons and ships: an object whose usefulness made it worthy of remembrance. Octans also offers a lesson in what a pole actually is. It is a direction defined by Earth’s rotational axis extended into space. A star near that direction can serve as a marker, but the star does not create the pole. Sigma Octantis lies close to the southern one and is often called a southern pole star; its faintness makes it much harder to use than Polaris. In a long exposure centered on the pole, stars trace circles around a point that may look almost empty. Their apparent motion comes from the turning Earth beneath the camera. A lack of bright stars does not make that center any less precisely defined. The instrument and the location consequently belong together in a useful way. Navigation depends on measured relationships, including relationships to points that are not themselves luminous objects. Octans gives a name to the sky around one of those invisible reference points. Its story begins with a faint field and a practical device.
