Pyxis

A compass beside an ancient ship

Pyxis brings an eighteenth-century instrument into a neighborhood shaped by the much older figure of Argo.

{% capture constellation_story %} Pyxis sits beside the surviving parts of the celestial ship Argo, making an attractive story almost unavoidable: surely this must be the Argonauts’ compass. The history requires a more careful distinction. Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille introduced the constellation in the eighteenth century as the mariner’s compass. It belonged to his new southern figures honoring contemporary instruments. Carina, Puppis and Vela became the three recognized divisions of Argo Navis; Pyxis was a separate named addition in the same nautical neighborhood. The territory nevertheless has older connections with the ship. Historical reconstructions identify stars now assigned to Pyxis with the mast described in Ptolemy’s catalog. The image and extent of Argo changed over centuries, so a statement about one period’s ship should not automatically be applied to every later drawing. That is why the constellation can be both a modern compass and a place whose stars once had a role in an ancient vessel. The modern figure does not establish that Jason navigated by magnetic needle. It records a later mapmaker’s choice. A mariner’s compass supplies a direction through magnetism. Its needle aligns with the local horizontal component of Earth’s magnetic field. That direction, magnetic north, need not coincide with geographic north. The difference is called magnetic declination, and it varies with location and time. A navigator using a compass and a chart must understand which north each represents. The instrument’s usefulness depends on interpreting it correctly, not simply trusting that every northward arrow means the same thing. Placed among Lacaille’s other devices, Pyxis belongs to the effort to make travel and measurement more dependable. The sky itself provided direction, time and angular measurements; a magnetic compass offered another kind of reference. The constellation’s faint stars form a modest figure. They do not compete with the larger outlines or the brilliant lights elsewhere in this region. Its attraction is the meeting of histories: an old ship, a newer instrument, and the changing decisions of people who drew the southern sky. Let the compass keep its own date. It can sit near Argo without becoming equipment from the heroic age.
