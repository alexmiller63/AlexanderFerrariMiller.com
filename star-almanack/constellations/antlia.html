{% capture constellation_story %} ## A machine in the company of myths An air pump is an unexpected thing to find on a star map. The instrument belongs beside a laboratory bench: a cylinder, a handle, a chamber from which air can be withdrawn. Yet there it is among the animals, heroes, and royal families of the constellations. Antlia asks us to remember that people have celebrated more than legendary deeds in the sky. They have also celebrated the tools that let them ask new questions. Its name is usually rendered in English as the Air Pump. The object intended is an early apparatus for vacuum experiments, rather than a pump for inflating a tire. Its purpose was to remove air, making it possible to investigate circumstances that ordinary life rarely provides. ## Lacaille's southern workshop The constellation comes from the work of Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille, the French astronomer who observed the southern sky from the Cape of Good Hope in the early 1750s. His additions to European celestial cartography included numerous scientific and artistic instruments. Antlia belonged to that deliberate choice of subjects. The original French name, *la Machine Pneumatique*, became the Latin *Antlia Pneumatica* and was eventually shortened to Antlia. In Lacaille's drawing, it was a single-cylinder pump of the kind associated with Denis Papin's vacuum experiments. Johann Bode later illustrated a double-cylinder version. Even a machine among the stars could be redrawn as instrument design changed. The point was not that the stars naturally resembled this particular apparatus. Naming and drawing supplied the connection. Antlia records an eighteenth-century decision about what deserved a place in an atlas. ## The experiment behind the picture We are surrounded by air so continuously that it is easy to overlook it as a physical substance. Removing some of it from a chamber turns that familiar background into something whose effects can be investigated. The pump embodies a change of conditions: create a different environment, observe what happens, and compare. That makes the constellation's subject especially apt for a history of science. A useful instrument does more than measure an answer already anticipated. It can make a previously inaccessible question practical. Antlia's story concerns that relationship between a device and the experiments it permits. There is no ancient Greek rescue or transformation attached to this constellation's creation. Its historical interest lies in the decision to place experimental equipment where earlier mapmakers had placed gods. ## Looking for a quiet constellation Antlia is faint; none of its stars reaches third magnitude. A chart and a clear southern view are more useful than the expectation of an unmistakable outline. Its brighter stars include Alpha and Epsilon Antliae, both orange stars. The region also contains NGC 2997, a spiral galaxy. Its presence offers a pleasing change of scale: the constellation commemorates an instrument for studying what happens inside a laboratory chamber, while a telescope directed into the same region can reach another galaxy.
