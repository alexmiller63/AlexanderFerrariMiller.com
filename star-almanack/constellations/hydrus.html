Hydrus

A southern snake with a separate history

Hydrus belongs to the southern constellation scheme of Dutch voyagers, sharing a watery name with Hydra but not its ancient battle.

{% capture constellation_story %} ## Similar words, different figures Hydrus is the Lesser Water Snake, a small far-southern constellation. Hydra is the much larger ancient Water Snake elsewhere in the sky. The grammatical distinction is often rendered as male and female, but it does not establish a mythological family. Hydrus should not automatically inherit the many heads, venom, or encounter with Heracles associated with Hydra. Recognizing the separate histories is more useful than treating the names as two sizes of one character. ## A figure from voyages Hydrus belongs to the southern group associated with observations by Pieter Dirkszoon Keyser and Frederick de Houtman in the late sixteenth century. The resulting figures appeared on globes made by Petrus Plancius and his collaborators, then in the atlas tradition represented by Bayer's Uranometria. A water snake fitted the natural-history subjects of that group. Its significance concerns what European sailors and mapmakers chose to represent, rather than the recovery of an otherwise lost Greek adventure. The stars themselves were already visible to southern peoples. The dated innovation is this particular figure's entry into European cartography. ## Between the Clouds Hydrus lies in the wider region between the Large and Small Magellanic Clouds. Those galaxies make the surrounding sky especially important to southern observers, even though they are not parts of the Snake. Their broad appearances provide context for learning a modest stellar figure nearby. A useful chart can show the target without stripping away the large landmarks that establish its position. The name Hydrus also recalls a time when the region's outlines were still being adjusted in European atlases. Later southern figures, including Octans, altered the allocation of stars near the pole. A constellation's modern extent is therefore not necessarily identical to every historical drawing of its tail. ## What the name preserves Hydrus has no need for a borrowed heroic battle. It preserves an authentic account of observation, organization, publication, and later revision. That account becomes more concrete when one compares the small current figure with its neighbors. The great external galaxies, the southern pole's neighborhood, and the older River all supply a setting larger than the Snake itself. The reward is partly a clearer vocabulary. Two water snakes can occupy the same atlas while referring to different periods and different ways of imagining the sky.
