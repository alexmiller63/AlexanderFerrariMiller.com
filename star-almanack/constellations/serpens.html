Serpens

One serpent, seen in two parts

{% capture constellation_story %} Serpens can look like an exception before its story is known. Its head appears on one side of Ophiuchus and its tail on the other. These are Serpens Caput and Serpens Cauda, two separate regions belonging to one official constellation. The historical drawing makes the arrangement easier to understand. Ophiuchus is the Serpent Bearer, and the snake passes through his grasp. A modern boundary divides the picture without requiring two different animals. In the influential identification of Ophiuchus with Asclepius, the serpent became part of the imagery of medicine. One tradition explained the relationship through an act of observation. The healer saw a snake revive another snake with an herb, then learned to use the plant himself. The story gives the animal an unusual role. It is a source of knowledge, showing a human practitioner something he does not yet understand. The healer’s achievement begins with watching another creature and recognizing that its action can be learned. The greater story of Asclepius makes the consequences dangerous. His ability to restore the dead provoked divine opposition, and Zeus killed him with a thunderbolt. His eventual celestial commemoration preserved both the physician and the serpent associated with his powers. These are mythological accounts, with variations among ancient sources. They do not establish that a real plant could perform the miracle described. Their interest lies in the way a serpent, so often treated as threatening, could also carry knowledge of renewal and healing. The two parts of Serpens contain very different astronomical attractions. In the tail’s region lies the Eagle Nebula and its associated young star cluster, known together through the Messier 16 designation. This is the setting of the famous Pillars of Creation. The pillars are dense structures of gas and dust within a star-forming environment. Radiation from nearby young stars illuminates and erodes their surfaces. Photographs reveal details and wavelengths unavailable to a simple naked-eye view of the constellation. A serpent drawn across the sky and a pillar photographed inside a nebula are both names based on resemblance, but they describe different kinds of thing. The snake is an inherited figure linking selected stars; the pillars are physical concentrations of interstellar material. The Almanack’s two portraits lead to this one story because the ancient scene still holds together.
