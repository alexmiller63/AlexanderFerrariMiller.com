Crux

A small cross with a wide horizon

Crux turns four conspicuous stars into a southern landmark, while the dark Coalsack beside them offers another way to read the Milky Way.

{% capture constellation_story %} ## Small on the map, prominent in the sky Crux, the Southern Cross, is the smallest of the official constellations by area. Its importance is out of proportion to that small allotment of sky. Four bright stars make the principal cross. Alpha and Beta Centauri, commonly called the Pointers, help lead an observer toward it, although those stars belong to Centaurus rather than Crux. The distinction is useful. A recognizable pattern, its neighboring guide stars, and its official constellation do not need to have identical membership. They work together as an observing route. ## Separated from the Centaur The stars now assigned to Crux were included in the ancient constellation Centaurus. The separate Cross emerged in the history of European globes and maps much later. It is misleading to assign the whole story to one late-seventeenth-century inventor. Cross imagery had appeared before then, including on late-sixteenth-century globes and in Bayer's treatment of the Centaur. Its visibility from Mediterranean latitudes also changed over long periods because of precession. A sky figure known from one historical setting could become inaccessible from places where later readers tried to recognize it. For people living farther south, however, these stars were not lost and rediscovered. The European story is one history of naming and mapmaking within a sky that other communities continued to observe. ## Finding a direction, not a pole star The long axis of the Cross provides a guide toward the south celestial pole. The pole is not one of the four main stars, and the Cross is not itself a bright southern equivalent of Polaris. Extend the axis beyond the foot of the Cross to approach the pole's location. Practical methods combine this with the Pointers or other references, rather than treating the star at the foot as the exact destination. A larger nearby pattern called the False Cross can cause confusion. Knowing the surrounding constellations and Pointers is more reliable than selecting any four stars that seem to make a cross. Once the correct figure is recognized, its changing orientation through the night becomes meaningful. It turns around the pole while retaining the relationship that makes it useful. ## The dark shape beside the lights The Coalsack is a dark nebula seen against the bright background of the Milky Way. Its apparent emptiness is produced by obscuring material, not by a literal hole through the Galaxy. It also participates in a different way of making celestial pictures. In some Australian Aboriginal sky traditions, the Coalsack forms the head of an Emu traced through dark regions of the Milky Way. That is not simply another name for the four bright stars of Crux. It organizes a different kind of visual evidence: darkness against a luminous background. The neighboring views make Crux especially valuable for learning how people read the sky. A cross of stars can supply a direction, a separated modern constellation can preserve an older map's history, and an obscuring cloud can become the central feature of another figure. Looking at both the light and the dark makes the region much richer than a four-point symbol. ## Sources - [ABC Science — Southern Cross: Crux, the Pointers, and the Coalsack](https://www.abc.net.au/science/archive/articles/2011/03/24/southern-cross.htm) - [Richard Hinckley Allen, Star Names, hosted by the University of Chicago — Crux: historical depictions](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Topics/astronomy/_Texts/secondary/ALLSTA/Crux%2A.html) - [Courtney Seligman — Crux and the ancient Centaur](https://cseligman.com/text/atlas/crux.htm) - [Astronomical Society of Southern Africa — Celestial Direction Finding](https://assa.saao.ac.za/wp-content/uploads/sites/23/2024/01/Celestial-direction-finding.pdf) - [ESO — The Southern Cross and the Coalsack's place in the Emu](https://messenger.eso.org/public/images/yb_southern_cross_cc/) {% endcapture %}{{ constellation_story | markdownify }}
