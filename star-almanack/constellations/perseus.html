Perseus

A hero carrying a dangerous victory

Perseus crosses the sky with Medusa’s head, a weapon whose power survived the monster’s death.

{% capture constellation_story %} Perseus is usually drawn holding the head of Medusa. It is a disturbing emblem for a rescuer: the proof of one victory has become a weapon that can still destroy anyone who looks at it. His story began under a prophecy. Acrisius feared that his daughter Danaë would bear a son who would kill him. Confinement failed to prevent the birth of Perseus, whose father was Zeus. The king set mother and child adrift in a chest, but they survived and reached Seriphos. There, the grown Perseus was sent to obtain Medusa’s head. The task was designed to be impossible. Medusa’s gaze turned those who met it to stone, so strength alone offered no safe way to approach her. Divine equipment and advice made the difference. With winged sandals, a concealing helmet and a weapon suited to the task, Perseus reached the Gorgon and killed her without looking directly at her face. The head retained its power after the beheading. On his return, he encountered Andromeda exposed to a sea monster. Her danger belonged to the punishment that followed her mother Cassiopeia’s boast. Perseus saved her, and the rescue connected his story with a whole neighborhood of constellations: Andromeda, Cassiopeia, Cepheus and Cetus. The helmet, sandals and other gifts vary in emphasis across ancient accounts. They should not be replaced automatically with a ride on Pegasus. Although Pegasus was born when Medusa was killed, the best-known ancient rider of the winged horse was Bellerophon. Later images can bring the heroes and horse together in ways that differ from the older narratives. In the constellation, Algol marks the Gorgon’s head, often shown as her eye. It is famously variable. In the principal eclipsing pair, one star passes in front of the other as seen from Earth, causing the system’s light to diminish at regular intervals. That physical explanation makes Algol especially rewarding to follow. The change can be detected through repeated comparisons with neighboring stars. Its ominous traditional associations and its measurable cycle belong to different ways people have interpreted the same light. Perseus’ celestial portrait consequently holds more than a triumphant pose. It carries the aftermath of a dangerous encounter, including a power that remains hazardous even after victory.
