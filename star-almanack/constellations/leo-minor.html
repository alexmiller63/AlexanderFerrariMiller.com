{% capture constellation_story %} Above the great curve of Leo’s mane lies a much less assertive animal. Leo Minor occupies the space between the larger lion and Ursa Major. Its name announces the comparison before an observer has even found its stars: this is the Lesser Lion. Johannes Hevelius created the constellation in the seventeenth century, and it appeared in his atlas of 1690. The figure therefore has a different ancestry from neighboring Leo. The older lion belongs to the classical sky and the stories of Heracles. The younger one came from an astronomer arranging faint stars into a new image. It is tempting to supply a family relationship: perhaps this is the great lion’s cub, or an animal following it through the heavens. Such a picture can help someone remember the location. It should not be mistaken for an inherited Greek myth. The documentary history gives us Hevelius’ invention, without an ancient episode explaining how a second lion arrived. That modest beginning is part of the constellation’s character. In a region bordered by two celebrated animals, a small lion was a way to make a quieter territory recognizable. A map does not need every figure to command attention equally. Some names serve best as a bridge between landmarks. Its brightest star is 46 Leonis Minoris, known as Praecipua. The name conveys its principal position in the figure. Yet a star can lead its own constellation without becoming one of the dominant lights in the surrounding sky. Under less favorable conditions, the larger patterns nearby will be much easier to pick out. Leo Minor also illustrates the difference between the drawn animal and the constellation used by astronomers. The modern constellation is a bounded region of sky. Its stars do not have to make a persuasive lion for an object within those borders to receive a Leonis Minoris designation. The picture supplies a memorable name; the boundaries supply a shared address. To find the lesser lion, begin with the greater one and look north toward the Great Bear. There is a small pleasure in learning a place that does not reveal itself immediately.
