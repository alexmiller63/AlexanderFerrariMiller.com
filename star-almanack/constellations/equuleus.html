Equuleus

A little horse with several pedigrees

Equuleus places a small equine head beside Pegasus, but the surviving explanations do not agree on which horse it represents.

{% capture constellation_story %} ## Beside the larger horse Equuleus means the Little Horse or Foal. Its small, faint pattern sits near the much more extensive Pegasus, so the comparison is built into both the names and the surrounding atlas. Traditional illustrations usually show only a horse's head and neck. A modern observer should not expect a complete four-legged animal hidden among the few principal stars. The distinction helps explain the figure's modest scale. The subject is recognizable as part of an animal, rather than an entire tiny horse squeezed into the available space. ## A family resemblance is not a settled origin One later explanation identifies the figure with Celeris, associated with Pegasus and given by Hermes, or Mercury, to Castor. Other accounts connect it with Cyllarus, a horse belonging to Pollux. These alternatives fit the strong horse associations of the Twins, but they are not equivalent names for a single securely documented event. The surviving traditions offer different pedigrees. Another explanation invokes Hippe, a daughter of Chiron who becomes a mare. The resemblance between horse-related names and figures encouraged connections that require careful attention to which author says what. It is better to retain the uncertainty than to make the Little Horse simply Pegasus's child in every telling. Familiarity is not proof of a single genealogy. ## An ancient place, a less certain biography Equuleus belongs to the constellation tradition recorded by Ptolemy. That establishes its presence in an ancient astronomical catalog more securely than it establishes one particular myth as the reason for its invention. A catalog and a myth answer different questions. One can tell us that a small figure was recognized and described; the other tries to explain the identity of the depicted creature. The gap is especially visible here because the picture is simple and its stars are not particularly conspicuous. A short label can survive more firmly than an extensive account of its origin. ## Learning the small interval Use the larger neighboring figures to approach Equuleus, then look for the modest group defining the head. A dark sky helps distinguish it from the background. The exercise illustrates how a constellation can be useful without dominating a wide panorama. It gives a small interval an identity and lets observations within that interval be described consistently. Its story is consequently one of restraint as much as recognition. There is an old horse-headed figure, there are several proposed mythological identities, and the evidence does not require them to be joined into a single narrative.
