Triangulum Australe

The triangle of the far south

Three conspicuous stars gave voyagers and mapmakers a simple figure to carry onto the southern celestial globe.

{% capture constellation_story %} Triangulum Australe does not need a long outline to establish itself. Three principal stars form the Southern Triangle, a compact geometrical landmark near the rich star fields around Centaurus and Ara. Its European mapping history belongs to the late sixteenth century. Observations associated with Pieter Dirkszoon Keyser and Frederick de Houtman helped Petrus Plancius place the figure on his 1598 globe. It became one of the southern constellations that entered the atlas tradition through Dutch voyages. This was not the first figure Plancius had called a Southern Triangle. An earlier use of the name referred to a different grouping. The eventual constellation emerged from the process of comparing observations and settling images, rather than appearing fully standardized in one stroke. The triangle’s practical value is easy to appreciate, but the original observers did not leave a clear explanation of every reason for their choices. It is reasonable to see it as a useful landmark; it is less reasonable to invent a specific navigational maneuver and claim that the stars were named for it. Nor should it be confused with Triangulum in the northern sky. The northern figure belongs to classical Greek astronomy, while this particular southern constellation entered European globes much later. Their common geometry does not give them the same history. The brightest corner is Atria, a star with a noticeable orange hue. Its name is a compact construction from Alpha Trianguli Australis, the designation for the principal star of the Southern Triangle. It sounds ancient, but its form is rooted in the abbreviated catalog name. Nearby within the constellation lies NGC 6025, also called Caldwell 95. It is an open star cluster that can be explored with binoculars. Lacaille discovered it during his southern observing work in the eighteenth century, adding another chapter of measurement and discovery to the region. The three corners of the constellation and the members of the cluster represent different kinds of grouping. The triangle is an apparent shape chosen on the sky. An open cluster is a population of stars with a shared physical history. That is enough story for a geometrical figure: travelers recording a less familiar sky, mapmakers deciding where a name belonged, and observers finding a real cluster within the adopted region.
