Chamaeleon

A quiet lizard above hidden nurseries

Chamaeleon brings a real animal into the far southern atlas, while dark clouds in its direction conceal stars still taking shape.

{% capture constellation_story %} ## A creature from the natural world Chamaeleon is the Chameleon, the lizard familiar for its distinctive eyes, grasping feet, and changing colors. The constellation belongs to the natural-history subjects that European mapmakers introduced into the southern sky around the end of the sixteenth century. Its faint stars lie near the south celestial pole. From many northern homes, they never rise. An observer can know the name from an atlas for years without having had an opportunity to see the figure itself. The absence of a bright landmark suits the animal's reputation for concealment, but that is an association a modern reader may make. It is not evidence that the figure's creators chose the region for that reason. ## Ships, observations, and globes The constellation belongs to the group associated with observations by Pieter Dirkszoon Keyser and Frederick de Houtman during Dutch voyages to the East Indies. Petrus Plancius used southern observations in making the constellation scheme displayed on a globe of 1598. Later publication helped establish the figures. Chamaeleon appeared with the southern group in the atlas tradition that followed, including Bayer's early-seventeenth-century work. These were changes to European maps. They do not establish that the sky was previously unknown to people living beneath it. The historical claim concerns the creation and circulation of this particular named figure. A chameleon offered a different kind of subject from the heroes and transformed beings of older Greek constellations. It represented an animal of interest to naturalists and travelers. Its atlas history does not require an otherwise unattested ancient myth. ## What darkness can contain Within the direction of Chamaeleon are clouds of interstellar gas and dust. Some appear dark in visible-light images because dust obscures the light behind them. A dark patch is therefore not necessarily an empty patch. The Chamaeleon cloud complex includes regions of active star formation, and observations at different wavelengths reveal structures that ordinary visual inspection cannot. This is a particularly useful region for changing one's expectations of a star map. The bright points indicate some of what can be seen easily, while the obscuring material can mark places where new stellar objects are forming. The clouds extend across more than one constellation. Their physical boundaries were not arranged to fit human divisions of the sky. ## A view that changes with wavelength ESA's Herschel observations of Chamaeleon I show the star-forming region in far-infrared light. Hubble images reveal other features, including reflection nebulae and glowing structures associated with young stars. These views are not interchangeable photographs made with slightly different cameras. They emphasize different physical components and processes. Reading an image requires knowing what kind of light was measured. The lizard itself does not explain any of this activity. It gives the region a memorable name, while the clouds supply a physical investigation quite independent of that name. Chamaeleon consequently offers an unusual combination: a faint animal figure introduced through voyages and printed maps, and a nearby astronomical laboratory whose apparent darkness conceals important activity.
