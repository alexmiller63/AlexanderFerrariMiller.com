# Start Here

*A reader’s guide to the Star Almanack*

## Use your birthday

Your birthday is first a season: a place in Earth’s yearly journey around the Sun. Think of the time of year it belongs to—the changing daylight, the weather, and the stars that return to the evening sky.

The Almanack marks the seasons by the equinoxes and solstices. It uses Northern Hemisphere names: Spring begins at the March equinox, Summer at the June solstice, Autumn at the September equinox, and Winter at the December solstice. In the Southern Hemisphere, the local seasons are reversed.

The stars of your birthday season give you a whole sky to explore. They are generally well placed for evening viewing throughout that season, so you can enjoy them on many evenings around your birthday. Which ones you can actually see depends on your latitude, horizon, and sky conditions.

Use the date you already know by heart to explore your season of the sky.

1. **Find your birthday’s season.** Open the [Calendar](/star-almanack/calendar.html), enter your birth month and day, and choose a year from the available 2025–2027 calendars. Open the birthday week, then use its season links to explore the season containing your birthday. You do not need to enter your birth year. If your birthday is close to an equinox or solstice, check the date’s season-and-day label: the season can change within that week.
2. **Explore the season’s sky.** Browse the seasonal calendar. Which constellations, stars, and deep-sky objects have their best-visibility dates in this part of the year? Follow something that interests you into its Sky Note, article, or finder chart.
3. **Explore three zodiac months.** Find the zodiac month containing your birthday, then include the preceding and following zodiac months. The Almanack shows which stars and other objects are well placed by date; this three-month range gives you ample viewing around your birthday. Browse the best-visibility entries across that whole range and follow their links to the stories and charts. Your birthday’s exact day is a starting point within this wider observing window. Also check the Moon and any events nearby.
4. **Try another year.** The seasonal sky returns around your birthday, while the Moon and dated events change. Compare the years to see both the returning patterns and the differences.

You are looking at the sky around your birthday in the year you choose. To see what you can observe now, return to the converter and enter today’s date.

The Star Almanack helps you connect a date on the calendar with something to find in the sky. From your first week, you can explore a season, learn a constellation, or follow a story to its chart. You do not need to understand the calculations before you begin.

Before going outside, check your circumstances: latitude, horizon, local time, weather, light pollution, and the Moon all affect what you can see. For calculations at your location, use [Ephemera](/star-almanack/ephemera.html).

The previous and next week buttons move through the calendar; the season links take you to the surrounding seasons. “Current week” in that navigation means the week you have opened.

## Weeks, years, and seasons

**The ISO week is the basic calendar unit.** It runs Monday through Sunday. In `2026-W20`, 2026 is the ISO week-numbering year and W20 is week 20. Near New Year, the ISO year can differ from the civil year printed beside the date.

A year and a season are ways to browse those weeks. A seasonal page stacks the complete ISO weeks that cover the season, so its first and last weeks can contain days from the neighboring season. The season-and-day label tells you where each date belongs.

Seasons follow the equinoxes and solstices. The labels use the Northern Hemisphere convention: March begins Spring, June Summer, September Autumn, and December Winter. Southern Hemisphere readers should reverse the seasonal weather association. Read the dates shown on the page, especially for winter, which crosses New Year.

The year buttons open full-year calendars. The previous, current, and next season buttons are centered on the period you are viewing; they are useful for moving between the weekly detail and the larger seasonal picture.

## Reading the calendar

Each row connects a civil date with a zodiac day and the events assigned to that date. Entries can include solar ingresses, lunar phases, equinoxes, solstices, meteor activity, and best-visibility dates for stars or deep-sky objects.

Separate boxes within an event cell distinguish separate entries. Follow an object’s link for its accompanying material. An empty cell means no tracked entry is assigned there; it does not mean there is nothing to observe.

**Zodiac signs and constellations are different.** The zodiac-day column uses twelve equal 30° divisions of the tropical zodiac. Aries begins at the March equinox. The official constellations are unequal regions of the sky. A zodiac date is a calendar coordinate, not a claim that the Sun is inside the constellation of the same name.

**Read the time label.** UTC is a common worldwide clock; converting to your local time can change the civil date. Local apparent solar time follows the actual Sun and differs from your clock time. Use the stated time convention rather than assuming every time is local.

## What “best visibility” means

For fixed objects, the Almanack’s evening landmark is culmination: crossing the local meridian at approximately **9 PM local apparent solar time**. This gives an annual date around which an object is conveniently placed for evening observing.

It is a planning landmark. You can often observe the object for weeks or months around that date. The date alone does not establish that it rises at your latitude, clears your horizon, or is visible through city lights. A southern object may remain below a northern observer’s horizon.

For the practical question “Can I see it here tonight?”, combine the calendar with the object’s chart, your location, and the conditions outside.

## Symbols, names, and brightness

Use the notation controls wherever they appear:

- **Greek/Symbols** gives compact astronomical notation.
- **English** spells out the corresponding names.
- **Mixed Learner** pairs symbols with words to help you learn them.

Controls can belong to an individual section or chart. If another section still uses a different notation, look for its own controls. The [Front Matter](/star-almanack/front-matter.html) provides a longer symbol key and explanatory background.

A designation such as **α Cyg** identifies a star by its Bayer letter and constellation abbreviation: alpha in Cygnus. A proper name, such as Deneb, may appear alongside it. Abbreviations in star designations serve a different purpose from the full constellation names used in prose.

The magnitude scale runs backward: smaller numbers mean brighter objects. A variable-star marker identifies a star whose brightness changes. Calendar entries simplify brightness information; charts can show finer precision to help compare neighboring stars. Missing a printed magnitude does not mean the object has no measured brightness.

Eye, binocular, and telescope markings indicate observing aids. They are guidance: sky darkness and the object’s size and surface brightness matter as well as its listed magnitude.

## Sky Notes and finder charts

Sky Notes explain the objects and events associated with a week. Read the short introduction, then open the linked article for its fuller account, finder material, and sources.

A finder chart shows relationships among a target and recognizable sky landmarks. Begin with the widest view, identify a familiar pattern, then use an inset or closer view if provided. Chart labels and captions tell you what area or view is being shown.

A **Planet Finder** places planets among reference stars. It is useful for understanding their celestial arrangement. For your local horizon or rise/set circumstances, use the location-specific calculations in Ephemera. A planet’s presence on a chart does not by itself mean it is above your horizon or observable in darkness.

## Three ways to use the atlases

- **[Panoramic Sky Atlas](/star-almanack/chart-library/panoramic/):** start wide to understand a large region of the sky and the relationships between constellations.
- **[Constellation Atlas](/star-almanack/chart-library/constellations/):** examine a constellation’s stars, adopted figure, and surrounding boundaries.
- **[Asterism Atlas](/star-almanack/chart-library/asterisms/):** learn familiar patterns that can lie within one constellation or cross several.

A constellation is an officially bounded sky region. An asterism is a recognizable star pattern. A constellation’s stick figure is a drawing convention, distinct from its boundary.

The [Constellations collection](/star-almanack/constellations/) tells the names, meanings, and histories of all 88 constellations. The atlas is a separate chart collection. Serpens has one history article and two chart portraits, Caput and Cauda. The [Front Matter explanation](/star-almanack/front-matter.html) discusses Serpens, Mensa, and Microscopium.

## Eclipses, meteors, and the Moon

Use the [Eclipse collection](/almanack/eclipses/) for the different kinds of eclipse and the individual event pages. An eclipse date does not mean the event is visible everywhere; check its visibility information and timing. For solar observing, follow the collection’s eye-safety guidance before looking toward the Sun.

Use [Meteor Showers](/almanack/meteors/) to learn the radiant and the expected conditions around a shower’s peak. A reference rate is not a promise of how many meteors you will see: moonlight, darkness, radiant height, and observing time affect the result.

The calendar’s principal lunar phases help you follow the Moon and plan around moonlight. A phase time is an astronomical instant; the Moon’s appearance and position from your location still depend on when you look.
