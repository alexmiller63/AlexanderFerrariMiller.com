*Star Almanack — Alexander Ferrari Miller*

This is a reconstructed draft of the opening material accumulated for the Star Almanack. The surviving manuscripts are linked below in full. ## What’s up with Mensa, Microscopium, and Serpens?

*Reconstructed explanation from the recorded figure convention and current constellation generator; the complete original prose has not been located.*

A constellation’s official boundary defines a region of the sky. A stick figure is a convention for connecting selected stars into a recognizable pattern. Different field guides may connect those stars differently.

The Star Almanack adopts the IAU/MacRobert stick-figure convention. It supplies figures for 86 of the 88 constellations and explicitly leaves Mensa and Microscopium without stick figures. The Almanack preserves that distinction rather than inventing figures to fill the two gaps.

Serpens presents a different case. It is one constellation, represented by two separate figures: Serpens Caput, the head, and Serpens Cauda, the tail. Thus 86 constellations with figures produce 87 individual figures when the two parts of Serpens are counted separately.

The current constellation generator gives Mensa and Microscopium an explicit brightness fallback based on the arithmetic mean of their α and β star magnitudes. That value is a fallback convention, not a median calculated from an invented stick figure. Serpens Caput and Cauda use their respective adopted figure members. Telescopium and Norma use their actual figures; an earlier proposal to treat them as exceptions was superseded.

## Reading the sky diagrams

*Assembled from the surviving chart and observer-view conventions.*

Constellation stick figures are blue; asterism stick figures are green; official constellation boundaries are white. Asterisms have no displayed boundaries. Their computational geometry must not be mistaken for an official region of the sky.

For geometric-center calculations, the Almanack treats a sky region as though it were cut out of the celestial sphere and balanced on a finger. The balance point is its spherical area centroid. A constellation uses its official boundary region; an asterism uses geometry derived from its selected member stars. This does not give an asterism an official boundary.

The observer-view renderer describes its finder, binocular, and eyepiece views as catalog-derived diagrams, not astrophotographs. Equipment, magnification, and field of view belong in the individual caption so the reader knows what kind of view is being represented.
